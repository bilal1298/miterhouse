A chain link fence installation runs $15–$40 per linear foot when you hire out, or $8–$18 per foot for materials if you go DIY. On a 200-foot residential perimeter, that's $3,000–$8,000 installed, or roughly $1,000–$1,900 in materials before you rent tools.

No other fence type comes close to that value per foot. It's not going to win any neighborhood design awards, but for pet containment, property lines, and keeping your kids in the yard, chain link delivers more function per dollar than wood, vinyl, or aluminum — and it'll outlast a pressure-treated wood fence by a decade if you maintain it.

Here's exactly where the money goes, what you can skip, and the mistakes that turn a $1,500 materials job into a sagging embarrassment.

## Height Is the Biggest Cost Lever

Chain link comes in 3-, 4-, 5-, and 6-foot heights. Each step up adds cost on every component — longer posts, more mesh, more concrete, more labor. Here's what to expect at each height:

| Height | Materials/ft | Installed/ft |
|--------|-------------|--------------|
| 3 ft   | $6–$12      | $12–$28      |
| 4 ft   | $8–$16      | $15–$35      |
| 5 ft   | $10–$18     | $18–$38      |
| 6 ft   | $12–$22     | $22–$45      |

Most residential backyards use 4-foot. If your municipality or HOA mandates 6-foot, your project cost climbs 40–50% over the same footprint in 4-foot — that's a real number worth knowing before you start calling contractors.

## Wire Gauge: Don't Buy the Cheapest Mesh

The mesh itself varies by thickness (gauge) and coating. Confusingly, lower gauge numbers mean thicker wire:

**11.5 gauge galvanized** — the standard residential spec. Sold everywhere, adequate for most yards, rated for 15–20 years. This is the right choice for the vast majority of homeowners.

**9 gauge galvanized** — commercial and industrial grade. Noticeably stiffer, rated 25+ years. Costs 30–50% more than 11.5 gauge. Buy it if you have a large breed that leans or pushes on the fence, or if you're near the coast.

**Vinyl-coated (any gauge)** — adds a color layer — black, green, or brown — over galvanized wire. Black is the best-looking option by far; it visually recedes at a distance in a way that silver galvanized never does. Budget an extra $2–$5 per linear foot over bare galvanized, but you get better corrosion resistance and a fence that doesn't look like a highway barrier.

Skip the cheaper "economy" mesh you'll sometimes see at big-box stores — it's often 12.5 gauge, and it shows its age within five years.

## Gates: Plan Their Location Before You Order Anything

Every gate adds $100–$400 depending on width. A standard 3-foot walk gate runs $100–$200 installed. A 10-foot double-swing drive gate lands at $250–$500. Automated sliding driveway gates are a different project entirely — budget $1,500 minimum and expect a longer lead time.

The thing most people get wrong: they decide where the gate goes after the posts are set. Don't do that. Gate posts are "terminal posts" — larger diameter, set deeper, with their own concrete footings. If you move a gate location after the fence is built, you're resetting posts and rebuilding that section from scratch.

Mark your gate locations on the ground with spray paint before you order a single post.

## What Complicates Your Job (And Your Quote)

Flat, soft soil is the best case. Everything else costs more:

**Rocky soil** takes 3–5x longer to dig. A rented auger won't cut it — you're looking at a jackhammer or manual rock bar. Add $2–$5 per linear foot.

**Slopes** require either racking the mesh (angling it within the framework, which works for moderate grades) or stepped sections with individual terminal posts at each step (for steep terrain). Sloped sections add $3–$8 per linear foot.

**Tree roots near the fence line** mean hand-digging and root cutting. Some roots you can't cut without killing the tree, which turns your fence project into a landscaping decision you weren't expecting.

If your yard has any of these conditions, get three quotes from fence contractors. The range can be enormous — one contractor may price around the complication; another may just estimate high and hope you don't notice.

## Materials for 200 Feet of 4-Foot Fence

Here's a concrete shopping list you can price out at your local fence supply house or Home Depot:

- 22 line posts (1-5/8" × 6'): ~$8–$15 each → $176–$330
- 4–6 terminal posts (2-3/8" × 7'): ~$18–$30 each → $72–$180
- 20 top rail sections (1-3/8" × 10.5'): ~$8–$12 each → $160–$240
- 4 rolls chain link mesh (50' × 48"): ~$60–$100 each → $240–$400
- Hardware kit (tension bars, bands, caps, ties): ~$120–$200
- 30–40 bags Quikrete 80lb: ~$5–$7 each → $150–$280
- 1–2 gates with hardware: ~$100–$300

**Total materials estimate: $1,000–$1,900**

Buy from a fence supply wholesaler rather than a big-box store if one's available in your area. You'll pay 15–25% less and get heavier-gauge materials in the same catalog names. Most will sell to homeowners — just call ahead.

## What You're Paying for When You Hire a Contractor

A contractor quoting $4,500 for $1,400 in materials isn't simply padding margin. Here's where the labor money actually goes:

**Utility locates and site prep.** They call 811, wait for the mark-out, clear brush along the line, and handle anything that turns up. You're responsible if you dig through a gas line on a DIY job.

**Equipment.** Commercial augers, concrete mixers, fence stretchers, and pipe cutters. Renting this yourself runs $100–$200 per day and you'll use tools you've never touched before. That's when expensive mistakes happen. It's also worth reviewing your [essential tools for home renovation](/blog/essential-tools-for-home-renovation/) before committing to any DIY scope — some of these tools you may already own.

**Speed.** A two-person pro crew installs 100–150 linear feet per day. The same run takes a first-time DIYer and a helper a full weekend, sometimes more. And the pro's posts will be plumbing straighter after post number 10 than yours will be after post number 3.

**Warranty.** Most fence contractors carry a one-year workmanship warranty and general liability insurance. If a post shifts in the first year, they come back.

The [American Fence Association](https://www.americanfenceassociation.com/) lists certified installers by region. Always verify permits — any contractor suggesting you skip the permit is cutting corners in other ways too.

## DIY: An Honest Assessment of What's Hard

Chain link is genuinely one of the more DIY-accessible fence types. But it's not beginner work. You need to be comfortable digging 20+ post holes to consistent depth (rent a two-man auger — the single-person version will wreck your back), mixing concrete fast, and using a fence stretcher, which is a specialized tool most people have never picked up.

The mistakes that ruin DIY chain link jobs:

**Posts that aren't plumb.** One inch off at the ground becomes 3+ inches off at the top of a 6-foot post. Use a post level that straps to the pipe so both hands are free. Check from two directions every time.

**Mesh tensioned wrong.** Too loose and it sags between posts within a season. Too tight and it pulls the terminal posts inward. The target: about one inch of deflection at the midspan between posts with moderate hand pressure.

**Concrete mixed too wet.** Soupy concrete shrinks as it cures, leaving voids around the post. Mix it stiff — barely workable. A lot of experienced fence installers use the dry-pack method: pour dry Quikrete around the post, tamp it, then soak it with water and let it cure in place. It sounds wrong but works reliably.

**Skipping the bottom tension wire.** A tension wire running 1–2 inches above grade along the bottom of the mesh keeps it from being pushed up by dogs, kicked out by kids, or sagging under its own weight over time. It takes 30 minutes to add on a 200-foot fence. Do it.

This is a different project from, say, [building a deck on a budget](/blog/how-to-build-a-diy-deck-on-a-budget/) — the tool learning curve is steeper, but the physical labor is more straightforward once you're set up.

## Adding Privacy Without Replacing the Fence

If the see-through look bothers you after installation, you have options short of building a new fence:

**Privacy slats** — vertical or diagonal slats woven through the mesh, providing 70–90% coverage. Budget $3–$7 per linear foot in materials. Important: slats increase wind load significantly. If you're planning to add slats, use 9-gauge mesh and tighten post spacing to 8 feet instead of 10.

**Black vinyl-coated mesh** — if you're buying new, just spec vinyl-coated mesh in black instead of galvanized silver. It disappears visually at any distance. Cheapest privacy upgrade available.

**Hedge screening** — plant arborvitae, privet, or holly along the inside of the fence. In 3–5 years the plantings grow through the mesh and you effectively have a living fence. It looks vastly better than any slat system and adds value to the property. This is the long game, but it's the right answer if you're keeping the house.

## Permits: Skipping Them Isn't Worth It

Most jurisdictions require a fence permit for anything over 3–4 feet. The process is usually straightforward: submit a site plan showing fence location relative to property lines, pay $25–$150, and schedule two inspections — one after post holes are dug (before concrete) and a final.

Setback rules vary by municipality. Some require the fence to sit 6 inches to 2 feet inside your property line. Some require the "finished side" to face outward, though with chain link both sides look the same so this rarely matters.

Don't skip the permit. An unpermitted fence can hold up a home sale, trigger a neighbor complaint, or force a tear-down. It's not a risk worth $75 in permit fees.

## How Long Chain Link Actually Lasts (And What Maintenance It Needs)

A well-maintained galvanized chain link fence lasts 20–25 years. Vinyl-coated versions regularly hit 30. At those lifespans, the per-year cost works out to under $1 per linear foot annually on a professionally installed fence — that's hard to beat.

To get there, the maintenance is minimal but non-negotiable:

**Every quarter:** Walk the line. Push each post to check for looseness. Clear any vines growing into the mesh — they add weight and trap moisture.

**Every year:** Inspect posts at ground level for rust. Hit any spots immediately with a wire brush and a can of Rust-Oleum cold galvanizing spray — the $8 aerosol version works fine. Check gate hinges and latches; lubricate with white lithium grease, not WD-40 (which attracts grit and dries out).

**After big storms:** Check for leaning posts and torn mesh. Wind events are when failures happen; catch them early before the fence shifts further.

The maintenance time is maybe two hours a year. Skip it for five years in a row and you'll be replacing sections, not just touching up.
