A professionally installed chain link fence costs between $15 and $40 per linear foot, putting a typical 200-foot residential perimeter fence in the $3,000 to $8,000 range. Going the DIY route cuts that roughly in half — $8 to $18 per linear foot for materials — but you will need a full weekend, a helper, and a few rented tools to get it done right.

Chain link remains the most cost-effective fencing per linear foot in the United States. It is not glamorous, but for property lines, pet containment, and security, nothing else delivers as much function for the money.

## What Drives the Cost Up or Down

Not every chain link fence project costs the same, and the differences are not random. These are the variables that actually move the needle on your final bill.

### Fence Height

Standard residential chain link comes in 3-foot, 4-foot, 5-foot, and 6-foot heights. Each step up in height increases the cost of posts, mesh, and labor:

| Height | Material Cost/ft | Installed Cost/ft |
|--------|-----------------|-------------------|
| 3 ft   | $6 - $12        | $12 - $28         |
| 4 ft   | $8 - $16        | $15 - $35         |
| 5 ft   | $10 - $18       | $18 - $38         |
| 6 ft   | $12 - $22       | $22 - $45         |

Most backyards get 4-foot fencing. If your municipality or HOA requires 6-foot, your project cost increases by roughly 40 to 50 percent over the same layout in 4-foot.

### Wire Gauge and Coating

The mesh itself comes in different thicknesses and finishes. Standard residential is 11 or 11.5 gauge galvanized steel. Lower gauge numbers mean thicker wire:

- **11.5 gauge galvanized:** The baseline residential option. Adequate for pet containment and property lines. Lifespan of 15 to 20 years.
- **9 gauge galvanized:** Commercial/industrial grade. Significantly stiffer and more durable. Costs 30 to 50 percent more than 11.5 gauge but lasts 25+ years.
- **Vinyl-coated (any gauge):** Adds a color layer (typically black, green, or brown) over galvanized wire. Improves appearance and corrosion resistance. Adds $2 to $5 per linear foot over bare galvanized.

For most homeowners, 11.5 gauge galvanized is the smart choice unless you have large dogs that push against the fence or you live near salt water, where vinyl-coated pays for itself in extended lifespan.

### Gates

Every gate adds $100 to $400 to the project depending on width and style. A standard 3-foot walk gate runs $100 to $200 installed. A 10-foot double-swing drive gate runs $250 to $500. Automated sliding gates for driveways push into $1,500+ territory.

Plan your gate locations before ordering materials. Moving a gate after the fence is built means resetting terminal posts — effectively rebuilding that entire section.

### Terrain and Soil

Flat, soft soil is the best-case scenario. Each of these complications adds cost:

- **Rocky soil:** Post holes take 3 to 5 times longer. Auger rental may not cut it — you might need a jackhammer or rock bar. Add $2 to $5 per linear foot.
- **Slopes:** Chain link can follow moderate grades by racking the mesh (angling it within the framework), but steep slopes require stepped sections with individual terminal posts at each step. Add $3 to $8 per foot on sloped sections.
- **Tree roots:** If your fence line runs near mature trees, roots in post holes mean hand-digging and cutting. Some roots you cannot cut without killing the tree.

## Material List for a 200-Foot, 4-Foot Fence

Here is a concrete shopping list so you can price it at your local supplier:

- 22 line posts (1-5/8" x 6'): ~$8-$15 each = $176-$330
- 4-6 terminal posts (2-3/8" x 7'): ~$18-$30 each = $72-$180
- 20 top rail sections (1-3/8" x 10.5'): ~$8-$12 each = $160-$240
- 4 rolls chain link mesh (50' x 48"): ~$60-$100 each = $240-$400
- Hardware kit (tension bars, bands, caps, ties): ~$120-$200
- 30-40 bags premix concrete: ~$4-$6 each = $120-$240
- 1-2 gates with hardware: ~$100-$300

**Total materials estimate:** $988-$1,890

That is a wide range because material quality varies. Buying from a fence supply wholesaler (not a big-box store) typically saves 15 to 25 percent and gets you better gauge materials.

## Pro Installation: What You Are Paying For

When a contractor quotes you $4,500 for a fence you could theoretically material for $1,400, the markup is not pure profit. Here is where the labor money goes:

**Site preparation:** Marking the layout, calling 811 for utility locates, clearing brush along the fence line. A good contractor handles all of this.

**Equipment:** Commercial augers, concrete mixers, fence stretching tools, and pipe cutters. Renting this yourself costs $100 to $200 per day, and you might damage unfamiliar equipment.

**Speed and quality:** A two-person crew installs 100 to 150 linear feet per day. The same run takes a DIYer and a helper two to three days. The pro's posts will be straighter, the mesh tighter, and the gates better aligned.

**Warranty and insurance:** Most fence contractors provide a one-year workmanship warranty and carry liability insurance. If a post shifts or a gate sags within the warranty period, they fix it.

When evaluating contractors, the [American Fence Association](https://www.americanfenceassociation.com/) can help you find certified installers. Also check that they pull permits — any contractor who suggests skipping the permit is cutting corners elsewhere too.

## The DIY Approach: Honest Assessment

Chain link is among the most DIY-friendly fence types, but "friendly" is relative. You need to be comfortable with:

- Digging 20+ post holes to consistent depth (exhausting even with an auger)
- Mixing and pouring concrete in rapid succession
- Using a come-along or fence stretcher to tension mesh (unfamiliar tool for most people)
- Cutting metal pipe with a hacksaw or angle grinder

**Where DIYers commonly go wrong:**

1. **Posts not plumb.** A post that is even 1 inch off plumb at ground level will be 3+ inches off at the top. Use a post level (the kind that straps to the post, freeing both hands) and check from two directions.

2. **Mesh not tight enough.** Under-tensioned mesh sags between posts and looks sloppy. Over-tensioned mesh pulls posts inward. The sweet spot is when you can deflect the mesh about an inch with moderate hand pressure at the midpoint between posts.

3. **Concrete too wet.** Soupy concrete takes forever to set and shrinks, leaving gaps around the post. Mix it stiff — barely workable. Some experienced installers pour dry mix into the hole around the post and soak it with water, letting it cure in place. This "dry pack" method works surprisingly well.

4. **Forgetting the bottom tension wire.** Running a tension wire along the bottom of the mesh, 1 to 2 inches above grade, prevents the mesh from being pushed up by animals or sagging outward over time. It takes 30 minutes to install on a 200-foot fence and makes a noticeable difference.

## Privacy and Aesthetic Options

If the see-through nature of chain link bothers you, there are ways to add privacy without replacing the entire fence:

**Privacy slats:** Vertical or diagonal slats woven through the mesh. They provide 70 to 90 percent privacy and come in several colors. Budget $3 to $7 per linear foot. They increase wind load significantly, so use heavier-gauge mesh and closer post spacing if you plan to add slats.

**Hedge screening:** Plant a hedge row along the inside of the fence. Privet, arborvitae, or holly will eventually grow through and obscure the chain link entirely. This takes 3 to 5 years but creates a living fence that looks far better than any manufactured privacy solution. Use [water-efficient planting practices](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) to keep irrigation costs reasonable.

**Vinyl-coated mesh in black:** Black chain link disappears visually at a distance far more than galvanized silver. This is the cheapest way to improve the appearance without adding anything.

## Permits and Regulations

Most municipalities require a fence permit for any fence over 3 or 4 feet tall. The permit process typically involves:

1. Submitting a site plan showing the fence location relative to property lines and structures
2. Paying a fee ($25 to $150 in most areas)
3. Scheduling an inspection after post holes are dug (before concrete) and sometimes a final inspection

Setback requirements vary. Some jurisdictions require the fence sit 6 inches to 2 feet inside your property line. Others require the "finished side" (the side without post brackets) to face outward. With chain link, both sides look essentially the same, so this is rarely an issue.

If you live in an [age-friendly or accessible community](https://www.aarp.org/livable-communities/housing/), check whether there are additional guidelines for fence heights near sidewalks and public pathways.

## Maintenance Schedule

Chain link requires minimal upkeep, but ignoring it entirely shortens the lifespan considerably.

**Quarterly:** Walk the fence line. Push on each post to check for looseness. Tighten any loose tie wires. Clear vegetation growing into the mesh — vines add weight and hold moisture against the metal.

**Annually:** Inspect every post at the ground line for rust. Treat rust spots immediately with a wire brush and cold galvanizing spray. Check gate hinges and latches — lubricate with white lithium grease, not WD-40 (which attracts dirt).

**After storms:** High winds stress fences. Check for leaning posts and stretched or torn mesh sections after any significant wind event.

A well-maintained galvanized chain link fence lasts 20 to 25 years. Vinyl-coated versions often make it to 30. At those lifespans, the per-year cost of chain link fencing is remarkably low — often under $1 per linear foot per year for a professionally installed fence.
