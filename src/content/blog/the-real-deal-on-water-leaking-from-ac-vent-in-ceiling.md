Water dripping from an AC vent in the ceiling means one of a few things: a clogged condensate drain line, frozen evaporator coils, or a ductwork condensation problem. The fix ranges from a 10-minute DIY task to an HVAC service call, but you need to act quickly because water on drywall leads to mold growth within 24–48 hours.

Here is how to diagnose the cause, stop the leak, and decide whether to handle it yourself or call a technician.

## Why Water Leaks From AC Vents

Your air conditioning system produces condensation as a normal part of operation — cold evaporator coils pull moisture from warm air, and that water collects in a drain pan and flows out through a condensate drain line. When something disrupts that process, the water finds another path. Usually, that path is through your ceiling vents.

The five most common causes, in order of likelihood:

### 1. Clogged Condensate Drain Line

This is responsible for roughly 70% of water-at-the-vent situations. Algae, mold, and dust accumulate inside the PVC drain line over time, eventually creating a blockage. Water backs up into the drain pan, overflows, and follows ductwork or framing down to the nearest ceiling opening — your supply vent.

**Signs it is the drain line:** Slow drip that gets worse when the AC runs for extended periods. Water may also appear at the indoor unit's drain pan area if you can access it.

**DIY fix:** Locate the drain line's cleanout port (a capped T-fitting near the indoor air handler, usually in the attic or closet). Remove the cap and pour 1 cup of distilled white vinegar down the line. Wait 30 minutes, then flush with warm water. For stubborn clogs, use a wet/dry vacuum on the exterior end of the drain line — seal the connection with a rag and run the vacuum for 2–3 minutes. This alone clears most blockages.

**Prevention:** Pour a cup of vinegar down the cleanout every three months during cooling season. Some homeowners install condensate drain line tablets (slow-dissolving algaecide) in the drain pan — they cost about $8 for a pack and last all season.

### 2. Frozen Evaporator Coils

When the evaporator coil freezes, ice builds up on the coil and surrounding surfaces. When the system cycles off or the ice gets heavy enough, it melts rapidly and overwhelms the drain pan.

**Signs it is a freeze-up:** The AC blows warm air or barely any air, the supply lines near the air handler feel unusually cold or frosted, and you see a sudden rush of water rather than a slow drip.

**Common causes of freezing:**
- **Dirty air filter.** Restricted airflow across the coil is the number-one cause. Check your filter first — always.
- **Low refrigerant.** A slow leak reduces system pressure and drops coil temperature below freezing. This requires a certified HVAC technician to locate the leak, repair it, and recharge the system. Per [EPA WaterSense guidelines](https://www.epa.gov/watersense), refrigerant handling requires certification.
- **Blower motor failure.** If the fan that pushes air across the coil stops working, the coil freezes.
- **Closed or blocked supply vents.** Closing too many vents in unused rooms restricts airflow enough to freeze the coil. Keep at least 80% of your supply vents open.

**Immediate action:** Turn the AC off but leave the fan running (switch the thermostat from "cool" to "fan only"). This circulates warm air across the coils and melts the ice gradually over 2–4 hours. Place towels and a bucket under the air handler if accessible. Once thawed, check your filter and vents before restarting.

### 3. Disconnected or Damaged Drain Line

If the PVC drain line has separated at a joint, cracked from age, or been bumped loose (common after attic work or insulation installation), condensate water spills directly onto framing and drywall instead of reaching the exterior drain point.

**Signs:** Water appears suddenly after attic work, or you find wet spots along the path of the drain line in the attic rather than just at the vent.

**DIY fix:** Inspect the drain line from the air handler to the exterior. Re-cement any separated PVC joints with PVC primer and cement. Replace cracked sections — this is standard PVC plumbing work using Schedule 40 pipe and fittings from any hardware store.

### 4. Ductwork Condensation

In humid climates, poorly insulated ductwork running through a hot attic develops condensation on the exterior of the ducts. This moisture drips onto ceiling drywall and appears at vent registers.

**Signs:** Water appears even when the drain line is clear, humidity in the home is higher than normal, and you can see moisture beading on exposed ductwork in the attic.

**Fix:** Insulate ductwork with R-6 or R-8 duct insulation wrap. Pay special attention to connections and joints where insulation tends to have gaps. For flex duct, make sure the insulation jacket is intact and the vapor barrier is not torn. Sealing duct joints with mastic (not duct tape — it fails in heat) before insulating is the correct approach.

### 5. Damaged or Missing Drain Pan

The secondary drain pan under the air handler is your backup — it catches overflow when the primary drain clogs. If this pan is rusted through, cracked, or missing entirely, overflow water goes straight into the ceiling.

**Signs:** You find water around the air handler even though the drain line is flowing. The pan has visible rust or holes.

**Fix:** Replace the drain pan. Standard sizes are available at HVAC supply houses for $20–$50. While you are at it, install a float switch in the pan — it shuts the system off automatically if water reaches a certain level, preventing ceiling damage. Float switches cost $15–$25 and are a straightforward wiring connection.

## The Water Damage Clock

Once water hits drywall and insulation, you are on a timeline:

- **0–24 hours:** Drywall absorbs water, staining begins. Damage is usually cosmetic and repairable.
- **24–48 hours:** Mold colonization begins in damp insulation and drywall paper facing.
- **48–72 hours:** Mold spreads. Remediation costs escalate significantly.
- **Beyond 72 hours:** Structural concerns with saturated drywall, and mold remediation becomes a major project ($1,500–$5,000+).

Place a bucket or pan under the active drip immediately. Remove any wet ceiling insulation you can access from the attic side. Run a dehumidifier in the affected room. If the drywall is sagging or feels spongy, it needs replacement — do not just paint over it.

Safety note: per [OSHA guidelines](https://www.osha.gov/), significant mold remediation in occupied spaces should follow proper containment protocols. Small areas (under 10 square feet) are manageable with N95 masks and plastic sheeting.

## What It Costs to Fix

Here is what you will pay depending on the cause:

| Problem | DIY Cost | Professional Cost |
|---------|----------|------------------|
| Clogged drain line | $0–$10 (vinegar) | $75–$200 |
| Frozen coils (dirty filter) | $5–$20 (new filter) | $100–$200 service call |
| Frozen coils (low refrigerant) | N/A — needs certified tech | $200–$600 |
| Disconnected drain line | $10–$30 (PVC fittings) | $100–$250 |
| Ductwork insulation | $100–$300 (materials) | $400–$1,200 |
| Drain pan replacement | $20–$50 | $150–$350 |
| Drywall repair (per patch) | $30–$60 | $150–$400 |

Most ceiling-vent water leaks resolve for under $200 if caught early. The expensive scenarios involve delayed response leading to mold remediation and extensive drywall replacement.

## When to Call an HVAC Professional

Handle it yourself if the cause is a clogged drain line, a dirty filter, or a disconnected PVC joint — these are all basic maintenance tasks.

Call a professional when:
- You suspect low refrigerant (the system is not cooling properly even with a clean filter and open vents)
- The blower motor is not running
- You have already cleared the drain line and the leak continues
- Water damage has been ongoing for more than 48 hours and you suspect mold behind the drywall
- The air handler is in a location you cannot safely access

An HVAC service call for diagnosis runs $75–$150 in most markets. If you have been dealing with recurring water at the vents, a technician can also install a safety float switch and verify your entire condensate system is sloped correctly — problems that are hard to diagnose without seeing the full system.

## Preventing Future Leaks

A simple maintenance schedule prevents nearly all AC-related ceiling water problems:

- **Monthly:** Check and replace your air filter. This single habit prevents frozen coils, improves efficiency, and extends equipment life.
- **Quarterly (during cooling season):** Pour vinegar down the condensate cleanout.
- **Annually:** Have an HVAC technician inspect the system, check refrigerant charge, clean the evaporator coil, and verify drain line flow. Schedule this in spring before the heavy cooling season.
- **Once:** Install a float switch on the drain pan if you do not already have one. It is cheap insurance against ceiling damage.

Check your [plumbing and water systems](/blog/plumbing-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/) at the same time for a complete picture of water-related risks in your home.

The key takeaway: water at a ceiling vent is almost always a condensate issue, not a roof leak or plumbing problem. Start with the drain line and air filter before assuming the worst.
