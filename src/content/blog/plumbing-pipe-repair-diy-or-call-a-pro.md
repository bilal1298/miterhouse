Most plumbing pipe repairs fall into two categories: ones you can absolutely handle with basic tools and a trip to the hardware store, and ones that will flood your house if you get them wrong. Knowing the difference before you start saves you money, time, and potentially thousands in water damage.

A professional plumber charges $150 to $350 per hour for pipe repair work, with most common fixes landing between $200 and $600 total. Some of those repairs take ten minutes of actual work. Others genuinely require specialized tools and experience.

## Repairs You Can Handle Yourself

These are the plumbing pipe fixes where the risk is low and the learning curve is manageable.

### Fixing a Leaky Compression Fitting

Compression fittings are found under sinks and behind toilets where supply lines connect. If one is dripping, the fix is usually as simple as tightening the compression nut a quarter-turn with an adjustable wrench. If that doesn't stop it, replacing the ferrule (the small brass ring inside) costs under $2 and takes five minutes.

### Replacing a P-Trap

That curved pipe under your sink catches debris and prevents sewer gas from entering your home. When it clogs or corrodes, a replacement P-trap costs $5 to $15 at any hardware store. The connections are hand-tight plus a quarter-turn with channel-lock pliers. No soldering, no special skills.

### Patching a Pinhole Leak (Temporary)

For a small leak on an accessible copper pipe, an epoxy pipe repair kit ($8 to $15) or a pipe repair clamp ($5 to $20) provides a reliable temporary fix. Clean the pipe, apply the patch, and plan for a permanent repair. This buys you days or weeks, not months.

### Replacing Supply Lines

The braided stainless steel hoses running to your faucets, toilets, and washing machine should be replaced every 8 to 10 years. They cost $5 to $15 each and install with a wrench. This is genuine preventive maintenance that avoids catastrophic failures.

### Clearing Drain Clogs

A plunger handles most clogs. For stubborn ones, a drain snake ($20 to $40 for a hand-crank model) reaches 15 to 25 feet into the pipe. Skip the chemical drain cleaners. They corrode pipes over time and rarely solve the underlying problem.

## Repairs That Need a Professional

These situations involve either significant risk, code requirements, or specialized equipment that makes professional help the smart choice.

### Burst or Severely Corroded Pipes

A burst pipe behind a wall or under a slab is not a DIY situation. You need someone who can locate the break accurately (often with a camera), access it with minimal demolition, and repair it to [code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/). A pro handles this in hours. A DIYer can easily turn it into a multi-day demolition project.

### Sewer Line Repairs

Anything involving your main sewer line requires professional equipment: camera inspection, hydro-jetting, or trenchless pipe lining. Sewer work also involves permits and inspections in most jurisdictions. The cost ranges from $200 for a simple cleanout to $5,000 or more for line replacement.

### Repiping

If your home has galvanized steel pipes (common in houses built before 1970), the corrosion will eventually affect every fixture. Whole-house repiping with copper or PEX costs $4,000 to $15,000 depending on size, but it is absolutely a licensed-plumber job. This involves cutting into walls, running new lines, and passing inspection.

### Gas Lines

Never touch gas piping yourself. Period. Improper gas connections cause explosions and carbon monoxide poisoning. Every gas line repair or modification requires a licensed plumber and a permit. The standards from [NFPA](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70) and local codes exist because the consequences of failure are catastrophic.

### Water Heater Connections

While you can drain and disconnect a water heater, the gas and water connections on the new unit should be done by a licensed plumber. See our guide on [water tank heater installation](/blog/installing-tank-water-heater-what-to-know-first/) for the full breakdown.

## How to Diagnose Common Pipe Problems

Before you can decide DIY vs. pro, you need to identify what you are dealing with.

**Low water pressure at one fixture:** Usually a clogged aerator. Unscrew it, clean the screen, reassemble. Five-minute fix.

**Low water pressure everywhere:** Could be a partially closed main shutoff valve, a failing pressure regulator, or corroded supply pipes. Check the valve first (free), then call a plumber if that is not it.

**Water stains on the ceiling:** A pipe or fixture is leaking above. You need to open the ceiling to find the source. If the leak is at a fitting, you might handle it. If it is a corroded pipe section, call a pro.

**Banging pipes (water hammer):** This happens when valves close abruptly. Installing a water hammer arrestor ($10 to $20) at the offending fixture usually solves it. This is a DIY-friendly fix.

**Sewage smell from drains:** Dry P-traps are the most common cause. Run water in every drain you do not use regularly. If the smell persists, you may have a broken vent pipe, which needs professional diagnosis.

## The Tools You Actually Need

For basic pipe repairs, you do not need a plumber's full kit. Here is what covers 90% of DIY plumbing work:

- Adjustable wrench (10-inch and 12-inch)
- Channel-lock pliers (two pairs in different sizes)
- Pipe wrench (14-inch, for stubborn threaded connections)
- Teflon tape (use the yellow gas-rated tape only on gas; white is fine for water)
- Hacksaw or tubing cutter
- Plunger (a flange plunger, not just a cup plunger)
- Hand-crank drain snake (25-foot)
- Bucket, towels, and a headlamp

Total cost for this kit: $80 to $150. You will use these tools repeatedly.

## When the Cost Math Favors a Pro

The decision is not always about skill. Sometimes hiring out just makes financial sense.

A plumber who charges $250 to fix a leaking pipe fitting in 30 minutes may seem expensive. But consider what happens if your DIY attempt goes sideways: water damage to flooring, drywall, and personal property can easily reach $5,000 to $10,000. Your homeowner's insurance may not cover damage caused by amateur plumbing work.

For anything behind walls, under slabs, or involving your main water or sewer lines, the professional's speed, tools, and insurance make them the better value even when the task seems simple.

## Getting Quotes and Avoiding Ripoffs

When you do hire a plumber, get at least three written quotes. Be specific about the problem and ask each plumber to explain what they plan to do. Red flags include:

- Quoting a price without seeing the problem
- Pressuring you to decide immediately
- Cannot provide a license number
- No written estimate or contract
- Insisting on cash payment only

A legitimate plumber carries insurance (ask for the certificate), holds a current license (verify with your state licensing board, usually [UL-listed](https://www.ul.com/) or equivalent), and will pull permits when required.

## Making the Call

If you can see the problem, reach it without demolition, and fix it with basic tools and fittings, do it yourself. If you cannot see it, cannot reach it, or it involves gas, sewer, or structural work, call a licensed plumber. There is no shame in knowing the boundary. The best DIYers are the ones who know when to hand off.
