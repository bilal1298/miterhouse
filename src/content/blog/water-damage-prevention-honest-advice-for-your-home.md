Water damage is the most common and most expensive type of homeowners insurance claim, averaging $12,000 to $15,000 per incident. The majority of those claims are preventable. A few hundred dollars in maintenance and a weekend of attention each year can save you from five-figure repair bills and months of disruption. Here are the specific steps that actually work, organized by where water gets in.

## Plumbing: The Number One Source of Indoor Water Damage

Plumbing failures cause more water damage claims than any other source. Supply lines, drain connections, and appliance hookups all develop problems over time — and they usually fail at the worst possible moment.

### Supply Lines and Hoses

**Washing machine hoses** are the single most common catastrophic failure point. Standard rubber hoses deteriorate from the inside out and can burst without warning, releasing 500 to 650 gallons of water per hour. Replace rubber hoses with braided stainless steel hoses ($15 to $25 per pair) every 5 years. Check them annually for bulging, cracking, or corrosion at the connections.

**Toilet supply lines** are the second most common failure. The flexible braided line that connects the shutoff valve to the tank can fail at the compression fitting. Replace these every 5 to 7 years. Turn off the shutoff valve and check that it actually stops the water — corroded valves that will not close are useless in an emergency.

**Ice maker and refrigerator water lines** are often forgotten because they are behind the appliance. Copper lines last longer than plastic, but both can fail at connections. Pull the refrigerator out annually and inspect the line. Replace plastic lines with braided stainless steel.

**Under-sink supply connections** in kitchens and bathrooms should be checked every time you open the cabinet. Look for drips, mineral deposits (white or green crusting), and dampness on the cabinet floor.

### Water Heaters

Your water heater has a finite lifespan — 8 to 12 years for a tank model. As it ages, the tank corrodes internally, and eventually it leaks or fails outright. If your [water heater is showing signs of trouble](/blog/water-heater-constantly-running-a-diyers-honest-take/), address it before it becomes a flood.

Preventive steps:
- Install a drain pan under the water heater with a connected drain line ($20 to $50 for the pan)
- Flush the tank annually to remove sediment that accelerates corrosion
- Check the temperature and pressure (T&P) relief valve annually — lift the lever briefly and verify water flows then stops
- Replace the anode rod every 3 to 5 years (this sacrificial rod protects the tank from corrosion)
- Know the age of your unit and plan for replacement before failure

If your [water heater is leaking](/blog/is-it-normal-for-water-heater-to-leak-honest-guide/), do not ignore it — even a small drip indicates a problem that will get worse.

## Exterior Drainage: Keeping Water Away From the Foundation

Water around the foundation is the source of most basement and crawl space moisture problems. The fix is almost always about directing water away from the house.

### Gutters and Downspouts

Clogged or overflowing gutters dump concentrated water right at the foundation. This is the single most common cause of basement water intrusion.

- Clean gutters twice a year — spring and fall, minimum
- Ensure downspouts extend at least 4 to 6 feet from the foundation. Extensions cost $5 to $15 each
- Check that gutters slope toward downspouts (about 1/4 inch of drop per 10 feet of gutter)
- Repair any leaking seams or joints with gutter sealant ($5 per tube)
- Consider gutter guards if trees overhang your roof ($3 to $12 per linear foot installed)

### Grading

The ground around your foundation should slope away from the house at a rate of at least 6 inches of fall over the first 10 feet. Over time, soil settles and grading can reverse, directing water toward the foundation instead of away from it.

Check your grading by placing a level on the soil surface near the foundation and measuring the slope. If it is flat or slopes toward the house, add topsoil to correct the grade. This is one of the cheapest and most effective water damage prevention measures — a few cubic yards of topsoil costs $100 to $300.

### Window Wells

Basement window wells collect water if they lack proper drainage. Each well should have:
- 4 to 6 inches of clean gravel at the bottom for drainage
- A clear well cover to keep rain out ($15 to $40 each)
- A drain that connects to the perimeter drain system or daylight

## The Roof: Your First Line of Defense

Roof leaks often start small and go unnoticed until they have caused significant damage to attic framing, insulation, and interior ceilings.

**Annual roof inspection checklist:**
- Walk the perimeter and look for missing, cracked, or curling shingles
- Check flashing around chimneys, vents, skylights, and valleys — failed flashing is the most common cause of roof leaks
- Look for moss or algae growth that holds moisture against the shingles
- Inspect from inside the attic for daylight, water stains, or damp insulation
- Check that the attic has adequate ventilation to prevent ice dams in cold climates

If your roof is more than 15 years old (asphalt shingles) or 25 years old (architectural shingles), budget for replacement within the next few years. Catching a failing roof before it leaks saves the cost of interior water damage repair on top of the roof replacement.

## Smart Water Leak Detection

Technology has made leak detection far more accessible and affordable. Three levels of protection are worth considering:

**Basic leak detectors ($10–$30 each).** Battery-powered sensors that sit on the floor near water heaters, washing machines, sinks, and toilets. They alarm when they detect moisture. Place them at every potential leak source. Brands like Govee, Honeywell, and First Alert make reliable units.

**Smart leak detectors ($30–$80 each).** WiFi-connected sensors that send alerts to your phone when moisture is detected. Useful if you travel or have a vacation property. Some integrate with home automation systems.

**Automatic shutoff systems ($200–$500+).** These mount on your main water line and can shut off the water supply automatically when a leak is detected. Systems like Flo by Moen, Phyn, and Guardian monitor water flow patterns and can detect both sudden bursts and slow leaks. They are the most effective prevention measure available but require professional installation.

## Seasonal Prevention Checklist

### Spring
- Inspect the roof for winter damage
- Clean gutters and check downspout extensions
- Check sump pump operation (pour water in to test)
- Inspect the foundation for new cracks
- Test all leak detectors and replace batteries

### Summer
- Check air conditioning condensate drain for clogs
- Inspect visible plumbing for drips
- Check washing machine hoses
- Ensure landscape irrigation is not spraying the foundation

### Fall
- Clean gutters after leaves drop
- Disconnect and drain outdoor hoses
- Shut off exterior hose bibs from inside and open the outdoor valve to drain
- Insulate exposed pipes in unheated spaces (crawl space, garage, attic)
- Service the water heater (flush and inspect)

### Winter
- Keep the thermostat at 55°F or higher, even when away, to prevent frozen pipes
- Open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls during extreme cold
- Know where your main water shutoff is and verify it works
- If leaving for an extended period, consider shutting off the main water supply

## What It Costs vs. What It Saves

| Prevention Measure | Cost | Potential Damage Prevented |
|---|---|---|
| Stainless steel washing machine hoses | $15–$25 | $5,000–$15,000 |
| Gutter cleaning (twice yearly) | $150–$300/year | $3,000–$10,000 |
| Grading correction | $100–$500 | $5,000–$20,000 |
| Leak detectors (5 sensors) | $50–$250 | $5,000–$25,000 |
| Water heater drain pan | $20–$50 | $3,000–$10,000 |
| Sump pump maintenance | $50–$100/year | $10,000–$30,000 |
| Automatic shutoff valve | $300–$600 installed | $5,000–$50,000 |

The math is overwhelmingly in favor of prevention. A few hundred dollars per year in maintenance protects against five- and six-figure repair bills.

Workplace safety standards from [OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/) apply to professional contractors working in your home, and the [UL (Underwriters Laboratories)](https://www.ul.com/) certifies the safety of leak detection devices and water shutoff systems. Look for UL-listed products when purchasing electrical devices that will operate near water.

The honest truth: most water damage is preventable with basic maintenance. The homeowners who get hit hardest are those who defer the small tasks — cleaning gutters, replacing hoses, checking the water heater — until something fails catastrophically. Spend a weekend twice a year on prevention, and you will likely never deal with a serious water damage event.
