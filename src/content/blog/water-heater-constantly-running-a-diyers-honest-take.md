A water heater that never seems to shut off is burning through energy and money while potentially signaling a problem that will get worse. Under normal conditions, a gas or electric water heater cycles on and off throughout the day — heating water until it reaches the set temperature, then shutting down until the tank temperature drops enough to trigger another heating cycle. If yours runs continuously or cycles far more frequently than it used to, something specific is wrong, and the fix depends on identifying the cause.

Most causes of a constantly running water heater cost $15 to $300 to fix. Ignoring the problem wastes $20 to $60+ per month in excess energy and accelerates wear on the heating elements, burner, and tank.

## How Normal Operation Looks

Before diagnosing the problem, understand what normal looks like. A properly functioning tank water heater operates in cycles:

- **Gas heater:** The burner fires for 30 to 45 minutes to heat a full tank from cold. During normal use, it cycles on for 15 to 25 minutes every 1 to 2 hours, depending on usage and standby heat loss.
- **Electric heater:** The elements heat in sequence — lower element first, then upper. A full recovery from cold takes 60 to 90 minutes. Normal cycling is 15 to 30 minutes on, followed by 1 to 3 hours off.

If your heater runs for significantly longer than these ranges, or if it never seems to reach temperature and shut off, one of these causes is likely responsible.

## Cause #1: The Thermostat Is Set Too High

This is the simplest explanation and the first thing to check. If someone cranked the thermostat up, the heater works harder and longer to reach the higher target temperature.

**The fix:** Check the thermostat setting. For gas heaters, the dial is on the gas control valve — the recommended setting is 120 degrees. For electric heaters, you need to remove the access panel(s) on the side of the tank and adjust the thermostat(s) behind the insulation. Use a flathead screwdriver to set both the upper and lower thermostats to 120 degrees.

According to [the Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/water-heating), every 10-degree reduction in water temperature saves 3% to 5% on water heating costs. Setting the temperature above 140 degrees also creates a scalding risk and accelerates mineral buildup inside the tank.

**Cost to fix:** $0.

## Cause #2: Sediment Buildup

Heavy sediment on the tank bottom acts as insulation between the burner (or lower element) and the water. The heater runs longer because it is heating sediment before it heats water. You may also hear popping or rumbling sounds — that is water trapped under the sediment layer boiling and bursting through.

**The fix:** [Flush the tank](/blog/how-often-should-i-flush-my-water-heater-honest-guide/). Connect a garden hose to the drain valve, drain the tank, and flush until the water runs clear. For severe buildup, you may need to repeat the process or use a deliming solution.

**Cost to fix:** $0 for DIY flush; $100 to $250 if you hire a plumber.

## Cause #3: Failed Thermostat

If the thermostat is stuck in the "on" position, it never signals the burner or element to shut off. The water gets progressively hotter, which is both a waste of energy and a safety hazard — the TPR (temperature-pressure relief) valve may start discharging as a safety response.

**How to diagnose:** Check the actual water temperature at a faucet using a cooking thermometer. If the water is significantly hotter than your thermostat setting (more than 10 degrees above), the thermostat is not controlling the heater properly.

**The fix:** For gas heaters, a thermostat failure means replacing the gas control valve ($100 to $250 for the part, $100 to $200 for labor). For electric heaters, the thermostat is a separate component — replacement cost is $15 to $30 for the part, $75 to $150 for professional labor.

## Cause #4: Hot Water Leak Somewhere in the System

This is the sneaky one. If hot water is constantly draining from the system — even slowly — the heater refills with cold water and has to heat it, creating a never-ending cycle. The leak may not be visible.

**Where to look:**
- **TPR valve discharge pipe.** Check outside where the discharge pipe exits the house. A steady drip or stream means the TPR valve is releasing water, which could indicate excessive pressure or temperature.
- **Hot water pipes under the house.** If you have a crawlspace, check for dripping pipes.
- **Toilets.** A faulty mixing valve or a cross-connection in a toilet supply line can siphon hot water into the cold water system. Turn off the water heater and check if the hot water pipes stay warm — if they do, water is flowing somewhere it should not.
- **Recirculation system malfunction.** If your home has a hot water recirculation pump, a failed check valve can cause hot water to flow backward through the cold water system, wasting heat.

**Cost to fix:** Varies widely — $0 for a simple valve adjustment to $200+ for pipe repairs.

## Cause #5: Undersized Heater

If your household's hot water demand has increased (new bathroom, more family members, new dishwasher) but the water heater is the same size, it may be running constantly because it simply cannot keep up.

**How to check:** Note how many people are showering, running the dishwasher, and doing laundry simultaneously during peak usage. A 40-gallon tank can typically serve 2 to 3 people. A family of 4 to 5 needs a 50 to 60-gallon tank.

**The fix:** Either reduce simultaneous demand (stagger showers and appliance use) or [upgrade to a larger tank](/blog/installing-tank-water-heater-what-to-know-first/). A tank upgrade costs $800 to $2,000 installed, but it resolves the constant-running issue while improving comfort.

## Cause #6: Failed Dip Tube

The dip tube is a plastic pipe inside the tank that routes cold incoming water to the bottom, where the burner or lower element heats it. If the dip tube breaks or deteriorates (common in heaters from the late 1990s with defective dip tubes), cold water enters at the top of the tank and mixes with the hot water, reducing the effective temperature. The heater runs constantly trying to compensate.

**Symptoms:** Lukewarm water at faucets even though the heater seems to be running nonstop. Small pieces of white or blue plastic in faucet aerators or filter screens.

**The fix:** Replace the dip tube. The part costs $10 to $30. The job involves removing the cold water inlet nipple at the top of the heater and pulling out the old tube. It is doable for a confident DIYer, but accessing the inlet can be challenging in tight spaces. Professional repair: $100 to $200.

## Cause #7: Poor Insulation / Cold Location

A water heater in an uninsulated garage, crawlspace, or exposed basement loses heat to the surrounding air faster than one in a heated utility room. In cold weather, the standby heat loss increases and the heater cycles more frequently.

**The fix:** Add a water heater insulation blanket ($20 to $40). Also insulate the first 6 feet of hot and cold water pipes leaving the heater with foam pipe insulation ($5 to $15). These measures can reduce standby heat loss by 25% to 45% and significantly reduce cycling frequency per [EPA WaterSense recommendations](https://www.epa.gov/watersense).

## When Constant Running Signals Replacement Time

If your water heater is 10+ years old, runs constantly, and none of the above fixes resolve the issue, the unit may be at the end of its useful life. Internal corrosion, deteriorated insulation, and worn components compound to make the heater progressively less efficient.

The crossover point where repair stops making sense is roughly when repair costs exceed 50% of a new unit. At that point, a new water heater with modern insulation and efficiency pays for itself within a few years through reduced energy costs.
