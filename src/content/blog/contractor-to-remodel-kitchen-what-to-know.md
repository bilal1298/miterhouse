Most kitchen remodel disasters weren't caused by bad luck. They were caused by a bad hiring decision made before a single cabinet was touched.

The contractor to remodel your kitchen is the most consequential choice in the whole project. The wrong one — and there are plenty of them — will finish 60% of the work, run out of money, and stop returning calls. Or they'll finish it, but badly, and you'll spend the next two years fixing their mistakes. The good ones exist, but they don't find you. You have to find them.

Here's how to do that without getting burned.

## The One Question That Separates Good Contractors from Bad Ones

Before you ask for references, before you look at portfolios, ask this: "Can I see the insurance certificate and your license number so I can verify both directly?"

A legitimate contractor hands you that information without hesitation. They know you're going to check, and they want you to. A contractor who hedges, says they'll email it later, or gives you a number that comes back expired when you look it up — that's your answer. Stop there. Don't proceed to the reference check, don't look at their photos. Move on.

Your state's contractor licensing board has a public lookup. Use it. Confirm active status, confirm the insurance is current (call the insurer if you're uncertain), and look for any complaints or disciplinary actions on record. For homes built before 1978, also confirm the contractor holds EPA RRP (Renovation, Repair, and Painting) certification — federal law requires it for work that disturbs lead-based paint, and if they don't have it, you're the one holding liability.

## What a Kitchen Remodel Contractor Actually Does

A general contractor on a kitchen remodel doesn't swing a hammer all day. Their job is to coordinate: they sequence the plumber, the electrician, the cabinet installer, the countertop fabricator, and the tile setter so none of them are waiting on each other. On a well-run project, the trades flow through in order and the schedule holds. On a poorly run project, the plumber shows up before the rough electrical is done, the inspector flags the work, and everything sits for two weeks.

You'll run into two types of contractors for this work. The first is a general contractor who self-performs some trades and subs out the rest. The second is a design-build firm, which handles both the design and the construction under one roof. Design-build firms charge a premium — often 10–20% more — but eliminate the friction of managing a separate designer and contractor who may not agree on details. For a cosmetic refresh or a same-footprint cabinet swap, a good GC is all you need. For a gut renovation with layout changes, the design-build model often produces cleaner results and fewer disputes.

## What It Costs by Scope

You need to know the ranges before you talk to anyone, because a low bid that sounds attractive is often just a contractor leaving scope out of the quote.

A **cosmetic refresh** — paint, hardware, new lighting, maybe a faucet and backsplash — runs $5,000–$15,000. No layout changes, no new cabinets. You can DIY most of it; a contractor adds speed and finish quality.

A **mid-range remodel** — new stock or semi-custom cabinets, new countertops, updated appliances, new flooring — runs $25,000–$60,000. This is the project where a contractor's coordination skills pay for themselves.

A **major renovation** — gut to studs, layout change, custom cabinets, structural work, premium finishes — starts around $60,000 and can reach $150,000 or more in high cost-of-living markets. This scope requires a contractor with documented experience managing complex kitchen builds specifically, not just general remodeling work.

## How to Find Contractors Worth Talking To

The best kitchen contractors are booked 8–12 weeks out. Start your search well before your planned start date, because the ones who are available next week usually are available for a reason.

**Start with people who've recently finished a kitchen.** Neighbors, coworkers, family — anyone who completed a kitchen remodel in the past two years is a useful source. If you can see the finished kitchen in person, open every drawer, check the alignment of cabinet doors, look at where the tile meets the countertop, and examine the caulk lines. These details tell you more about workmanship than any photo.

**Ask contractors about their subcontractors specifically.** A GC is only as good as their subs. Ask who handles their plumbing and electrical. Long-term relationships with reliable subcontractors are a strong positive signal. A contractor who uses whoever's available that week is telling you something about how they run a project.

**Review their portfolio for scope similarity.** A contractor who's excellent at modern, open-plan kitchens may not be the right fit for a traditional craftsman renovation that requires detailed millwork. Look for kitchens similar to what you're planning.

The same vetting principles apply whether you're hiring someone for the kitchen, a deck, or any major exterior project — and they're covered in depth in our post on [hiring a deck building contractor](/blog/hiring-a-deck-building-contractor-what-to-look-for/).

## Red Flags That Predict Specific Problems

**A vague single-page quote.** "Install new kitchen per plans, $42,000" is not a scope of work. Every line item should be specified by brand, model, material, and finish. Allowances should be called out explicitly as allowances. Vague quotes lead to change orders for things you assumed were included — and every one of those change orders will be written in the contractor's favor.

**A request for 30–50% upfront.** Industry standard is 10% at signing with progress payments tied to verifiable milestones. A contractor who needs a large deposit before starting is either undercapitalized — meaning they're floating your money across multiple jobs — or doesn't plan to finish. Neither is acceptable.

**Resistance to pulling permits.** Kitchen remodels involving plumbing, electrical, or structural work require permits almost everywhere. A contractor who suggests skipping them is shifting risk entirely onto you. Unpermitted work can void your homeowner's insurance policy and create significant legal complications when you sell. It's also a sign they don't want their work inspected.

**No written change order process.** Ask directly: "How do you handle changes that come up mid-project?" The answer should be: written change order, signed by both parties, with agreed cost and timeline impact before the work proceeds. Any other answer — "we'll figure it out," "we'll square up at the end" — means disputes are coming.

## The Payment Schedule That Keeps You in Control

Structure your payments to maintain leverage from start to finish:

1. **10% at contract signing** — enough to demonstrate mutual commitment
2. **25% at demolition and rough-in start** — funds material procurement
3. **25% at rough-in completion and inspection passed** — tied to a verifiable, third-party-confirmed milestone
4. **25% at cabinet and countertop installation** — the most visible progress point
5. **15% at punch list completion** — your leverage for getting every detail right

The 15% holdback at the end is not negotiable. That's what gets the contractor back to fix the cabinet door that won't close flush and the grout line that was never sealed. Without it, small items have a way of never getting done.

Never pay ahead of progress. If a contractor asks for the next draw before the corresponding milestone is complete, that's the project in trouble — not a cash flow quirk.

## What You're Responsible For During the Build

The number-one cause of schedule overruns on kitchen remodels is homeowners who haven't made decisions. Not bad contractors. Homeowners who change the tile selection after demolition is done, or pick a countertop material after the cabinet install is underway, or add a window after rough electrical is roughed in.

Finalize every selection before demo day: cabinet door style, finish, and hardware; countertop material and edge profile; backsplash tile; flooring; lighting fixtures; faucet. If you're not sure about something, decide quickly — delay costs money in two directions, your contractor's time and their subcontractors' rescheduling fees.

Set a weekly walkthrough with your contractor. Thirty minutes, same time each week, review progress against the schedule and surface any issues before they compound. Document everything with photos. Communicate through one channel — email creates the best paper trail — and go through the GC for everything. Giving instructions directly to subcontractors undermines the GC's authority and creates contradictory direction on the job site.

A rough timeline for a mid-range remodel that stays on track:

- **Weeks 1–2:** Demo, rough plumbing, rough electrical
- **Weeks 3–4:** Inspections, drywall repair, prime coats
- **Weeks 5–6:** Cabinet installation, countertop templating
- **Weeks 7–8:** Countertop fabrication (lead times vary; order early)
- **Weeks 9–10:** Backsplash, painting, flooring, fixture installation
- **Weeks 11–12:** Appliance hookup, hardware, punch list, final walkthrough

## What to DIY Even With a Contractor on the Job

You can save $1,500–$3,500 by handling a few tasks yourself without touching anything that requires a license:

**Demolition.** Ripping out old cabinets, backsplash tile, countertops, and flooring is physically demanding but requires no specialized skill. Budget a full weekend with one helper. Confirm asbestos and lead testing before you start on pre-1980 kitchens.

**Painting.** Once the drywall is done and primed, painting the walls yourself saves $800–$2,000. Cutting in around new cabinets takes patience but not expertise.

**Hardware installation.** Cabinet pulls, knobs, and outlet/switch covers after everything's installed.

**Appliance shopping.** Buying appliances directly through a retailer instead of through the contractor typically saves 10–15%. Coordinate delivery to align with the installation week so you're not storing a refrigerator in the garage for three weeks.

Do not DIY plumbing, electrical, gas connections, or anything structural. Our guide on [when to call a plumber vs. DIY](/blog/when-to-call-a-plumber-vs-diy/) draws that line clearly if you're uncertain where it falls.

## Financing the Work

If you're not paying cash, home equity is the most common route — either a home equity loan (fixed rate, lump sum) or a HELOC (variable rate, draw as needed). A HELOC works well for a kitchen remodel because you draw incrementally as the project progresses rather than carrying the full balance from day one.

Avoid financing a kitchen remodel on a credit card unless you can pay it off within a promotional 0% window. The interest on a $40,000 balance at a standard APR eats the project's return on investment quickly. The financing options covered in our [bathroom remodel financing guide](/blog/bathroom-remodel-financing-options/) apply directly to kitchen projects at similar cost levels.

## The Hire That Pays for Itself

A kitchen remodel contractor who's good at their job earns their margin by managing complexity you shouldn't have to learn from scratch. What you're paying for is sequencing, accountability, and quality control across six or eight different trades.

The way you protect that investment is by hiring carefully — verifying the license and insurance yourself, getting a scope that specifies every material, structuring payments to milestones, and holding that final 15% until the punch list is closed. Do those four things, and you've eliminated most of what goes wrong on a kitchen remodel.
