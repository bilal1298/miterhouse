Tile flooring removal costs $2 to $6 per square foot when you hire a professional, or roughly $100 to $300 in tool rental and disposal fees if you do it yourself. For a 200-square-foot room, that means $400 to $1,200 for professional removal or about $200 for a weekend of hard physical labor on your own.

The cost spread depends on the type of tile, what it is bonded to, whether you need to preserve the subfloor, and how much prep the next flooring requires. Here is where the money actually goes.

## What Drives the Cost of Tile Removal

### Tile Type Matters

**Ceramic tile on thinset over concrete** is the most common and the easiest to remove. The tiles typically break away in pieces with a floor scraper or demolition hammer, and the remaining thinset can be ground off the concrete. This is the low end of the cost range.

**Porcelain tile** is denser and harder than ceramic. It bonds more aggressively to thinset and takes longer to remove. Expect labor costs at the higher end of the range.

**Natural stone tile** (marble, travertine, slate) is heavy, thick, and often set in a thicker mortar bed. Removal generates more weight to haul out and more impact on the subfloor. Stone removal can push professional costs to $5 to $8 per square foot.

**Tile set in a mud bed** (pre-1980s installations) involves removing not just the tile but also a 3/4-inch to 1-1/2-inch layer of sand-cement mortar underneath. This is heavy, time-consuming work that can double removal costs. The mud bed adds significant weight to disposal as well.

### Subfloor Type Matters More

**Tile over concrete slab:** Removal leaves the concrete intact. The main post-removal work is grinding off remaining thinset to create a smooth surface. A concrete grinder rental runs $50 to $100 per day. Professionals charge $1 to $3 per square foot for thinset grinding.

**Tile over cement backer board over plywood:** If the backer board comes up with the tile (it often does), you are left with the plywood subfloor, which may be in fine condition for new flooring. If you need to remove just the tile and leave the backer board, the job is harder and slower.

**Tile over plywood with no backer board:** This is a problem installation (tile should not have been installed this way). The thinset bonds to the plywood, and removing the tile often destroys the plywood surface. Plan on replacing the subfloor layer, adding $2 to $3 per square foot.

## The DIY Removal Process

### Safety First

Tile removal generates sharp debris, heavy dust, and significant noise. You need:

- Safety glasses (not just sunglasses)
- N95 respirator mask (tile dust and old thinset can contain silica)
- Heavy leather gloves
- Steel-toed boots or at minimum heavy work boots
- Hearing protection
- Long sleeves and pants

If your home was built before 1985, the adhesive or mortar under the tiles could contain [asbestos](https://www.epa.gov/asbestos). Do not disturb it until you have had it tested. Asbestos testing costs $25 to $75 per sample through a certified lab. If asbestos is present, professional abatement is legally required in most jurisdictions and costs $15 to $30 per square foot.

### Contain the Dust

Seal off the work area with plastic sheeting taped over doorways. Tile removal creates fine dust that will migrate throughout your house if you do not contain it. Cover any HVAC return vents in the room with plastic and tape. A box fan pointed out a window helps with ventilation while keeping dust moving out instead of in.

### Breaking Up the Tile

**For small areas (under 50 square feet):** A 3-inch hand-held floor scraper and a 3-pound hand maul work fine. Angle the scraper under a tile edge and strike it with the maul to pop tiles off. Work from an edge or a broken tile toward the center.

**For medium areas (50 to 200 square feet):** Rent an electric demolition hammer with a wide chisel bit ($60 to $100 per day). This is the same tool as a small jackhammer but with a flat scraper attachment. Let the tool do the work. Hold it at a shallow angle (15 to 20 degrees) under the tile and guide it forward. Going too steep gouges the subfloor.

**For large areas (over 200 square feet):** A ride-on floor scraper ($200 to $400 per day rental) removes tile dramatically faster but requires a clear, open floor to operate. For most residential jobs, the demolition hammer is the right tool.

### Removing Residual Thinset

After the tiles are off, the thinset ridges remain bonded to the substrate. You need a relatively smooth surface for whatever goes down next.

**On concrete:** Rent a concrete grinder with a diamond cup wheel. Move slowly in overlapping passes. The goal is not to remove every trace of thinset but to get the surface flat within 1/8 inch. Self-leveling compound handles the rest before new flooring goes down.

**On plywood:** Scrape what you can with a floor scraper. If significant thinset remains embedded in the wood grain, it may be easier to install a new layer of underlayment over the top rather than trying to achieve a perfectly clean surface.

## Disposal: The Cost People Forget

Tile is heavy. A 200-square-foot room of ceramic tile with thinset generates 800 to 1,500 pounds of debris. That is 10 to 20 heavy-duty contractor bags.

**Dumpster rental** for a small (10-yard) dumpster runs $250 to $400 for 3 to 5 days. This is the easiest option and lets you toss debris as you work rather than bagging it.

**Dump runs** in your own truck or trailer cost $30 to $80 per load at most municipal transfer stations, but you need a vehicle that can handle the weight. A standard pickup bed holds about 1,000 pounds of tile debris safely.

**Hauling services** charge $150 to $300 for a single pickup of tile debris from your driveway or garage.

Do not put tile debris in regular trash service. Most municipalities do not allow it in curbside collection.

## When Professional Removal Makes Sense

Hire a professional when:

- You suspect asbestos in the mortar or adhesive
- The area exceeds 300 square feet (the physical toll is significant)
- The tile is set in a mud bed (removing a mud bed requires experience to avoid damaging the structural subfloor)
- You need the subfloor preserved in perfect condition for the next installation
- Access is difficult (second floors with narrow stairways, finished basements with low ceilings)

## Timeline Expectations

**DIY removal of 200 square feet:**
- Tile breaking: 4 to 8 hours
- Thinset grinding: 2 to 4 hours
- Cleanup and disposal: 2 to 3 hours
- Total: 1 to 2 full days

**Professional removal of 200 square feet:**
- Typically completed in 4 to 6 hours by a two-person crew

**Mud bed removal** adds 50% to 100% more time regardless of who does the work.

## Saving Money on Tile Removal

**Do your own demolition.** Even if you hire a professional for the new flooring installation, removing the old tile yourself saves $400 to $1,000. Many flooring installers prefer working with a clean subfloor and will quote a lower installation price if the demo is already done.

**Share a dumpster.** If a neighbor is also renovating, splitting a dumpster rental cuts disposal costs in half.

**Time it with a larger project.** If you are already renting a dumpster for a bathroom remodel or kitchen renovation, tile removal debris goes in the same container at no additional cost.

**Check for rebates.** Some areas offer reduced disposal rates for construction debris that can be recycled. Concrete and unglazed ceramic tiles are recyclable at some facilities.

## What Comes After Removal

The condition of your subfloor after tile removal determines what you can install next and at what cost. Before shopping for new flooring:

1. Check the subfloor for flatness using a 6-foot straightedge. Mark high and low spots.
2. Test concrete for moisture using the plastic sheet method (tape a 2x2-foot sheet, check after 48 hours).
3. Verify plywood subfloor thickness and joist spacing if you plan to install new tile.
4. Address any squeaks, soft spots, or damage discovered during removal.

Getting this right before the new installation starts is what separates a floor that lasts 20 years from one that starts cracking in 2.
