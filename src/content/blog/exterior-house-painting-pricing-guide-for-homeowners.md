Painting the exterior of your home typically costs between $3,000 and $8,000 for a standard single-story house, or $5,000 to $14,000 for a two-story home when you hire professionals. The actual number depends on your home's size, the condition of existing surfaces, and your region's labor rates. Understanding where that money goes helps you set a realistic budget and avoid sticker shock when quotes come in.

## What Drives Exterior Painting Costs

The price you pay breaks down into three main buckets: labor, materials, and prep work. Labor usually accounts for 60 to 70 percent of the total. Materials — paint, primer, caulk, tape — run 20 to 30 percent. The remaining 10 to 20 percent covers prep-related items like power washing, scraping, and repairs.

Here is a rough breakdown for a 2,000-square-foot exterior surface area:

| Cost Component | Low Estimate | High Estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Labor | $1,800 | $9,000 |
| Paint and primer | $400 | $1,200 |
| Prep materials (caulk, tape, patching) | $100 | $400 |
| Power washing | $150 | $400 |
| Scaffolding or lift rental | $0 | $800 |
| **Total** | **$2,450** | **$11,800** |

Most homeowners in mid-cost-of-living areas land somewhere around $4,500 to $7,500 for a complete exterior repaint of a two-story house.

## Siding Type Matters More Than You Think

The material your house is clad in changes the cost significantly. Smooth surfaces like vinyl and aluminum take paint quickly and need less prep. Rough or porous surfaces like stucco, brick, and wood shingles absorb more paint and require more labor.

**Wood siding** demands the most prep. Old paint needs scraping, bare wood needs priming, and any rot needs replacing before a drop of finish coat goes on. Expect to pay $1.50 to $4.00 per square foot for wood siding.

**Vinyl siding** can be painted but requires a specific type of paint (100% acrylic latex) and you should never go darker than the original color — darker colors absorb heat and can warp the panels. Cost runs $1.00 to $2.50 per square foot.

**Stucco** soaks up paint. You will use 20 to 30 percent more paint on stucco than on smooth siding. Budget $1.50 to $3.50 per square foot, and consider elastomeric paint for its crack-bridging properties.

**Brick** is usually the most expensive to paint because of the texture and the amount of paint it absorbs. A typical range is $1.50 to $4.50 per square foot. Also worth knowing: once you paint brick, you are committed to repainting it every 5 to 7 years.

## The Prep Work Nobody Wants to Talk About

Prep is where painting projects succeed or fail, and it is the part most people underestimate. A professional crew typically spends 50 to 65 percent of total project time on preparation — not painting.

Here is what proper prep involves:

**Power washing.** Every exterior needs washing before painting. A professional power wash runs $150 to $400 depending on home size. The surface needs 24 to 48 hours to dry completely before priming.

**Scraping and sanding.** Loose, flaking, or peeling paint must come off. On older homes, this can take days. If your house was built before 1978, the existing paint may contain lead, and you will need to follow the [EPA's Renovation, Repair, and Painting (RRP) Program rules](https://www.epa.gov/lead/renovation-repair-and-painting-program). That means certified contractors, specific containment procedures, and proper disposal — all of which add cost.

**Caulking and patching.** Every gap around windows, doors, trim joints, and penetrations needs fresh caulk. Old, cracked caulk should be removed first. Wood rot, holes, and cracks in siding get filled with exterior-grade patching compound. Budget $100 to $300 for materials alone.

**Priming.** Bare wood, repaired areas, and stain-prone spots need a dedicated primer coat. A good exterior primer runs $30 to $50 per gallon. Skipping primer on bare surfaces means the finish coat absorbs unevenly and peels faster.

## Paint Quality: Where Not to Cheap Out

Exterior paint ranges from $25 per gallon for builder-grade to $80 or more per gallon for premium lines. The difference is not just marketing. Higher-quality paints contain more solids (the stuff that stays on the wall after the water evaporates) and better resins that resist UV degradation, cracking, and mildew.

For most homes, a mid-range to premium 100% acrylic latex paint is the best value. Brands like Benjamin Moore Aura Exterior, Sherwin-Williams Duration, and PPG Timeless all test well for longevity. Expect to spend $45 to $70 per gallon for a paint that will hold up 8 to 12 years.

A typical two-story house with 2,000 square feet of paintable surface needs 10 to 15 gallons of finish paint for two coats, plus 3 to 5 gallons of primer. That puts material cost at $550 to $1,100.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro

The DIY savings on exterior painting are real — you can cut the total cost by 60 to 70 percent because you are eliminating labor. But exterior painting is physically demanding, time-consuming, and comes with legitimate safety risks.

**DIY makes sense when:**
- Your home is single-story or you are comfortable working on ladders
- The existing paint is in decent shape (minimal scraping needed)
- You have a full week to dedicate to the project
- You can recruit a helper for the high and awkward spots

**Hire a pro when:**
- Your home is two stories or has steep roof lines that require scaffolding
- The existing paint is failing badly and needs extensive scraping
- Your home was built before 1978 (potential [lead paint issues](https://www.epa.gov/lead))
- You need the job done within a tight timeline

A DIY exterior paint job on a single-story ranch typically runs $600 to $1,500 in materials and equipment rental. The same job by a professional crew runs $3,000 to $5,000. The trade-off is your time — a solo DIYer should budget 40 to 60 hours of work.

## Getting Accurate Quotes

When you request quotes from painting contractors, ask for itemized bids rather than a single lump sum. A detailed quote should list:

- Square footage being painted
- Number of coats (should be two minimum)
- Paint brand and product name
- Prep work included (washing, scraping, caulking, priming)
- What is excluded (wood rot repair, lead paint abatement)
- Warranty terms

Get at least three quotes. If one bid comes in dramatically lower than the others, that is a red flag — they may be cutting corners on prep, using cheap paint, or planning to apply only one coat.

Check that your contractor carries both general liability insurance and workers' compensation. Verify their license through your state's contractor licensing board. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/) website has useful resources for understanding safety requirements related to home improvement projects.

## Timing Your Project

The best time to paint exterior surfaces is during dry weather with temperatures between 50 and 85 degrees Fahrenheit. In most of the US, that means late spring through early fall. Avoid painting in direct sunlight on hot days — the paint dries too fast and does not adhere properly.

Humidity matters too. Paint needs the moisture to evaporate as it cures. Relative humidity above 85 percent slows drying and can cause the finish to sag or run. Below 50 percent and the paint may dry before it levels out, leaving brush marks.

If you are working with contractors, booking in the off-season (late fall or early spring) can sometimes get you a 10 to 15 percent discount as crews look to fill their schedules.

## Hidden Costs to Watch For

Several expenses catch homeowners off guard:

**Wood rot repair.** If your contractor finds rot during prep, the repair is almost always an extra charge. Replacing a few boards of siding runs $200 to $800 depending on the extent. Major rot repair can add $1,000 or more.

**Lead paint abatement.** Homes built before 1978 may need certified lead-safe work practices. This adds $500 to $2,000 or more to the project, depending on the extent of the lead paint.

**Color changes.** Going from a dark color to a light one (or vice versa) usually requires an extra coat of primer and possibly a third finish coat. That adds 20 to 30 percent to material and labor costs.

**Trim and detail work.** Multi-color schemes with contrasting trim, shutters, and accent colors take significantly more time than single-color jobs. Expect a 15 to 25 percent premium.

## Making Your Paint Job Last

Once the project is done, basic maintenance extends the life of your paint job considerably:

- Wash the exterior annually with a garden hose and soft brush to remove dirt and mildew
- Recaulk gaps as they appear, especially around windows and doors
- Touch up chips and scratches within the first year before moisture gets behind the paint film
- Trim bushes and trees so they do not rub against the painted surface
- Clean gutters regularly to prevent water overflow staining

A well-prepped and properly painted exterior should last 7 to 12 years on wood siding and 5 to 7 years on stucco. If you used quality paint and the prep was thorough, you are looking at the upper end of those ranges. Cutting corners on either prep or paint quality means you will be doing it again sooner — and the second time around, you are paying to fix the first job's problems too.
