Interior painting runs $1.50 to $3.50 per square foot of wall area for professional work, or about $200 to $500 per room for a standard bedroom. If you handle it yourself, materials for a single room cost $100 to $250. Those numbers have crept up about 8 to 12 percent since 2023, driven mainly by labor rate increases and paint reformulations that improved quality but raised prices.

## Interior Painting Costs by Project Scope

The scope of your project determines which pricing tier you fall into. A single room is a weekend project. A full interior is a multi-week commitment with very different economics.

**Single room (walls only):**
- DIY: $100–$250 in materials
- Professional: $300–$800

**Single room (walls, ceiling, and trim):**
- DIY: $150–$350
- Professional: $500–$1,200

**3–5 rooms:**
- DIY: $350–$900
- Professional: $1,200–$4,000

**Full house interior (8–12 rooms):**
- DIY: $700–$2,000
- Professional: $4,000–$12,000

These ranges assume standard 8-foot ceilings and walls in reasonable condition. Vaulted ceilings, extensive wall damage, wallpaper removal, or lead paint issues all push costs higher.

## Understanding Paint Pricing Tiers

Paint is the single largest material cost, and the price range is wide. Here is what you get at each tier:

**Economy ($20–$30/gallon):** Brands like Glidden or store-brand options. Coverage is adequate but requires more coats in many situations. The finish dulls and scuffs faster. Acceptable for rentals, utility rooms, or closets where appearance matters less.

**Mid-range ($35–$55/gallon):** This is where most homeowners should shop. Behr Ultra, Sherwin-Williams SuperPaint, and Benjamin Moore Ben offer good coverage in two coats, reasonable durability, and a wide color selection. For the majority of rooms in a home, this tier delivers the best cost-to-quality ratio.

**Premium ($55–$80/gallon):** Benjamin Moore Aura, Sherwin-Williams Emerald, and PPG Diamond deliver superior hide (coverage per coat), stain resistance, and longevity. Worth the premium for kitchens, bathrooms, entryways, and any high-traffic room. Some premium paints achieve acceptable coverage in a single thick coat, though two thin coats still produce a better finish.

A gallon covers 350 to 400 square feet on smooth drywall. Textured surfaces reduce that to 250 to 300 square feet. Always round up when buying — having a partial gallon left over for touch-ups is far better than running short mid-wall.

## The Real Cost of Prep Work

Prep is where many DIYers underestimate both time and cost. It is also where professionals earn a significant portion of their fee.

**Basic prep (included in most pro quotes):**
- Cleaning walls — free if you do it yourself, included in pro labor
- Filling small nail holes and dings — $5–$10 in spackle
- Light sanding — $5–$10 in sandpaper or sanding sponges
- Taping edges — $15–$30 in painter's tape
- Drop cloths and plastic sheeting — $10–$25

**Moderate prep (often quoted separately):**
- Patching larger holes (fist-sized or bigger) — $10–$30 per patch in materials, $50–$150 per patch in labor
- Caulking window and door trim — $10–$25 in caulk
- Skim coating rough or damaged areas — $30–$60 in materials, $200–$500 in labor per room
- Priming stains, bare spots, or dark-to-light color changes — $20–$40 per gallon of primer

**Extensive prep (always quoted separately):**
- Wallpaper removal — $1–$3 per square foot for professionals
- Lead paint testing and safe handling — $10–$30 for test kits, $500–$2,000 for professional abatement
- Major drywall repair — $200–$800 depending on scope

If your walls are in good shape and you are repainting a similar color, prep is quick and cheap. If you are dealing with wallpaper, water stains, or old oil-based paint, prep can cost as much as the painting itself.

For homes built before 1978, the [EPA's Renovation, Repair, and Painting Program](https://www.epa.gov/lead/renovation-repair-and-painting-program) requires specific lead-safe practices whenever painted surfaces are disturbed. This applies to both DIYers and professionals, though the certification requirements differ.

## Sheen Selection and Why It Matters

Choosing the right sheen is a practical decision, not just an aesthetic one. The wrong sheen in a room creates maintenance headaches.

The progression from least shiny to most: flat, matte, eggshell, satin, semi-gloss, high-gloss. As sheen increases, so does durability and cleanability — but so does the visibility of wall imperfections.

**Flat/matte** works best on ceilings and in formal rooms where the walls will not be touched. It hides drywall imperfections better than any other sheen but marks permanently.

**Eggshell** is the default for most living spaces. Slight sheen, decent cleanability, good at masking minor surface issues. If in doubt, go eggshell.

**Satin** belongs in kitchens, bathrooms, kids' rooms, and hallways — anywhere that needs regular wiping. It shows wall imperfections more than eggshell, so wall prep matters more.

**Semi-gloss** is standard for all trim, baseboards, doors, and cabinets. Some homeowners use it on bathroom walls. Durable and easy to clean but highlights every bump and wave in the surface.

## Calculating Paint Quantities

Here is a practical method for figuring out exactly how much paint to buy for any room:

1. **Measure the perimeter** of the room in feet (add all wall lengths together)
2. **Multiply by ceiling height** in feet — this gives total wall area in square feet
3. **Subtract 21 square feet** for each standard door
4. **Subtract 15 square feet** for each standard window
5. **Divide by 350** for smooth walls or **300** for textured walls — this is gallons per coat
6. **Multiply by 2** for two coats
7. **Round up** to the next whole gallon

Example: A 14x16 room with 9-foot ceilings, two windows, and one door.
- Perimeter: 60 feet x 9 feet = 540 square feet
- Minus openings: 540 - 21 - 30 = 489 square feet
- Per coat: 489 / 350 = 1.4 gallons
- Two coats: 2.8 gallons
- Buy: 3 gallons

If you are painting multiple rooms the same color, add all the room calculations together before rounding. Buying a 5-gallon bucket instead of individual gallons saves 15 to 25 percent.

## Timing and Scheduling

Interior painting is less weather-dependent than exterior work, but conditions still matter.

**Temperature:** Keep the room between 60 and 80 degrees Fahrenheit. Paint applied in cold rooms dries too slowly and may not cure properly. In overly warm rooms, paint dries before it can level, leaving roller marks.

**Humidity:** Ideal range is 40 to 60 percent relative humidity. High humidity (above 70 percent) slows drying and can cause paint to sag. Use a dehumidifier if needed.

**Ventilation:** Open windows and use fans. Not just for comfort — proper airflow helps paint cure evenly and clears VOC fumes. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/) recommends adequate ventilation during all painting projects, and the [EPA's guidance on lead safety](https://www.epa.gov/lead) adds specific requirements for older homes.

**Scheduling pros:** Professional painters are busiest from April through October. Booking interior work during winter months (November through February) often gets you better availability and sometimes a 5 to 10 percent discount, since exterior work slows down and crews are looking for projects.

## Maximizing Value Whether You DIY or Hire

If you are doing it yourself, invest in the paint and the brush. These two items affect your result more than anything else. A quality 2.5-inch angled brush ($12–$18) cuts crisp lines and lasts for years with proper cleaning. A cheap brush sheds bristles into your paint and frays after one project.

If you are hiring a pro, get itemized quotes. Compare the scope of prep work, the paint brand and product specified, the number of coats, and what is included in cleanup. Our guides on [room painting costs](/blog/painting-rooms-pricing-guide-for-homeowners/) and [professional painting costs](/blog/professional-painting-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/) have detailed advice on evaluating contractor bids.

For the hybrid approach — doing prep yourself and hiring painters for the actual application — see our [room pricing guide](/blog/painting-room-pricing-guide-for-homeowners/) for tips on dividing the work effectively. This can cut 15 to 20 percent off a professional quote while keeping the skilled labor where it matters most.

One last thing: keep your leftover paint. Label the cans with the room name, color name, and date. Store them in a climate-controlled space (not a garage that freezes in winter). Touch-ups are inevitable — a ding from moving furniture, a scuff from a doorknob, a mark from a picture hanger. Having the exact paint on hand means a 5-minute fix instead of a trip to the store and a color-matching gamble.
