Attic insulation in 2026 costs between $0.80 and $3.50 per square foot installed, depending on the insulation type you choose. For a 1,000-square-foot attic — which is typical for a 1,500 to 2,000-square-foot home — that works out to $800 to $3,500 for a professional job. DIY brings the floor down to around $400 for basic blown-in cellulose.

Those numbers have shifted over the last few years. Cellulose prices have risen 10 to 15 percent due to recycled paper supply costs, while spray foam has actually stabilized as more contractors enter the market. Here is an updated cost estimator based on current pricing.

**For a quick estimate:** Take your attic floor area in square feet, pick your insulation type, and multiply by the per-square-foot cost. Then add 10 to 15 percent for waste, odd angles, and access challenges.

## What Actually Drives the Cost

Square footage and insulation type are the big variables, but several other factors affect your final bill.

### Current Insulation Level

If your attic already has R-19 of fiberglass batts and you need to reach R-49, you only need R-30 more — about 8 to 10 inches of blown-in cellulose over the top of the existing batts. This costs roughly 60 percent of a full insulation job because you are covering less depth.

If you are starting from zero (uninsulated attic), the full depth is needed and the cost reflects it.

### Attic Access and Layout

An attic with a pull-down ladder, an open floor plan, and good headroom is straightforward to insulate. An attic with a small scuttle hole, multiple levels, HVAC equipment blocking the path, and low eaves where the roof meets the floor — that takes twice the time and costs 20 to 40 percent more.

Spray foam contractors price access difficulty explicitly because their equipment includes hoses and a spray gun that requires room to maneuver. Tight spaces add $0.50 to $1.50 per square foot to spray foam pricing.

### Air Sealing Scope

Smart contractors bundle air sealing with insulation because the two work together. Air sealing alone costs $350 to $1,000 professionally but saves 15 to 25 percent on heating and cooling above what insulation alone provides.

If your contractor does not mention air sealing, ask about it. If they seem unfamiliar with the concept, find a different contractor. Any insulation professional worth hiring knows that air sealing is half the equation.

### Hazardous Material Removal

If your attic contains vermiculite insulation (often contaminated with asbestos), damaged or moldy insulation, or evidence of rodent infestation, removal must happen before new insulation goes in. This adds $1,000 to $4,000 depending on the material and contamination level. Asbestos-containing materials require licensed abatement — do not attempt DIY.

## Regional Price Variations

Insulation costs vary by geography more than most home improvement projects because of climate-driven demand and local labor rates.

**Northeast and Upper Midwest:** Higher demand for insulation means more contractors and competitive pricing, but higher R-value requirements (R-49 to R-60) mean more material. Expect the upper end of the price ranges.

**South and Southwest:** Lower R-value requirements (R-30 to R-38) mean less material, but fewer insulation contractors means less price competition. Costs are mid-range.

**Pacific Northwest:** High demand due to energy codes, moderate R-value requirements. Pricing tends toward the higher end due to labor costs.

**Rural areas:** Fewer contractors may mean higher per-project costs because of travel time, or it may mean lower overhead and more competitive pricing from smaller operators. Get quotes from both local and regional companies.

## Rebates and Tax Credits in 2026

Federal energy efficiency tax credits under the Inflation Reduction Act continue through 2032. As of 2026:

- **Tax credit:** 30 percent of insulation costs up to $1,200 per year for qualifying insulation and air sealing.
- **Qualifying products:** All insulation types that meet [Energy Star](https://www.energystar.gov/) specifications, including blown-in, batts, and spray foam.

Many state and utility companies offer additional rebates of $200 to $1,000 for attic insulation. Check the DSIRE database or your utility's website for current programs in your area.

These credits and rebates can effectively reduce the cost of a professional insulation job by 25 to 40 percent. Factor them into your budget — they make the higher-performing insulation options much more affordable.

## Timeline for the Project

**DIY blown-in (1,000 sq ft):** 1 day for air sealing, 1 day for insulation. You need a helper for blown-in — one person feeds the machine, the other operates the hose in the attic. Total commitment: a weekend.

**Professional blown-in:** 3 to 6 hours for a crew of two. They will seal major air leaks, install baffles at the soffits, blow the insulation, and clean up in a single visit.

**Professional spray foam:** 1 to 2 days depending on the area. The foam needs to cure, and some contractors prefer to spray in multiple passes (especially closed-cell) to manage heat buildup during the curing reaction.

## How to Evaluate Quotes

When comparing contractor quotes, make sure each one specifies:

- **Insulation type and brand.** Not just "blown-in" — which product specifically?
- **Target R-value and depth.** "R-49" and "14 inches of cellulose" should both appear.
- **Air sealing scope.** Is it included? What specifically gets sealed?
- **Baffle installation.** Soffit baffles must be installed to maintain attic ventilation. This should be in the quote.
- **Existing insulation handling.** Are they blowing over it, removing it, or compressing it? (Compressing it reduces its effective R-value.)
- **Cleanup.** Who cleans up overspray, debris, and excess material?

A quote that simply says "insulate attic — $2,000" is not detailed enough to compare against anything. Insist on specifics.

## The Bottom Line

For most homeowners in 2026, blown-in cellulose over the existing attic floor is the best value: $800 to $1,500 professionally installed, payback in 3 to 5 years through energy savings, and decades of service life. Spray foam makes sense for cathedral ceilings and conditioned attic spaces where an air barrier matters as much as R-value. Fiberglass batts remain an option for DIYers who prefer hands-on installation without machinery, but blown-in products outperform them in real-world conditions because they fill gaps that batts cannot.

Whatever you choose, start with air sealing. It is the highest-return step in the process, and skipping it is like weatherstripping a door while leaving a window open.
