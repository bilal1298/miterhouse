You'll spend $0.80 to $3.50 per square foot for professionally installed attic insulation in 2026. For a 1,000-square-foot attic — the norm for a 1,500 to 2,000-square-foot home — that's $800 to $3,500. Go the DIY route with blown cellulose and the floor drops to around $400.

Cellulose prices have climbed 10–15% thanks to recycled paper supply costs, while spray foam has actually stabilized as more contractors enter the market. Here's where every option lands right now.

**Quick estimate:** Multiply your attic floor area by the per-square-foot cost, then add 10–15% for waste and access challenges.

Blown cellulose is the right call for most attic floors. It's the cheapest per R-value, fills every gap and corner, and the machine rental is free at big box stores when you buy enough bags. Spray foam makes sense for cathedral ceilings and conditioned attic spaces where you need an air barrier as much as R-value. Batts work for DIYers who prefer hands-on work without machinery, but they underperform blown products in real-world conditions because they can't fill irregular spaces.

## What Actually Drives the Price

### Existing Insulation Level

If your attic already has R-19 of fiberglass batts and you're targeting R-49, you only need R-30 more — about 8–10 inches of blown cellulose over the existing batts. That costs roughly 60% of a full insulation job.

Starting from bare joists? You're paying for the full depth.

### Access and Layout

A pull-down ladder, open floor plan, and decent headroom? Straightforward job. A tiny scuttle hole, multiple levels, HVAC equipment blocking the path, and low eaves where the roof meets the floor? That takes twice the time and adds 20–40% to the bill.

Spray foam contractors price access difficulty explicitly because the hoses and spray gun need room to maneuver. Tight spaces add $0.50–$1.50 per square foot.

### Air Sealing

This is the step that separates a good insulation job from a waste of money. Air sealing costs $350–$1,000 professionally but saves 15–25% on heating and cooling above what insulation alone provides.

If your contractor doesn't mention air sealing, ask about it. If they seem confused by the question, hire someone else. Any insulation professional worth paying knows that stopping air leaks is half the equation.

### Hazardous Material Removal

Vermiculite insulation (often contaminated with asbestos), moldy insulation, or rodent-infested material all need removal before new insulation goes in. That adds $1,000–$4,000 depending on contamination level. Asbestos requires licensed abatement — don't touch it yourself. ## Regional Price Swings

Insulation costs vary more by geography than most home projects because of climate-driven demand and local labor rates.

**Northeast and Upper Midwest:** More contractors and competitive pricing, but higher R-value requirements (R-49 to R-60) mean more material. Expect the upper ranges.

**South and Southwest:** Lower R-value targets (R-30 to R-38) mean less material, but fewer insulation contractors means less price competition. Mid-range costs.

**Pacific Northwest:** High demand from aggressive energy codes, moderate R-value requirements. Pricing skews high due to labor costs.

**Rural areas:** Fewer contractors may mean higher per-project costs from travel time, or lower overhead from smaller operators. Get quotes from both local and regional companies.

## The 30% Federal Tax Credit

The Inflation Reduction Act credit runs through 2032. In 2026:

- **30% of insulation and air sealing costs** up to $1,200 per year
- **All insulation types qualify** as long as they meet [Energy Star](https://www.energystar.gov/) specifications
- State and utility rebates stack on top — often $200–$1,000 more

These credits and rebates can cut the effective cost of a professional job by 25–40%. That makes the higher-performing options much more affordable. Factor them into your budget before choosing materials.

## Project Timeline

**DIY blown-in (1,000 sq ft):** One day for air sealing, one day for insulation. You need a helper — one person feeds the machine, the other operates the hose in the attic. Budget a full weekend.

**Professional blown-in:** 3–6 hours for a two-person crew. They'll seal major air leaks, install soffit baffles, blow the insulation, and clean up in a single visit.

**Professional spray foam:** 1–2 days depending on area. The foam cures, and some contractors spray in multiple passes (especially closed-cell) to manage heat buildup during the curing reaction.

## Evaluating Quotes

Every quote should specify:

- **Insulation type and brand** — not just "blown-in," but which product
- **Target R-value and installed depth** — "R-49" and "14 inches of cellulose" should both appear
- **Air sealing scope** — is it included? What gets sealed?
- **Soffit baffles** — must be installed to maintain attic ventilation
- **Existing insulation plan** — blowing over, removing, or compressing? (Compressing reduces effective R-value)
- **Cleanup** — who handles overspray, debris, and excess material?

A quote that says "insulate attic — $2,000" tells you nothing. Insist on the details.

## The Bottom Line

Blown-in cellulose over the existing attic floor is the best value for most homeowners in 2026: $800–$1,500 professionally installed, 3–5 year payback through energy savings, and decades of service life. But whatever you choose, start with air sealing. Skipping it is like weatherstripping a door while leaving a window open.
