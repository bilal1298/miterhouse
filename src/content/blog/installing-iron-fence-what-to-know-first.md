Wrought iron and ornamental iron fencing adds serious curb appeal and security to a property, but it comes at a premium. Professional installation runs $30 to $75 per linear foot — roughly 2 to 3 times the cost of chain link and on par with high-end vinyl or composite. For a typical 150-linear-foot front yard, you are looking at $4,500 to $11,250 installed.

Whether you are considering true wrought iron, modern tubular steel (which most people call "iron"), or aluminum alternatives, here is what the installation involves, what it costs, and where the project gets complicated.

## Iron vs. Steel vs. Aluminum — What You Are Actually Buying

Almost no residential fence sold today is genuine wrought iron. True wrought iron is hand-forged, costs $50 to $100+ per linear foot for materials alone, and is reserved for custom estate work and historic restorations.

What most contractors and home centers sell as "iron fence" is actually **tubular galvanized steel** with a powder-coated finish. It mimics the look of traditional ironwork at a fraction of the cost. Steel panels are manufactured in standard sizes (typically 6 or 8 feet wide by 3, 4, or 6 feet tall) and installed between posts.

**Aluminum ornamental fencing** is the third option. It looks almost identical to steel from 10 feet away but weighs about one-third as much, never rusts, and costs 20 to 30 percent less. The trade-off is durability — aluminum bends more easily than steel and is less suitable for security applications.

**Material cost comparison per linear foot (panels only):**

| Material | 4-ft height | 6-ft height |
|---|---|---|
| Tubular steel | $15–$30 | $25–$50 |
| Aluminum | $12–$24 | $20–$40 |
| True wrought iron | $50–$100+ | $75–$150+ |

## What Professional Installation Costs

The total installed price includes panels, posts, concrete, gates, hardware, and labor. Here is how it breaks down for a standard tubular steel fence.

**Posts:** Steel posts matching the panel style run $30 to $80 each. They are set in concrete-filled holes, typically 30 to 42 inches deep depending on frost line. Post spacing matches panel width (6 or 8 feet).

**Panels:** Pre-fabricated panels in standard heights. $100 to $300 per panel depending on style and height.

**Gates:** A 4-foot walk gate with hinges, latch, and self-closing hardware runs $200 to $600. A double-swing driveway gate (10 to 12 feet wide) runs $800 to $2,500. Automated driveway gates with openers start at $2,000 and can exceed $5,000.

**Labor:** Professional installation labor runs $15 to $30 per linear foot. The work is moderately skilled — post placement must be precise because the pre-fabricated panels have no adjustment tolerance. Posts that are off by even half an inch create visible gaps or binding.

**Total installed costs:**

| Project Scope | Tubular Steel | Aluminum |
|---|---|---|
| 50 LF front yard with gate | $2,000–$4,500 | $1,500–$3,500 |
| 150 LF perimeter with 2 gates | $5,500–$12,000 | $4,000–$9,000 |
| 300 LF full property | $10,000–$22,000 | $7,500–$16,500 |

## Can You Install Iron Fence Yourself?

Pre-fabricated tubular steel and aluminum panels are designed for professional installation, but a capable DIYer can handle it. The panels are assembled — you are setting posts and attaching panels, not welding or fabricating.

**What makes it doable:**
- Panels are pre-assembled and come with brackets.
- Post holes follow a predictable layout based on panel width.
- No specialized skills beyond digging, mixing concrete, and using a level.

**What makes it challenging:**
- Posts must be perfectly plumbed and spaced to the exact panel width. There is no forgiveness in the system — if a post is 1/4 inch off, the panel bracket will not align.
- Iron and steel panels are heavy. A 6-foot-tall, 8-foot-wide steel panel weighs 40 to 60 pounds. You need a helper.
- Cutting panels to fit odd dimensions requires a metal-cutting saw and generates sparks that can damage the powder coating. Touch-up paint is essential.
- Slopes require racking (angling the panel to follow grade) or stepping (level panels at staggered heights). Most pre-fab panels can rack 1 to 2 inches per panel width. Beyond that, you need custom-cut stair-step panels.

**DIY cost for 150 LF with materials and tool rental:** $2,500 to $5,500. The savings over professional installation are real but more modest than with simpler fence types because the materials are the dominant cost, not labor.

### DIY Installation Steps

**Mark the post locations.** Use stakes and string to mark the fence line. Mark each post center at exactly the panel width apart — measure three times. Transfer corner and gate post positions from your site plan.

**Dig post holes.** Use a power auger (rent for $50 to $75 per day) to dig holes 10 to 12 inches in diameter and 30 to 42 inches deep. The [IRC code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) specifies minimum post depth based on frost line in your region.

**Set posts in concrete.** Drop 3 inches of gravel in each hole for drainage. Set the post, plumb it with a level on two adjacent faces, and brace it temporarily. Fill with concrete, crown the top so water sheds away from the post. Do not disturb the posts for 24 to 48 hours.

**Attach panels.** Once the concrete is cured, mount the panel brackets to the posts and set the panels. Most systems use self-tapping screws or bolts. Work from one end to the other, checking level and alignment as you go.

**Install gates.** Hang gate panels on hinges, adjust for plumb and clearance, install the latch and self-closing mechanism. Test the swing — gates should self-close without slamming and latch securely.

**Touch up.** Any scratches, drill holes, or cut edges need immediate touch-up with matching paint to prevent rust. The bare steel underneath the powder coat will rust within weeks if exposed.

## Maintenance Expectations

**Tubular steel:** Inspect annually for chips, scratches, and rust spots. Touch up immediately with a rust-inhibiting primer and matching paint. In coastal or high-humidity environments, expect more frequent touch-ups. A well-maintained steel fence lasts 30 to 50 years.

**Aluminum:** Virtually maintenance-free. The material does not rust. The powder coating may fade over 15 to 20 years and can be refreshed with spray paint. Aluminum fences last 30+ years with no structural maintenance.

**True wrought iron:** Requires sanding, priming, and repainting every 3 to 5 years to prevent rust. Beautiful but high-maintenance.

## Permits, Codes, and Property Lines

Iron fencing typically requires a fence permit ($50 to $200) and must comply with local height restrictions, setback requirements, and style guidelines — especially in HOA-governed neighborhoods and historic districts. Many HOAs mandate specific styles of ornamental iron and restrict chain link, making iron the default choice regardless of preference.

Pool enclosures have additional requirements: self-closing, self-latching gates, minimum 48-inch height, and maximum 4-inch spacing between pickets. Most standard ornamental iron panels meet pool code, but verify with your local building department.

## Is Iron Fence Worth the Cost?

Iron fencing returns roughly 50 to 65 percent of its cost at resale according to industry estimates, which is in line with most fencing types. The real value is in the daily use — security, curb appeal, and the fact that you will not be replacing it in 15 years the way you would a wood fence.

For front yards and highly visible applications, the aesthetic premium is usually worth it. For large backyard perimeters where cost per foot matters more than appearance, [chain link](/blog/professional-chain-link-fence-installation-costs-and-tips/) or [woven wire](/blog/installing-woven-wire-fence-what-to-know-first/) deliver more footage for less money.
