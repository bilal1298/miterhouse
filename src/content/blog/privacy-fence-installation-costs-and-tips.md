A privacy fence does one job that no other fence type does as well: it creates a visual barrier between your property and everything outside it. Whether you want to block a neighbor's sight lines, contain noise from a busy road, or simply create a secluded backyard space, the privacy fence is the solution — but material choice, height, and installation quality determine whether that barrier lasts 10 years or 25.

Privacy fence installation costs $20 to $55 per linear foot installed, depending on the material and height. For a typical 150-foot backyard perimeter, that works out to $3,000 to $8,250. DIY installation can cut the cost by 30% to 45%, bringing materials-only costs to $10 to $30 per linear foot.

## Material Options Compared

The privacy fence market has four serious contenders. Each has real trade-offs.

### Wood (Cedar or Pressure-Treated Pine)

Wood is the most popular privacy fence material, and for good reason — it costs less upfront, looks natural, and can be customized to almost any design.

**Cedar** costs $12 to $25 per linear foot for materials. It is naturally resistant to rot and insects, weathers to a silver-gray if left untreated, and accepts stain beautifully. Its main limitation is durability in ground contact — cedar posts should be set in gravel or on concrete rather than buried directly in soil.

**Pressure-treated pine** costs $8 to $18 per linear foot. It handles ground contact better than cedar (the chemical treatment prevents rot), but it does not look as good, tends to warp and twist more as it dries, and the green tint of fresh treated lumber requires staining for aesthetic purposes.

Both require staining or sealing every 2 to 4 years to maintain appearance and prevent premature decay. Skip this maintenance and a wood privacy fence may only last 8 to 12 years instead of 15 to 20+.

### Vinyl (PVC)

Vinyl privacy fencing costs $20 to $40 per linear foot for materials. It never needs painting, resists rot and insects, and maintains a clean appearance for 20 to 30 years with only occasional washing.

The drawbacks: higher upfront cost, limited color options (mostly white, tan, and gray), and a manufactured appearance that some homeowners find unappealing. In extreme cold, vinyl can become brittle and crack from impacts. In extreme heat, it can warp if installed without adequate expansion room.

For a detailed breakdown, see our [PVC fence installation guide](/blog/installing-pvc-fence-what-to-know-first/).

### Composite

Composite fencing costs $25 to $50 per linear foot. Made from a blend of wood fiber and plastic, it offers the most natural wood-like appearance of any low-maintenance option. It is heavier than vinyl, which gives it a more substantial feel and better wind resistance.

The tradeoff is cost — composite is the most expensive common fencing material. It also cannot be cut and modified as easily as wood in the field.

### Metal Privacy Panels

Corrugated metal or steel privacy panels cost $15 to $30 per linear foot. They give a modern, industrial look and last 25 to 40 years. They are gaining popularity in contemporary home designs but are still a niche choice for residential privacy fencing.

## Height: What Code Allows and What Works

Most municipalities allow 6-foot fences in back yards and 4-foot fences in front yards without a variance. Some areas permit up to 8 feet in back yards with a permit. Check local code before ordering materials — building a fence 6 inches too tall can result in a forced modification.

For actual privacy:
- **4 feet** blocks the view from ground level but anyone standing can see over it
- **6 feet** blocks sightlines from people standing in an adjacent yard — this is the standard privacy height
- **8 feet** blocks views from raised decks and second-story windows — effective but can feel imposing

If code limits you to 6 feet but you need more screening, add a lattice topper (12 to 18 inches). Many jurisdictions allow lattice above the solid fence height limit because it does not fully block airflow. Alternatively, plant tall shrubs behind the fence for the additional screening.

## Installation: The Details That Matter

### Post Spacing and Sizing

For privacy fences up to 6 feet tall, use 4x4 posts spaced every 8 feet, set in concrete at least 2 feet deep (deeper in frost areas). For 8-foot fences, upgrade to 6x6 posts — the added height creates significantly more wind leverage on the post.

Gate posts should always be 6x6, regardless of fence height. Gates swing, slam, and bear load in ways that regular fence posts do not. Undersized gate posts are the most common premature failure point in privacy fences.

### Proper Post Setting

Dig holes 10 to 12 inches in diameter and at least one-third the total post length deep. For a 6-foot fence with an 8-foot post, that means 24 to 30 inches of post below grade.

Pour 4 to 6 inches of gravel at the hole bottom for drainage. Set the post, check plumb on two sides, brace with temporary stakes, and pour concrete. Crown the concrete above grade so water sheds away from the post. Standing water at the post base is the primary cause of premature post rot, even with pressure-treated lumber.

### Three-Rail vs. Two-Rail Construction

Privacy fences use horizontal rails between the posts that support the vertical pickets (boards).

**Two-rail construction** uses a top rail and a bottom rail. It is adequate for fences up to 5 feet and in low-wind areas. It costs less and is faster to build.

**Three-rail construction** adds a middle rail. This prevents pickets from warping and bowing between rails, significantly improves wind resistance, and is the standard for 6-foot and taller fences. The additional rail adds $2 to $4 per linear foot in materials and 10% to 15% more labor time.

For any fence where you want longevity, go with three rails. The difference in material cost is minimal compared to the improvement in durability.

### Picket Attachment: Board-on-Board vs. Side-by-Side

**Side-by-side** (also called stockade) places pickets flush next to each other on the same side of the rails. It is faster to install and uses less lumber. The downside: as boards shrink over the first year, gaps open between them, reducing privacy.

**Board-on-board** (also called shadowbox) overlaps adjacent pickets by 1 to 1.5 inches, alternating which side of the rail they mount on. This creates a fence with no direct sight lines, even after boards shrink. It uses 15% to 20% more lumber but maintains complete privacy indefinitely.

For a fence where privacy is the primary goal, board-on-board is worth the extra material cost.

### The Finished Side

Decide which side faces out. Convention (and many local codes) requires the "finished" side (smooth pickets facing out) to face the neighbor or the street, with the rails and posts visible on your side. Some homeowners build board-on-board specifically because it looks good from both sides.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Not checking property lines.** A survey costs $200 to $500 and is the only way to know exactly where your property ends. Building even partially on a neighbor's property can result in forced removal. This is especially important when the fence line runs along the side yard between houses.

**Ignoring drainage.** A solid privacy fence blocks water flow during heavy rain. If the fence runs along a low point in the yard, install gaps at grade level or use gravel board (a sacrificial bottom board) so water can drain through without pooling against the fence.

**Using the wrong fasteners.** Galvanized or stainless steel screws are mandatory for outdoor fencing. Standard zinc-plated screws rust within a year and leave black streaks on cedar. Ring-shank nails hold better than smooth nails in wood fencing.

Per the [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) and the [American Wood Council](https://www.awc.org/), treated lumber used in ground contact must carry a minimum retention level of 0.40 pcf for posts and must be rated for the intended exposure. Using above-ground-rated lumber in ground contact voids any decay warranty.

## DIY vs. Professional: The Honest Math

A 150-foot cedar privacy fence installed professionally costs approximately $4,500 to $6,750. The same fence in a DIY installation costs $1,800 to $3,000 in materials plus $50 to $100 for tool rentals (post hole auger, primarily).

The savings are real — $2,000 to $4,000 on a typical project. But the time investment is significant: 2 to 3 full weekend days for an average-skill DIYer with one helper. If your time is worth more than the savings, hiring out makes sense.
