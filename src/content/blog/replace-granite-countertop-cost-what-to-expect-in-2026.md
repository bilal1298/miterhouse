Replacing an existing granite countertop costs between $2,500 and $8,000 for most kitchens in 2026, including removal of the old slab, new material, fabrication, and professional installation. The extra step of removing existing granite — which is heavier and more tightly bonded than laminate — adds $300 to $800 to what a fresh installation would cost.

If your granite is cracked, badly stained, or just dated, replacement makes more sense than repair once the damage exceeds a single cosmetic chip. Here is a thorough look at what drives the cost and how to navigate the process without overspending.

## Why Replace Rather Than Repair?

Not every damaged granite countertop needs full replacement. Knowing the threshold saves you from an unnecessary $5,000 project.

**Repair makes sense when:** You have a single chip or crack shorter than 6 inches, a stain limited to one area, or minor seam separation. Granite repair specialists can fill chips with color-matched epoxy for $200 to $500 per repair. Stain extraction (poulticing) costs $100 to $300 per treatment.

**Replacement makes sense when:** You have multiple cracks, widespread staining that has penetrated below the surface, a broken slab segment, or the granite is a dated color or pattern that hurts your home's appeal. Also consider replacement if your granite is only 2cm thick (common in installations from the early 2000s) and you want the more substantial look and durability of modern 3cm slabs.

## Cost Breakdown for Replacement Projects

Replacement involves two phases that new installations skip: removal and potential repair of what is underneath.

### Phase 1: Removal Costs

| Task | Cost Range |
|------|-----------|
| Granite slab removal | $300–$600 |
| Disposal/hauling | $100–$200 |
| Cabinet inspection and repair | $0–$500 |
| Backsplash repair (if damaged during removal) | $200–$800 |
| Plumbing disconnect | $100–$250 |
| Subtop repair or replacement | $75–$200 |

Granite removal is tricky because the slabs are typically adhered with silicone to a plywood subtop and clipped to undermount sinks. A careful removal crew can sometimes preserve the sink, but plan to replace it — especially if it is an older model that may not fit new cutout dimensions.

### Phase 2: New Installation Costs

New granite runs the same rates as a first-time installation: $50 to $130 per square foot fully installed, depending on grade. But replacement projects have two unique cost factors.

First, if your cabinets have suffered any water damage, sagging, or structural fatigue under the old granite, they need repair before the new stone goes on. I have seen cabinet repair add $200 to $500 to replacement projects — it is not glamorous spending, but skipping it means your new countertop sits on a compromised base.

Second, your backsplash will almost certainly need attention. Granite countertops are typically installed tight against the backsplash with caulk, and removing the countertop damages the bottom row of tile or the drywall behind it. Budget $200 to $800 for backsplash repairs, or use this as an opportunity to upgrade the backsplash entirely.

## Replacement Material Options

You are not limited to granite-for-granite. Many homeowners replacing dated granite switch materials entirely.

**Granite to granite:** Straightforward swap. Your fabricator can often reuse the same template dimensions if you are keeping the same sink and layout. Cost stays in the $50 to $130 per square foot range.

**Granite to quartz:** A popular upgrade path. Quartz runs $55 to $150 per square foot installed, never needs sealing, and offers more consistent coloring. The trade-off is that quartz cannot handle direct heat from pots — you need trivets.

**Granite to quartzite:** Natural stone with quartz-like durability. Runs $70 to $200 per square foot installed. It is harder than granite and resists etching better than marble, making it a strong option for heavy-use kitchens.

**Granite to butcher block:** A dramatic aesthetic change. Costs $40 to $80 per square foot installed. Works best on islands or supplementary counter sections rather than full-kitchen coverage because of maintenance requirements.

If your replacement is part of a [broader kitchen renovation](/blog/home-additions-and-renovations-what-homeowners-should-know/), coordinate the countertop timeline with cabinet and flooring work. Countertops should go in after cabinets are set and leveled but before the final backsplash installation.

## The Removal Process: What Actually Happens

Understanding how removal works helps you prepare your kitchen and set realistic expectations.

**Day before removal:** Clear everything off the countertops and out of the sink area. Move small appliances, dish racks, and anything breakable away from the work zone. Disconnect the garbage disposal if you have one — or let the plumber handle it.

**Removal day (2 to 4 hours for most kitchens):**

1. The crew disconnects the plumbing and removes the sink
2. They score the silicone adhesive bond between granite and cabinets using a thin blade
3. The slab gets pried up in sections (sometimes whole pieces, sometimes broken into manageable chunks if the layout prevents whole-piece removal)
4. They inspect the subtop and cabinet boxes for damage
5. Old material gets loaded onto the truck for disposal

The dust and noise are modest compared to demolition-heavy projects, but cover nearby furniture and block doorways to adjacent rooms with plastic sheeting. Silicone adhesive residue on the cabinet tops needs to be scraped clean before new countertops can go on.

## Salvaging and Reusing Components

You can reduce replacement costs by reusing components that are still in good condition.

**Undermount sinks:** If your sink is in good shape and the new countertop cutout dimensions match, reusing it saves $200 to $600 on a new sink plus installation. Confirm with your fabricator during templating that the existing sink will work.

**Faucets:** Almost always reusable unless you are changing hole configurations. A plumber can reinstall your existing faucet for $75 to $150 versus buying new.

**Support brackets:** If you have island overhangs with support brackets, the existing brackets may work with the new stone if they are properly anchored to the cabinet structure. Have the installer verify they are still secure.

**Cabinet hardware:** Drawer pulls and hinges are unaffected by countertop work. One less thing to worry about.

## Timing and Planning

The total timeline from decision to finished replacement is typically 3 to 5 weeks:

- **Week 1:** Get quotes, visit slab yards, select material
- **Week 2:** Templating visit (after you have chosen your fabricator)
- **Weeks 3-4:** Fabrication
- **Week 4-5:** Removal of old countertop and installation of new one (usually same day or consecutive days)

One important detail: you will be without a functional kitchen sink for at least a full day, sometimes two if the plumber cannot reconnect immediately after installation. Set up a temporary dishwashing station in the laundry room or bathroom, and plan on takeout or simple meals for 24 to 48 hours.

## Is Replacing Granite Worth It?

The [EPA's WaterSense program](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) recommends updating plumbing fixtures during countertop replacement to improve water efficiency — it is a natural time to swap in a low-flow faucet or touchless model since the plumbing is already disconnected.

From a pure financial standpoint, countertop replacement recovers roughly 60 to 70% of costs at resale. But the daily quality-of-life improvement matters too. A cracked, stained countertop makes your kitchen feel worn even if everything else is in good shape, and it is the surface you interact with most frequently.

If your granite is functional but you just want a different look, consider whether professional refinishing ($500 to $1,500) might achieve what you want at a fraction of replacement cost. Some fabricators can even re-polish and re-seal existing granite to restore its original appearance.

For homes built before 1978, be aware that [lead paint](https://www.epa.gov/lead) may be present in the kitchen — any demolition or removal work should follow EPA renovation guidelines if your home falls in this age range.

## Bottom Line

Budget $3,500 to $7,000 for a standard granite-to-granite replacement in a typical kitchen. Add $1,000 to $2,000 if you are upgrading to quartz or quartzite, and set aside 15% as contingency for the surprises that hide under every old countertop. The process is manageable, the disruption is brief, and the result is one of the most visible improvements you can make in your kitchen.
