A full interior paint job on a typical 1,500 to 2,500-square-foot home costs between $3,500 and $9,000 when hiring professionals, or $500 to $2,000 if you do it yourself. The single biggest factor in that range is labor — a professional crew charges $25 to $75 per hour per painter, and a whole-house interior takes 3 to 7 days with a two-person crew. Here is how to understand where every dollar goes.

## Whole-House Interior Painting Costs

When contractors quote a whole-house interior, they typically price by the square foot of paintable wall area or by the room. Here is what the numbers look like for different home sizes:

| Home Size (sq ft) | Number of Rooms | Pro Cost Range | DIY Cost Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000–1,500 | 5–7 | $2,500–$5,500 | $400–$1,000 |
| 1,500–2,500 | 7–10 | $3,500–$9,000 | $600–$1,600 |
| 2,500–3,500 | 10–14 | $6,000–$13,000 | $900–$2,200 |
| 3,500+ | 14+ | $9,000–$18,000 | $1,200–$3,000 |

These ranges include walls only. Ceilings add 30 to 50 percent to the total. Trim, doors, and crown molding are usually quoted separately and can add $1,000 to $3,000 for a whole house.

## Where the Money Actually Goes

Understanding the cost breakdown helps you decide where to invest and where you can save.

**Paint:** The material cost for a full interior runs $400 to $1,500, depending on the quality you choose and how many colors are involved. A single-color scheme (walls one color, trim another) uses less paint overall because you buy in bulk. Three or more wall colors means more individual gallons and more leftover waste.

**Labor:** Professional painters charge $1.50 to $3.50 per square foot of wall area, or $200 to $800 per room. Labor accounts for 70 to 80 percent of a professional quote. The wide range reflects regional differences — painters in Manhattan charge very differently from painters in Memphis.

**Prep work:** Wall repairs, patching, sanding, cleaning, and caulking typically run $0.50 to $1.00 per square foot when included in a professional bid. Some contractors include basic prep in their price; others charge separately for anything beyond light sanding and dusting.

**Trim and detail work:** Painting baseboards, window casings, door frames, and crown molding is time-intensive. Expect $1 to $3 per linear foot for trim. A house with 500 linear feet of trim could add $500 to $1,500.

## Paint Selection: What Actually Matters

After painting more than 30 rooms across several houses, I have strong opinions on paint. The short version: mid-range to premium 100% acrylic latex paint outperforms everything else for interior walls.

**Budget paint ($20–$30/gallon):** Covers adequately in two coats on a good day, but the finish dulls faster, shows scuffs sooner, and does not clean well. For a rental or a room you plan to repaint in a year, it works. For your own home, spend more.

**Mid-range paint ($35–$50/gallon):** The sweet spot. Products like Sherwin-Williams Cashmere, Benjamin Moore Regal Select, and Behr Marquee offer excellent coverage, good durability, and reasonable prices. Most homeowners should start here.

**Premium paint ($50–$80/gallon):** Benjamin Moore Aura, Sherwin-Williams Emerald, and similar lines offer the best coverage per coat, superior stain resistance, and the longest-lasting finishes. Worth it for high-traffic areas, kitchens, and bathrooms. Whether you need it for a guest bedroom is debatable.

The coverage claims on the can (350–400 square feet per gallon) assume smooth, primed surfaces. Textured walls, porous drywall, and dark-to-light color changes will reduce actual coverage by 15 to 30 percent. Always buy more than the math says you need.

## The VOC Question

Paint releases volatile organic compounds as it dries — that "new paint smell" is actually chemicals off-gassing into your home. Most modern interior paints are low-VOC by default, but if you are painting bedrooms, nurseries, or spaces where someone has chemical sensitivities, look for zero-VOC options.

The [EPA has detailed guidance on VOCs and indoor air quality](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality). Key takeaway: ventilate well during and for 48 to 72 hours after painting, regardless of the VOC rating.

Zero-VOC paint used to mean compromised performance, but that gap has closed significantly. Products like Benjamin Moore Natura and Sherwin-Williams Harmony are genuinely zero-VOC and perform well.

## Room-by-Room Strategy for a Whole House

Painting an entire interior is a different animal than painting one room. Here is how to approach it efficiently:

**Start upstairs, work down.** This prevents dust and drips from contaminating already-finished rooms. If you are doing ceilings, do every ceiling first before any walls.

**Group by color.** If six rooms are the same wall color, buy in 5-gallon buckets and do those rooms consecutively. This saves setup and cleanup time, reduces waste, and ensures perfect color match across rooms.

**Do all trim last.** After every wall and ceiling is done, come back and do all the trim in one pass. Trim requires different tools (angled brush, possibly a small roller for doors) and a different sheen, so it is more efficient to batch it.

**Hallways and stairwells are the hardest.** These spaces have minimal floor area for setting up, lots of cutting in around doorframes, and often require ladder work. Budget extra time for these. If you hire out any part of the job, the hallways and stairwell are the rooms where a pro earns their money.

## Common Whole-House Mistakes

**Choosing colors under store lighting.** Paint looks completely different under fluorescent store lights versus natural light in your home. Buy sample quarts and paint large test patches (at least 2x2 feet) on the actual walls. View them at different times of day.

**Ignoring the ceiling.** A fresh wall color next to a dingy, yellowed ceiling looks wrong. If you are painting all the walls, paint the ceilings too — even if it is just fresh white. Ceiling paint is cheaper ($15–$25/gallon) and goes quickly.

**Underestimating time.** A solo DIYer painting a 2,000-square-foot home should budget 80 to 120 hours of work. That is four to six full weekends, assuming nothing goes wrong. If you are doing this while living in the house (moving furniture back and forth, working around bedtime for kids), add 30 to 50 percent more time.

**Skipping primer where it matters.** New drywall, patched areas, and dramatic color changes need primer. Paint-and-primer combos work for same-shade repaints, but they are not a substitute for real primer on bare or problem surfaces.

## Hiring Painters for a Whole House

When hiring for a full interior, negotiate the whole-house price rather than per-room rates. Contractors offer better pricing for larger jobs because they can plan more efficiently and keep their crew busy for multiple days.

Red flags in a painting estimate:
- Only one coat mentioned (two is the standard)
- No paint brand or product name specified
- "Prep included" without specifying what prep entails
- No timeline or start date commitment
- Cash-only pricing with no written contract

Request references from jobs similar in scope to yours. A painter who excels at single-room repaints may not have the crew or logistics experience for a full-house job.

Also verify that contractors follow applicable safety guidelines. For homes built before 1978, the [EPA's lead paint program](https://www.epa.gov/lead) requires specific certifications for anyone disturbing painted surfaces. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/) has additional safety resources for home improvement projects.

## Getting the Most From Your Budget

If you want professional results on a homeowner budget, consider a hybrid approach: do the prep work yourself (patching, sanding, cleaning, taping) and hire painters for the actual painting. Some contractors are willing to work this way, and it can cut 15 to 25 percent off the labor cost because prep is the most time-consuming part.

Another option: paint the easy rooms yourself and hire out the difficult spaces. Bedrooms with standard ceilings and minimal trim are straightforward DIY. Stairwells, vaulted-ceiling rooms, and kitchens with lots of cabinet work are where professional skill pays for itself.

If you are painting yourself and doing the whole house, invest in quality tools once rather than cheap tools per room. A professional-grade brush and roller frame set costs $40 to $60 and will last through every room. Cheap tools make the work harder and produce worse results. Your largest single purchase should be the paint itself — put your money there and into a good 2.5-inch angled brush. Those two things affect your final result more than anything else.
