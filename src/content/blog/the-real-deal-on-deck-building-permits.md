Most deck building projects require a permit, and the process costs between $75 and $500 depending on your municipality, deck size, and whether the deck is attached to the house. Skipping the permit to save a few hundred dollars puts you at risk of fines, forced demolition, and serious problems when you sell your home. The process is more straightforward than most homeowners expect once you understand what the building department actually needs from you.

## When Is a Deck Permit Required?

The short answer: almost always if the deck is more than 30 inches above grade. But the specifics vary by city, county, and state. Here are the general rules that apply in most US jurisdictions.

**A permit is typically required when:**
- The deck surface is more than 30 inches above finished grade at any point
- The deck is attached to the house (ledger board connection)
- The deck exceeds 200 square feet (some jurisdictions set this at 100 square feet)
- The project includes electrical wiring for lighting or outlets
- The deck is built over a walkout basement or egress window

**A permit may not be required when:**
- The deck is freestanding, ground-level, and under 200 square feet
- The deck surface is less than 30 inches above grade
- No electrical work is involved
- The structure is considered a "platform" rather than a deck by local code

The only way to know for certain is to call your local building department. Spend 10 minutes on the phone before you spend $10,000 on lumber. Most building departments can tell you within minutes whether your project needs a permit based on a basic description.

## What the Permit Process Looks Like

### Step 1: Gather Your Documents

Most building departments require a permit application that includes:

**A site plan** showing your property boundaries, the house footprint, and where the deck will go. This does not need to be professionally drawn. A scaled sketch on graph paper showing dimensions and distances from property lines is typically sufficient.

**Deck plans** showing the structural design: post locations, beam sizes, joist spacing, ledger connection details, railing height and configuration, and stair layout. For a standard rectangular deck, many building departments accept plans from deck design software or manufacturer-provided span tables.

**Material specifications** listing the lumber species and grade (e.g., #2 Southern Yellow Pine, pressure-treated for ground contact), fastener types, and connector hardware.

[Simpson Strong-Tie](https://www.strongtie.com/) publishes free deck-building guides with details on connector selection and code-compliant connections that building departments recognize and accept.

### Step 2: Submit and Pay

Submit your application and plans in person or online, depending on your jurisdiction. Pay the permit fee, which is calculated based on the project's estimated value.

**Typical permit fees:**

| Deck Size | Estimated Value | Typical Permit Fee |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Under 200 sq ft | $3,000–$8,000 | $75–$200 |
| 200–400 sq ft | $8,000–$20,000 | $150–$350 |
| Over 400 sq ft | $20,000+ | $250–$500 |

Some jurisdictions charge a flat fee; others calculate based on a percentage of the project value (typically 1% to 2%).

### Step 3: Wait for Approval

Plan review takes 3 to 15 business days in most jurisdictions. Simple decks with standard framing may get same-day approval. Complex designs, elevated decks, or projects requiring engineering reviews take longer.

If the plans need revisions, the building department will provide specific feedback. This is not a rejection. It is a request for more detail or corrections. Address the notes and resubmit.

### Step 4: Build (With Inspections)

Once your permit is approved, you build. But the permit comes with required inspections at specific milestones. Typical inspection points for a deck:

1. **Footing inspection** before pouring concrete. The inspector verifies hole depth (below frost line), diameter, and location.
2. **Framing inspection** after the substructure is complete (posts, beams, joists, ledger) but before decking goes on. This is the most critical inspection.
3. **Final inspection** after the deck is complete, including railings, stairs, and any electrical work.

Schedule inspections 24 to 48 hours in advance. Do not cover or proceed past an inspection point before the inspector signs off. If you pour concrete and then call for a footing inspection, you have created a problem.

## Common Code Requirements for Decks

Building codes exist to prevent decks from collapsing. This is not theoretical. The [International Residential Code (IRC)](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) section on decks was significantly expanded after multiple deck collapse incidents at gatherings and parties caused serious injuries and deaths.

### Ledger Board Connection

The connection between the deck and the house is the most failure-prone point. Code requires:
- Lag screws or through-bolts into the rim joist of the house (not just into siding or sheathing)
- Flashing over the top of the ledger to prevent water intrusion into the house framing
- No attachment to cantilevered floor systems without engineering review
- Specific fastener spacing based on the span and load

### Footings

Deck post footings must extend below the frost line, which varies from 12 inches in the Deep South to 48 inches or more in northern states. Minimum footing diameter is typically 12 to 16 inches for standard residential loads. Concrete must reach 2,500 PSI compressive strength (standard bag mix achieves this).

### Railing Requirements

Guardrails are required when the deck surface is more than 30 inches above grade. The minimum railing height is 36 inches for residential decks (some jurisdictions require 42 inches). Baluster spacing must not allow a 4-inch sphere to pass through. This prevents children from getting their heads through the railing.

### Stair Requirements

Stairs must have a maximum rise of 7-3/4 inches and a minimum tread depth of 10 inches. All risers must be consistent in height within 3/8 inch of each other. A graspable handrail is required on at least one side for stairs with four or more risers.

## What Happens If You Build Without a Permit

Building without a required permit is not a gray area. It creates specific, tangible problems.

**Fines.** Most jurisdictions impose fines for unpermitted work, typically double the original permit fee. Some municipalities assess daily fines until the situation is resolved.

**Forced removal.** The building department can order you to tear down the unpermitted structure. This is rare for first offenses but does happen, especially for decks that are clearly unsafe or violate setback requirements.

**Insurance liability.** If someone is injured on an unpermitted deck, your homeowner's insurance may deny the claim. The logic is straightforward: the structure was not built to code, was not inspected, and the risk was not disclosed.

**Resale complications.** Title searches and buyer inspections flag unpermitted structures. You may be required to obtain a retroactive permit (which requires the deck to pass inspection as-built, which it may not), remove the deck, or reduce the sale price. In competitive markets, unpermitted structures kill deals entirely.

**Retroactive permits** are available in some jurisdictions. They cost more than original permits (often 2x to 4x the fee) and require the deck to pass inspection in its current state. If the deck does not meet code, you tear it apart enough to make corrections, then rebuild.

## Setback Requirements: The Rule Nobody Remembers

Most zoning codes require structures to be set back a minimum distance from property lines. For decks, the typical setback is 5 to 15 feet from side property lines and 10 to 25 feet from rear property lines. A deck that violates setback requirements may need to be modified or removed regardless of whether you have a building permit.

Check your property survey for exact lot lines. The fence line is not always the property line. Getting this wrong is expensive.

## Working With Your Building Department

Building inspectors are not adversaries. They are checking that your deck will not collapse under a load and that the connection to your house will not leak water and rot the framing. A few tips for a smooth process:

**Be prepared.** Have your plans, material lists, and site plan ready before your first visit or call. Departments that see organized applicants move them through faster.

**Ask questions early.** If you are unsure about a span table, connection detail, or footing requirement, ask during plan review rather than during inspection. It is much cheaper to change a plan on paper than to change framing on site.

**Be present at inspections.** You are not required to be there, but being present lets you ask questions, understand any corrections, and build a working relationship with the inspector who will return for the next checkpoint.

**Keep your permit posted.** Display the permit card at the job site in a visible, weather-protected location. This is a code requirement in most jurisdictions.

## Permits for Deck Repairs and Modifications

Replacing decking boards, staining, and minor repairs typically do not require a permit. But structural modifications do. Adding a section, changing post locations, replacing the ledger, or adding a roof or screen enclosure to an existing deck usually triggers a new permit.

When in doubt, call the building department. The phone call is free. The consequences of guessing wrong are not.

## Bottom Line

A deck permit costs a fraction of the deck itself and provides structural validation, legal protection, and insurance coverage. The process takes 1 to 3 weeks from application to approval for a standard deck. Factor this timeline into your project planning, prepare your documents, and treat the building department as a resource rather than an obstacle. Your deck will be safer, your liability will be lower, and your future self will thank you when it is time to sell.
