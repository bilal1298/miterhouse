Installing a bathroom vanity countertop costs $200 to $3,000 depending on the material, with labor adding $150 to $500 if you hire it out. The good news is that this is one of the most approachable bathroom upgrades for a confident DIYer — most countertop swaps take a single Saturday from start to finish, and the visual impact is immediate.

Whether you are replacing a tired laminate top with quartz or installing a countertop on a brand-new vanity as part of a larger [bathroom remodel](/blog/80s-kitchen-remodel-honest-advice-for-your-home/), this guide walks through the real costs, material trade-offs, and installation details that matter.

## Countertop Material Costs

### Prefabricated Options (Budget-Friendly)

Prefab countertops come ready to install with the sink cutout already made. They are designed to fit standard vanity sizes (24, 30, 36, 48, and 60 inches wide).

- **Cultured marble:** $100 to $300 for a single-sink vanity top. Integrated sink and backsplash. Light, easy to install, available everywhere. Looks decent but shows scratches over time.
- **Laminate:** $50 to $200. The cheapest option. Comes in many patterns including stone imitations. Not waterproof at seams. Works fine in a powder room but avoid in full baths.
- **Solid surface (Corian-type):** $200 to $500 for a prefab top. Repairable, seamless appearance, decent durability. A solid middle-ground option.
- **Prefab granite:** $200 to $600. Real stone, pre-cut and polished. Limited color choices but genuine stone at a fraction of custom cost.

### Custom Fabricated Options (Mid to High End)

Custom countertops are measured, cut, and finished to your specific vanity dimensions. They allow any sink type, any edge profile, and any configuration.

- **Granite (custom):** $400 to $1,200 for a single vanity. Beautiful, heat-resistant, unique patterns. Requires sealing annually.
- **Quartz (engineered stone):** $400 to $1,500. Non-porous, no sealing needed, consistent color. The most popular choice for bathroom vanity upgrades in the last five years.
- **Marble:** $500 to $2,000. Elegant but high-maintenance. Etches from acidic products (including many bathroom cleaners). Best for powder rooms where exposure is minimal.
- **Quartzite (natural stone):** $600 to $2,500. Harder than granite, resists etching better than marble. Stunning appearance, but the premium price is real.
- **Concrete:** $500 to $2,000. Custom-poured and sealed. Industrial aesthetic, heavy, requires periodic sealing. Not a typical DIY material.

### The Hidden Cost: Sink and Faucet Compatibility

Your countertop choice dictates your sink options. Prefab cultured marble tops come with an integrated sink — no additional purchase needed. Custom stone countertops need a separate sink ($50 to $400) and a cutout ($100 to $200 extra from the fabricator).

Undermount sinks require specific support clips and adhesive, adding complexity to the install. Drop-in (top-mount) sinks are simpler — they just sit in the cutout. Vessel sinks (bowls that sit on top) need only a small drain hole in the countertop, which makes them popular for DIY countertop installs.

## Labor Costs

### DIY Installation

For a prefab vanity top, the labor is free and the learning curve is shallow. You need:
- Silicone caulk ($5 to $10)
- A drill/driver
- Adjustable wrench for plumbing connections
- Level
- Pencil and measuring tape
- Possibly a jigsaw for minor trimming

### Professional Installation

- **Prefab top installation:** $100 to $250 if added to another service call
- **Custom stone template and install:** $200 to $500 for the installation itself (fabrication cost is separate)
- **Plumbing reconnection:** $100 to $200 if done by a plumber as a standalone job

Most custom countertop fabricators include installation in their quote, so the labor cost is folded into the total price. When comparing quotes, confirm whether installation is included.

## Step-by-Step Installation: Prefab Countertop

This is the process for replacing an existing countertop on an existing vanity — the most common scenario.

**1. Turn off the water.** Shut off both supply valves under the sink. Open the faucet to drain remaining water. Place a bucket under the P-trap.

**2. Disconnect plumbing.** Remove the P-trap (hand-tighten fittings usually come apart without tools). Disconnect the supply lines from the faucet. If you are reusing the faucet, carefully remove it from the old countertop.

**3. Remove the old countertop.** Check for screws from underneath (inside the cabinet) holding the top to the vanity. Cut through any caulk along the wall and backsplash with a utility knife. Lift the old top off. This usually requires a helper for stone tops.

**4. Check level.** Set a 2-foot level on the vanity cabinet in multiple directions. Shim the vanity if needed — an unlevel cabinet causes countertop rocking, poor water drainage, and visible gaps at the backsplash.

**5. Dry-fit the new countertop.** Set it in place without adhesive. Check that it sits flush against the wall, overhangs evenly at the front, and the sink drain aligns with the cabinet plumbing.

**6. Install the faucet first.** It is much easier to install the faucet, drain assembly, and supply connections before the countertop is permanently attached. Flip the countertop upside down on a padded surface and install all hardware.

**7. Set the countertop.** Apply a bead of silicone along the top edges of the vanity cabinet. Carefully set the countertop in position. Press firmly and check alignment. For wall-mounted backsplash pieces, apply silicone to the bottom edge and press into place.

**8. Reconnect plumbing.** Hook up supply lines to the faucet (hand-tight plus a quarter turn with a wrench). Reassemble the P-trap. Turn on water and check for leaks at every connection.

**9. Caulk.** Apply a neat bead of silicone caulk where the countertop meets the wall, where the backsplash meets the wall, and around the sink rim if you installed a drop-in sink. Use painter's tape for clean lines and smooth with a wet finger.

The whole process takes 2 to 4 hours for a single vanity. A double vanity adds about an hour.

## Custom Stone: What the Process Looks Like

If you are going with a custom granite or quartz countertop, the process is different because you are not doing the fabrication yourself.

1. Choose your material at a stone yard or showroom
2. The fabricator sends a templater to your home after the vanity is installed and leveled
3. Templating takes 30 to 45 minutes
4. Fabrication takes 5 to 10 business days
5. The fabricator returns with the finished piece and installs it — usually 1 to 2 hours

The templating step is critical. The fabricator creates an exact pattern of your vanity dimensions, wall angles, and sink/faucet locations. This is why the vanity must be fully installed and level before templating. Measure twice, template once.

### Considerations for Stone Countertops

Stone is heavy. A 48-inch granite vanity top weighs 50 to 80 pounds. Getting it through doorways and positioned correctly without chipping an edge requires two people and some care.

Stone also needs proper support. Most vanity cabinets are designed for the weight of a countertop, but floating vanities and open-shelf designs need verification. Check that the cabinet can handle the load before committing to a 100-pound quartzite slab.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Not checking for square walls.** Bathroom walls are rarely perfectly straight or square. If your countertop is cut perfectly square and your wall has a 1/4-inch bow, you will have a visible gap. Custom fabrication accounts for this; prefab tops may need scribing (trimming to match the wall profile).

**Overtightening faucet connections.** Brass compression fittings crack when over-torqued. Hand-tight plus a quarter to half turn is all you need. If it leaks, tighten an eighth turn at a time.

**Skipping the leak test.** Run both hot and cold water for at least 5 minutes after connecting everything. Check every joint, including the drain tailpiece, P-trap, and supply connections. Then check again 24 hours later.

**Forgetting the overflow.** If your sink has an overflow hole, it needs to connect to the drain assembly. Some prefab tops with integrated sinks have this built in. If you are installing a separate sink, verify the overflow connection is included in the drain kit.

## Matching Your Countertop to the Overall Bathroom

The countertop sets the tone for the entire bathroom aesthetic. A few practical combinations that work well:

- **Tight budget, rental property, or kids' bathroom:** Cultured marble prefab. It looks clean, holds up fine, and replacing it in 5 years costs very little.
- **Mid-range primary bathroom:** Quartz in a neutral tone. Pair with a quality [undermount sink](/blog/kitchen-plumbing-remodel-a-diyers-honest-take/) and a single-hole faucet for a clean, modern look.
- **High-end guest or primary bath:** Natural stone (marble or quartzite) with an undermount sink and wall-mounted faucet. This is the combination that makes bathrooms feel finished and intentional.

The vanity countertop is one of the few bathroom elements where spending a bit more produces a noticeably better result. A $400 quartz top feels and looks meaningfully different from a $100 cultured marble top — and you will touch it every single day.

For most homeowners, the sweet spot is a prefab quartz or solid-surface top installed DIY, with professional plumbing help if needed. Total project cost: $300 to $600, one Saturday of work, and a bathroom that feels updated without the cost of a full renovation.
