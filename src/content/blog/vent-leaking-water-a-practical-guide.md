Water dripping from a vent -- whether it is an HVAC register, a plumbing vent pipe, or a roof vent -- usually has one of four causes: condensation, a roof leak channeling water along the vent pipe, a failed boot seal around the vent penetration, or a cracked vent pipe itself. The fix ranges from free (adjusting your thermostat) to $500 or more (replacing a roof boot), but you need to identify which type of vent and which cause before spending a dollar.

Here is how to diagnose vent leaks, fix the common ones, and know when to call a roofer or plumber.

## HVAC Vent Leaking Water

This is the most common type of "vent leak" homeowners report. Water drips from or around an air conditioning supply register or return, usually on the ceiling. Despite appearances, the vent itself is rarely the problem.

### Likely Causes

**Condensation on the ductwork.** When humid air meets cold metal duct surfaces, moisture condenses and drips. This is especially common in attics with poor insulation around the ducts, or in very humid climates. You will see the most dripping during peak cooling season.

**Clogged condensate drain line.** Your AC system produces gallons of condensate water daily. If the drain line clogs (algae growth is the usual culprit), that water backs up and overflows the drain pan, often finding its way to the nearest vent opening. This is the single most common cause.

**Frozen evaporator coil.** A dirty air filter, low refrigerant, or a failing blower motor can cause the evaporator coil to freeze. When the system cycles off, the ice melts and overwhelms the drain pan.

### How to Fix It

**For condensation:** Insulate the ductwork running through unconditioned spaces. R-6 or R-8 duct insulation wrap costs $0.50 to $1.50 per linear foot and dramatically reduces condensation. Also check that your duct joints are sealed with mastic or foil tape (not standard cloth duct tape, which fails over time).

**For a clogged drain line:** Locate the drain line exit (usually a PVC pipe near the outdoor unit). Use a wet/dry vacuum to suction the clog from the outside end. To prevent future clogs, pour a cup of distilled white vinegar into the drain pan monthly during cooling season.

**For a frozen coil:** Replace the air filter first -- a $5 fix that solves the problem about 40% of the time. If the coil continues to freeze with a clean filter, you likely have a refrigerant issue that requires an HVAC technician.

## Plumbing Vent Pipe Leaking

Your plumbing system has vent pipes (usually 1.5-inch to 3-inch PVC or cast iron) that run from the drain system up through the roof. These vents equalize pressure in the drain system and allow sewer gas to exit above the roofline.

### Where the Water Comes From

The vent pipe itself does not carry water under normal conditions. When water appears around a plumbing vent, it is almost always entering from the roof penetration -- the hole where the pipe passes through the roof.

**Failed vent boot (most common).** The rubber or neoprene boot that seals the vent pipe to the roof deteriorates after 10 to 20 years. UV exposure cracks the rubber, and water follows the pipe down into the house. This shows up as stains on the ceiling around the vent pipe location.

**Cracked flashing.** Metal flashing around the vent pipe can corrode, crack, or separate from the roofing material. This is especially common on older roofs.

**Ice dams (cold climates).** In winter, warm moist air rising through the vent can cause ice to form at the top of the pipe. This ice can block the vent and cause water to back up. It can also cause ice to build up around the boot, lifting shingles and creating entry points.

### Repair Options

**Replacing a vent boot** costs $10 to $25 for the boot itself. The challenge is working on the roof. If you are comfortable on a roof and the pitch is moderate (6/12 or less), this is a DIY job:

1. Carefully lift the shingles around the existing boot
2. Remove the old boot
3. Slide the new boot over the pipe
4. Apply roofing sealant under the flange
5. Replace the shingles over the boot flange
6. Seal the top of the boot to the pipe with a bead of roofing sealant

If the roof is steep, tall, or you are not comfortable working at height, hire a roofer. They charge $150 to $350 per vent boot replacement, which is reasonable given the safety considerations.

**For ice dam issues:** Install a vent pipe extension or a vent cap designed for cold climates. These allow air flow while preventing ice buildup. Cost is $15 to $40 for the cap.

## Roof Vent Leaking

Separate from plumbing vents, roof vents include ridge vents, soffit vents, gable vents, and powered attic ventilators. Each can leak for different reasons.

**Ridge vents** leak when the filter material deteriorates, when wind-driven rain pushes under the cap, or when the nail sealant fails. Re-sealing a ridge vent with compatible sealant costs under $20 in materials. Replacing the ridge vent entirely costs $300 to $600 for a typical home.

**Soffit vents** rarely leak water into the house. When they do, it is usually because the soffit itself has deteriorated, allowing water to enter through the eave rather than the vent. Inspect the soffit material around the vent for soft spots, gaps, or deterioration.

**Powered attic vents** have a flashing ring where they penetrate the roof. Like plumbing vent boots, this seal deteriorates. Replacing the flashing or applying roofing sealant is the fix. Cost: $15 to $30 for materials.

## Diagnosing the Source

Finding where the water actually enters is more important than where it drips. Water can travel along rafters, pipes, and wires for several feet before dripping, so the visible drip point may be far from the actual entry point.

**Step 1: Check the attic.** If you have attic access, go up there during or immediately after a rain event with a flashlight. Follow the water trail from the drip point back to its source. Look for:

- Water tracks on the underside of the roof deck
- Damp insulation
- Stains on rafters or framing
- Daylight visible through the roof (in a dark attic)

**Step 2: Check the vent penetrations.** Examine every pipe, duct, and wire that passes through the roof. Look for cracked boots, corroded flashing, and gaps in sealant.

**Step 3: Garden hose test.** If you cannot find the source during rain, have someone go on the roof with a garden hose and wet one section at a time, starting at the lowest point and working up. You stay in the attic and watch for water entry. This is slow but reliable.

## When to Call a Professional

Call a roofer if:
- The roof pitch is steep (8/12 or greater)
- You are not comfortable working at height
- The leak involves multiple penetrations or a large area
- The roof is old and the repair may uncover more extensive damage

Call a plumber if:
- The vent pipe itself is cracked or damaged inside the wall
- The leak is traced to an interior plumbing vent joint
- You suspect a [sewer gas issue](/blog/plumbing-pipes-repairs-a-homeowners-guide/) (rotten egg smell)

Call an HVAC technician if:
- The evaporator coil keeps freezing despite a clean filter
- The condensate drain pan is cracked or corroded
- You suspect refrigerant issues

Water intrusion of any kind should be addressed promptly -- according to the [IRC building code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/), moisture management is a fundamental requirement for healthy housing, and the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) and [OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/) both recognize prolonged moisture exposure as a health hazard.

The key takeaway: a leaking vent is rarely about the vent itself. Trace the water to its actual entry point, and you will find a manageable repair rather than a mystery.
