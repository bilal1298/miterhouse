The average cost of cabinet painting runs $3,000 to $7,000 for a professional job on a standard kitchen, or $200 to $500 in materials if you tackle it yourself. Bathroom vanity cabinets are considerably less — $300 to $800 professionally, $50 to $150 DIY — since you are dealing with far fewer doors and a smaller footprint.

Cabinet painting is arguably the highest-impact, lowest-cost kitchen update you can do. Before you commit to a [full kitchen overhaul](/blog/80s-kitchen-remodel-honest-advice-for-your-home/), a fresh coat on the cabinets may be all you need.

## What Determines the Price

### Number of Cabinet Pieces

Professionals price primarily by the piece — each door, drawer front, and end panel is a unit. Cabinet boxes (the frames visible when doors are open) are usually quoted as a lump sum.

The typical kitchen breakdown:

- **Small kitchen (10–15 doors/drawers):** $1,800 to $3,500 professional
- **Medium kitchen (20–30 doors/drawers):** $3,000 to $6,000 professional
- **Large kitchen (35+ doors/drawers):** $5,000 to $10,000 professional

Per-piece pricing generally falls between $50 and $100 per door or drawer front. Larger doors (pantry, corner lazy Susan) cost more. Small drawer fronts cost less.

### Cabinet Condition

Cabinets in good structural condition with a smooth, intact finish are straightforward to paint. Cabinets with damage, peeling veneer, grease buildup, or previous bad paint jobs require extra prep time.

Add $500 to $1,500 for:
- Stripping old failing paint
- Repairing veneer delamination
- Filling gouges, dents, or router damage
- Sanding and re-profiling damaged door profiles

### Paint Quality and Finish Type

The paint product affects both cost and longevity. Here is what each level actually costs and delivers:

**Standard acrylic-alkyd ($40–$60/gallon):** Products like Benjamin Moore Advance or Sherwin-Williams ProClassic. Self-leveling, durable, moderate dry time. Two gallons covers most kitchens. This is the sweet spot for most projects.

**Premium hybrid ($55–$80/gallon):** Products like Sherwin-Williams Emerald Urethane Trim Enamel. Harder finish, better adhesion, and faster recoat time. Slightly easier to work with. Worth the upgrade for dark colors where finish quality is critical.

**Professional lacquer/conversion varnish ($80–$150/gallon):** Applied only by professionals in spray booths. The hardest, most durable finish available. Factory-smooth appearance. This is what high-end cabinet shops use.

### Geographic Pricing Variation

Cabinet painting costs vary significantly by region:

- **Major metros (NYC, LA, SF, Chicago):** 20% to 40% above national average
- **Mid-size cities and suburbs:** Generally at the national average
- **Rural areas and small towns:** 10% to 20% below average, but fewer specialists available

Labor rates drive most of this variation. Materials cost roughly the same everywhere.

## DIY vs. Professional: An Honest Comparison

### Where DIY Saves Real Money

Materials for a DIY cabinet paint job: $200 to $500. Professional quotes for the same kitchen: $3,000 to $7,000. That gap is entirely labor, because cabinet painting is extremely labor-intensive.

A professional crew spends 3 to 5 days on a typical kitchen. You will spend 5 to 10 days spread over 2 to 3 weekends — but your labor is free.

### Where DIY Falls Short

Finish quality. There is no getting around this. A professional spraying doors in a dust-free booth produces a flawless finish that is nearly impossible to replicate in a garage with a rented sprayer.

The biggest DIY finish problems:
- **Brush marks** on flat-panel doors
- **Orange peel** from improper spray technique or distance
- **Dust nibs** from particles landing in wet paint
- **Runs and drips** from applying too much at once
- **Adhesion failure** from inadequate prep

If you are painting white or a light neutral, many of these imperfections are less visible. If you are going dark (navy, charcoal, black), every flaw shows. Plan your color choice according to your skill level.

### The Hybrid Approach

Some homeowners paint the cabinet boxes themselves and send the doors out to a professional spray shop. This saves roughly 30% to 40% compared to full professional service while getting the factory finish on the parts you see most.

Professional door-only spray service: $40 to $80 per door, including prep and two coats. For a 25-door kitchen, that is $1,000 to $2,000 for the doors, plus your labor on the boxes.

## Prep Work That Cannot Be Skipped

### Degreasing

Kitchen grease is invisible and everywhere. Years of cooking deposits a thin film on every surface, especially upper cabinets near the range. TSP (trisodium phosphate) solution works well. Scrub every surface, then rinse with clean water and let dry completely.

If your home was built before 1978, test for lead paint before any sanding or scraping. The [EPA's lead safety guidelines](https://www.epa.gov/lead) apply to any disturbance of pre-1978 painted surfaces.

### Sanding

Use 120-grit sandpaper or a sanding sponge on every surface that will be painted. You are creating mechanical tooth for the primer to grip — not removing the existing finish. Sand with the grain on wood doors. Wipe all dust with a tack cloth.

For slick factory finishes (thermofoil, high-gloss lacquer), use a liquid deglosser before sanding. The chemical etching gives sandpaper something to work with.

### Priming

Non-negotiable. A bonding primer (shellac-based like Zinsser BIN, or a dedicated cabinet primer like Stix) creates the bridge between the old surface and your new paint. Without it, even the best paint will chip.

One coat of primer, lightly sanded with 220-grit after it cures, gives you a surface that paint loves to stick to.

## The Painting Process

For brush-and-roll applications:

1. Paint the cabinet boxes first — they are harder to access and more forgiving of minor imperfections since the doors cover most of the frame.
2. Use a small foam roller for flat areas and a quality 2-inch angled brush for edges, profiles, and inside corners.
3. Apply thin coats. Two thin coats look better and last longer than one thick coat.
4. Let each coat dry completely before recoating. Check the label — most cabinet-grade paints need 16 to 24 hours between coats.
5. Paint door fronts flat on sawhorses. Do the backs first, let them dry, flip, and do the fronts.

For spray applications:

1. Set up a spray area in a garage or covered outdoor space. Hang doors from wire or lay flat.
2. Use an HVLP sprayer set to the manufacturer's recommended PSI and tip size for your paint.
3. Keep the gun 8 to 12 inches from the surface. Move at a consistent speed.
4. Apply 2 to 3 light coats rather than one heavy coat.
5. Let dry between coats per product specs.

## Timeline Expectations

| Phase | Professional | DIY |
|---|---|---|
| Prep (clean, sand, prime) | 1–2 days | 2–3 weekends |
| Painting | 2–3 days | 2–3 weekends |
| Cure before reinstall | 3–7 days | 7–14 days |
| Reinstall and adjust | Half day | Half day |
| **Total** | **1–2 weeks** | **3–5 weeks** |

During the painting process, your kitchen is functional but unpleasant — no doors on any cabinets, tape and drop cloths everywhere, and paint smell (especially with alkyd products). Plan meals accordingly.

## Bathroom Cabinet Painting

Bathroom vanities are simpler because they are smaller — typically 2 to 6 doors and 2 to 4 drawers. But bathroom environments add a challenge: moisture.

For bathroom cabinets:
- Use a moisture-resistant primer
- Choose a semi-gloss or high-gloss finish (better moisture resistance than satin)
- Pay extra attention to areas around the sink where water splashes
- Consider [WaterSense-certified faucets](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) during the upgrade to reduce splash
- Ensure adequate ventilation after painting — bathroom humidity can interfere with paint cure

Professional bathroom vanity painting: $300 to $1,000 depending on size and complexity. DIY: $50 to $150 in materials.

## Making It Last

A well-executed cabinet paint job should last 8 to 15 years before it needs a refresh. The factors that determine longevity:

- **Prep quality:** Proper cleaning, sanding, and priming prevent peeling
- **Paint quality:** Cabinet-grade paints resist chipping and yellowing
- **Cure time:** Rushing to reinstall doors before the paint fully hardens causes sticking, marring, and fingerprints that become permanent
- **Daily use:** Soft-close hinges reduce door slamming, which chips paint at the frame contact points. If you are painting cabinets, spending $3 to $5 per hinge on soft-close upgrades is one of the best investments you can make

Touch-up is easier if you keep a small amount of the original paint. Store it in a tightly sealed small jar (the original gallon will skin over). A small artist's brush and matching paint handles chips before they become eyesores.
