Laminate wood flooring installation runs $3 to $8 per square foot for labor alone, or $6 to $14 per square foot when you bundle materials, underlayment, prep, and trim into a complete installed price. The wide range reflects real differences in project complexity — a straightforward rectangular living room over a flat plywood subfloor is a different job than a kitchen with angled walls, multiple doorways, and a concrete slab that needs moisture mitigation.

Here is how to build an accurate budget for your specific situation, understand what installers are actually charging for, and decide whether the DIY savings justify the learning curve.

## The Complete Pricing Picture

Most homeowners fixate on the per-square-foot material price and underestimate everything else. Here is what a complete laminate installation actually costs, broken into the components that show up on a professional quote:

### Materials ($2 to $6 per square foot)

This is the laminate itself. You are choosing between three quality tiers:

**Entry-level (6-8mm, AC3 wear layer):** $0.75 to $2.00 per square foot. Thin planks with a basic photographic layer and minimal texture. These products feel noticeably hollow underfoot, scratch more easily, and have locking mechanisms that are less forgiving during installation. Appropriate for closets, spare bedrooms, or properties where cost is the primary concern.

**Standard (8-10mm, AC4 wear layer):** $2.00 to $4.00 per square foot. The volume segment that represents the best balance of cost and performance for most homes. Solid feel, reasonably convincing wood appearance, and durability that handles regular household traffic for 15-20 years. This is what I install in my own home.

**Premium (10-14mm, AC5 wear layer):** $4.00 to $8.00 per square foot. Thick-core planks with registered embossing (texture that aligns with the printed grain pattern for a realistic look and feel), superior sound performance, and enhanced water resistance. At the top of this range, compare carefully with luxury vinyl plank and engineered hardwood — you may get better value from those alternatives.

### Underlayment ($0.15 to $1.00 per square foot)

Required unless your laminate has pre-attached padding. The choice depends on your subfloor:

**Over plywood:** Standard 2mm foam underlayment ($0.15-$0.30/sq ft) works. Upgrade to 3mm cork ($0.50-$1.00/sq ft) for upper floors in multi-story homes where sound transmission matters.

**Over concrete:** Moisture-barrier underlayment is mandatory. A 6-mil polyethylene sheet or combination foam-plus-barrier product ($0.30-$0.75/sq ft) prevents slab moisture from reaching the laminate core. Skipping this step on concrete is the fastest way to destroy a new laminate floor. The moisture wicks up through the slab, swells the laminate core at the edges, and causes visible peaking and buckling within months.

### Removal and Disposal ($0.75 to $3.00 per square foot)

The existing flooring has to come out before new laminate goes in (usually — there are exceptions). Costs by flooring type:

- Carpet: $0.75-$1.50/sq ft (includes pad and staple removal)
- Old laminate: $1.00-$2.00/sq ft
- Vinyl: $1.00-$2.50/sq ft (adhesive removal can push higher)
- Tile: $2.00-$4.00/sq ft (heavy, slow, often damages subfloor)

You can save this entire cost by doing the removal yourself. Old laminate and carpet removal are low-skill tasks that require more patience than expertise. Budget a half day for each 200 square feet.

### Subfloor Preparation ($0 to $3.00 per square foot)

A flat, sound, dry subfloor adds nothing to the cost. An uneven, damaged, or moist subfloor adds substantially. Per the [IRC building standards](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/), structural subfloor requirements must be met before any floor covering is installed.

Common prep tasks and costs:
- **Self-leveling compound** for low spots on concrete: $1.50-$3.00/sq ft for the affected area
- **Grinding high spots** on concrete: $1.00-$2.50/sq ft for the affected area
- **Securing loose subfloor panels** with screws: $0.25-$0.50/sq ft
- **Patching water-damaged subfloor sections:** $5-$10/sq ft for cut-and-replace repair
- **Moisture testing** on concrete slabs: $100-$300 (calcium chloride test or RH probe test)

If your installer quotes subfloor prep as a lump sum without inspecting the subfloor, push back. No one knows what the subfloor looks like until the old flooring comes up. A reputable installer either inspects the subfloor before quoting or provides a base price with a clear per-square-foot rate for prep work if needed.

### Installation Labor ($2 to $5 per square foot)

What you are paying the installer to do:
- Lay underlayment (if separate from the planks)
- Install planks row by row with proper staggering pattern
- Cut planks to fit at walls, doorways, and obstacles
- Undercut door jambs and casings
- Maintain consistent expansion gaps
- Install transition strips at doorways and material changes
- Address any minor subfloor imperfections discovered during installation

Experienced laminate installers can lay 200 to 400 square feet per day depending on room complexity. A skilled two-person crew handles most single-room installations in one day.

### Trim and Finishing ($1 to $4 per linear foot)

Baseboards or quarter-round molding cover the expansion gap between the laminate and the wall. Options:

**Remove and reinstall existing baseboards:** $1.00-$2.00/linear foot. Removes the baseboards before installation, installs the laminate underneath, and reinstalls the existing trim. This gives the cleanest look but risks breaking older or painted baseboards during removal.

**Install quarter-round over existing baseboards:** $0.75-$1.50/linear foot. Faster and cheaper, but adds a visible piece of trim at the bottom of the wall. Some people find this looks fine; others think it looks like a shortcut (because it is).

**Install new baseboards:** $2.00-$4.00/linear foot. The premium option for rooms where the existing trim is damaged, mismatched, or too short to cover the new floor height.

## Real Project Estimates

Here are three scenarios representing actual project scopes:

**Scenario 1: Master bedroom, 250 sq ft, plywood subfloor, carpet removal**
- Carpet removal: $275
- Subfloor prep (secure a few squeaky spots): $75
- Laminate material (mid-range, 10mm AC4): $750 (275 sq ft at $2.75, including 10% waste)
- Underlayment (foam): $55
- Installation labor: $750
- Quarter-round molding: $120
- One transition strip: $35
- **Total: $2,060 ($8.24/sq ft installed)**

**Scenario 2: Open living/dining area, 450 sq ft, concrete slab, vinyl removal**
- Vinyl removal and adhesive scraping: $900
- Subfloor prep (spot leveling, moisture testing): $350
- Laminate material (mid-range, 12mm AC4): $1,575 (500 sq ft at $3.15, including waste)
- Moisture-barrier underlayment: $225
- Installation labor: $1,575
- New baseboards: $640
- Three transition strips: $105
- **Total: $5,370 ($11.93/sq ft installed)**

**Scenario 3: Whole first floor, 800 sq ft, plywood subfloor, old laminate removal**
- Old laminate removal: $1,040
- Subfloor prep (moderate leveling, screw loose boards): $400
- Laminate material (premium, 12mm AC5): $3,520 (880 sq ft at $4.00, including waste)
- Pre-attached underlayment (no separate cost): $0
- Installation labor: $2,800
- Remove and reinstall baseboards: $480
- Five transition strips: $175
- **Total: $8,415 ($10.52/sq ft installed)**

## Factors That Raise the Price

Beyond the base installation, several common situations increase costs:

**Stairs.** Laminate on stairs is a different installation method — the planks are glued and fastened rather than floated. Expect $40 to $100 per step (tread and riser) on top of the regular per-square-foot pricing. Stair nosing trim adds $25 to $50 per step.

**Radiant floor heating.** Laminate works with radiant heat, but the underlayment must be compatible (no thick foam that insulates the heat away from the surface), and the surface temperature must stay below the manufacturer's limit (typically 80-85 degrees Fahrenheit). An experienced installer familiar with radiant heat requirements may charge a premium.

**Large open areas without breaks.** Laminate manufacturers specify maximum run lengths before an expansion joint is required — typically 30-40 feet. Very large open floor plans may need T-molding expansion joints mid-room, which affects both aesthetics and cost.

**Asbestos in existing flooring.** Pre-1985 homes with vinyl tile or sheet vinyl may have asbestos in the flooring material or adhesive. Professional testing costs $25-$50 per sample. If asbestos is confirmed, professional abatement costs $5-$15 per square foot, or you can encapsulate by installing over the existing flooring if it is in good condition and firmly adhered.

## Hiring an Installer: What to Verify

Laminate installation does not require a specialty license in most states, which means the quality of installers varies enormously. Before hiring:

**Verify experience with laminate specifically.** A tile installer or hardwood installer does not automatically know laminate. The floating floor system, expansion gap management, and click-lock assembly techniques are different skills.

**Ask about warranty.** A professional installer should warranty their labor for at least one year. This covers issues like planks separating, squeaking, or lifting due to installation errors — not product defects or homeowner damage.

**Confirm the scope in writing.** The quote should specify: removal of old flooring (yes/no and what type), subfloor prep (included or hourly/per-sqft), material supply (by you or by them), underlayment (type and cost), trim work (baseboards, quarter-round, transitions), and furniture moving.

**Check reviews that mention floor work.** General handyman reviews are not enough. Look for reviews specifically mentioning flooring installation. The [National Wood Flooring Association](https://www.nwfa.org/) and the [Tile Council of North America](https://www.tcnatile.com/) both offer installer certification programs, though laminate-specific certification is less formalized than hardwood or tile.

## When DIY Makes the Most Sense

Laminate is the most DIY-accessible flooring material on the market. The click-lock system is designed for non-professional installation, and the tools required are basic. DIY makes the strongest case when:

- You are doing a single room with a simple rectangular layout
- The subfloor is in good condition and does not need significant prep
- You are comfortable with a miter saw and measuring tools
- You have a full weekend available (plan conservatively)
- You want to save $1,000+ on labor for a 300-square-foot room

DIY is less compelling when:
- Multiple rooms need to flow continuously (getting transitions right between rooms requires experience)
- The subfloor needs leveling or repair work
- The space has many cuts — angles, pipes, island cabinets, irregular walls
- You are installing on concrete and need to manage moisture properly

For a [laminate flooring replacement project](/blog/laminate-flooring-replacement-cost-breakdown-real-numbers/) in a straightforward room, the DIY approach saves roughly 35-45% of the total installed cost. That savings comes entirely from labor — you are still paying the same material price.

If [moisture damage to existing laminate](/blog/moisture-damage-laminate-flooring-what-homeowners-should-know/) is the reason you are replacing the floor, invest more in understanding and addressing the moisture source this time. The cheapest laminate installation in the world is worthless if the same moisture problem destroys it again in two years.

Plan for 10% material waste, acclimate the product for 48-72 hours, and do not skip the underlayment. Those three rules — simple as they sound — prevent the majority of laminate installation failures, whether you hire a professional or do it yourself.
