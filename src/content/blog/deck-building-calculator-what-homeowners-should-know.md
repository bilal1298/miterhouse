Most online deck calculators estimate $50–$100 for hardware and fasteners on a 16×20 deck. The real number is $390. That single line item gap explains why calculators consistently underestimate actual project costs by 15–30% — and why homeowners who trust the calculator output end up making mid-project hardware store runs that blow their budget.

Here's what the calculators get right, what they miss, and how to do the math yourself so the number you plan around is the number you actually spend.

## What Calculators Miss

After testing a dozen popular deck calculators, these are the line items they consistently undercount or omit:

1. **Hardware and connectors.** Joist hangers, post bases, beam connectors, angle brackets, carriage bolts, and structural screws. Real cost: $350–$450 for a 16×20 deck.
2. **Blocking and bridging.** Required between joists at mid-span on longer runs and at the rim board.
3. **Gravel and landscape fabric** under the deck footprint to prevent weed growth.
4. **Concrete volume for footings.** Calculators assume minimum hole size; real holes are larger.
5. **Waste factor on framing lumber.** Joists cut from standard lengths generate unusable offcuts.
6. **Ledger flashing.** Copper or aluminum — only $30–$60, but critical for preventing water damage to the house.
7. **Permit fees.** $100–$500 depending on jurisdiction.
8. **Delivery charges.** A 16×20 deck requires 1,500–2,000 pounds of lumber that may not fit in a pickup.

A good calculator gives you 70–80% of the real cost. Budget the remaining 20–30% as contingency — or better yet, do the math yourself.

## Manual Calculation: 16×20 Deck

### Decking Boards

For a 16×20-foot deck using 5/4×6 boards in straight lay:
- (20 × 12) ÷ 5.5 = 43.6 → round to 44 boards at 16 feet long
- Plus 10% waste = 49 boards
- At $1.50/linear foot for pressure-treated: 49 × 16 × $1.50 = **$1,176**

Waste factor: 10% for straight-lay, 15% for diagonal, 20% for herringbone or picture-frame borders.

### Joists

16-inch on-center spacing for a 20-foot-wide deck:
- (20 × 12) ÷ 16 + 1 = 16 joists, each spanning 16 feet
- 2×10 PT lumber at $1.10/linear foot (minimum per code span tables for a 16-foot span at 16" OC)
- 16 × 16 × $1.10 = **$282**

### Beams

One freestanding beam (doubled 2×10) running the 20-foot width:
- 2 × 20 × $1.10 = **$44**

### Posts

Doubled 2×10 beam supports posts at roughly 8-foot spacing. A 20-foot beam needs 4 posts.
- 6×6 posts at $4/linear foot, 4-foot heights: 4 × 4 × $4 = **$64**

### Footings

12-inch diameter Sonotube at 42 inches deep uses roughly 2.5 bags of 80-lb concrete per pier:
- 4 × 2.5 × $5 = **$50**

The [American Wood Council](https://www.awc.org/) and [IRC](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) both specify which connections require metal hardware. Skipping it to save money is a code violation and a structural risk.

## Railing: The Budget Surprise

Railings are required on any deck surface more than 30 inches above grade — and they can double the cost of a basic deck. For a 16×20 deck with railing on three sides (52 linear feet):

| Material | Cost/Linear Foot | 52 LF Total |
|---|---|---|
| Wood (PT or cedar) | $15–$30 | $780–$1,560 |
| Composite | $25–$60 | $1,300–$3,120 |
| Aluminum | $40–$100 | $2,080–$5,200 |
| Cable | $60–$120 | $3,120–$6,240 |

Calculate railing separately and choose the material deliberately — don't let it be an afterthought that blows the budget.

## Using Your Math to Vet Contractor Quotes

Even if you're hiring out, running your own calculation has value. When three quotes come back and one is 30% lower, your numbers tell you whether the low bidder is cutting scope or just efficient.

Ask for itemized quotes. Compare their joist count, post count, and hardware quantities against your math. Significant discrepancies are worth questioning before you sign. Our [deck building contractor guide](/blog/finding-good-deck-building-contractors-near-you/) covers the full vetting process.

## Timing and Seasonal Pricing

Pressure-treated lumber peaks in spring (March–May) and drops in late fall and winter. Buying materials in November or December saves 10–20% on lumber. Store it under a tarp with air circulation.

Composite pricing is more stable year-round, but end-of-season clearance at big-box stores can hit 15–25% off discontinued colors.

Build your estimate, then track local prices for a few weeks before committing. The math stays the same — the dollars per foot shift with timing.
