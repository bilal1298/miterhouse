Removing tile flooring costs $2 to $6 per square foot for professional demolition and disposal, putting the average kitchen or bathroom tear-out between $400 and $2,400. The actual price depends on the tile type, what it is bonded to, the size of the area, and your local labor market. If you are willing to swing a hammer and invest a weekend, DIY tile removal can bring that cost down to $50 to $200 in tool rentals and disposal fees.

Before you commit to either path, you need to understand what you are dealing with under that tile — because that is where the real costs hide.

## What Drives Tile Removal Costs

### Tile Type and Bond Strength

Not all tile removes the same way. The material and installation method have a massive impact on difficulty and time.

**Ceramic and porcelain tile on thinset over concrete slab:** The most labor-intensive removal scenario. Thinset mortar bonds aggressively to concrete, and tile breaks into sharp fragments during removal. Professional crews use power chisels (SDS rotary hammers) and floor scrapers. Budget $3 to $6 per square foot for professional removal.

**Ceramic tile on thinset over cement board on a wood subfloor:** Easier because you can remove the cement board (backer board) along with the tile. Unscrew the cement board from the subfloor and carry out the whole assembly. Budget $2 to $4 per square foot.

**Tile set in a mortar bed (mud bed):** Found in older homes (pre-1980s). The mortar bed is a thick layer (3/4 to 1-1/4 inch) of sand-cement mortar under the tile. This is extremely heavy and labor-intensive to remove. Budget $4 to $7 per square foot, and expect significant dust and debris.

**Vinyl or peel-and-stick tile:** Not technically ceramic tile, but homeowners often lump these in. Removal is much easier — a floor scraper handles most of it. Budget $1 to $2 per square foot, or DIY for near-zero cost.

### Subfloor Condition After Removal

The surface under the tile determines whether the removal cost is the end or just the beginning.

**Concrete slab:** After removing tile and thinset from concrete, you are left with a rough, uneven surface. The remaining thinset needs to be ground down with a concrete grinder ($50 to $100 per day rental) or skim-coated with self-leveling compound ($1 to $3 per square foot) before new flooring can go down.

**Plywood subfloor:** If the tile was installed over cement board, removing the cement board often leaves the plywood in reusable condition. If thinset was applied directly to plywood (poor practice, but common), the plywood may need replacement — thin spots, moisture damage, and embedded thinset make it unsuitable for new flooring.

**Mud bed over plywood:** Removing the mortar bed often damages the plywood underneath. Budget for subfloor replacement ($2 to $4 per square foot for new 3/4-inch plywood installed).

### Hazardous Materials

In homes built before 1980, two hazards may be present:

**Asbestos in tile or adhesive.** Vinyl floor tiles and their black mastic adhesive from the mid-20th century commonly contain asbestos. If you suspect asbestos, stop and get a sample tested ($25 to $50). Professional asbestos abatement for tile removal costs $5 to $15 per square foot — dramatically more than standard removal.

**Lead paint on surrounding trim.** If removing tile disturbs painted baseboards or trim in a pre-1978 home, [EPA lead paint regulations](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) require certified renovation practices. The [TCNA (Tile Council of North America)](https://www.tcnatile.com/) provides additional guidance on proper tile installation and removal practices.

## Professional Removal: What to Expect

### The Quote

A professional tile removal quote should include:
- Demolition of the tile and mortar/thinset
- Removal of baseboards (if applicable)
- Hauling and disposal of debris
- Basic subfloor preparation (scraping remaining adhesive smooth)
- Cleanup

Items often quoted separately:
- Cement board removal (sometimes included, sometimes extra)
- Subfloor repair or replacement
- Self-leveling compound application
- Baseboard reinstallation
- New flooring installation

### Timeline

Professional crews work fast with the right tools:
- **Bathroom (50 to 80 sq ft):** 2 to 4 hours
- **Kitchen (100 to 200 sq ft):** 4 to 8 hours
- **Large area (300+ sq ft):** 1 to 2 days

These times assume power tools (SDS hammer drills, ride-on floor scrapers for large areas) and an experienced crew.

### Finding a Contractor

Tile removal is offered by:
- **Flooring companies** (often as part of a new flooring installation package)
- **Demolition contractors** (specialize in tear-outs)
- **General contractors and handymen**

The best value is usually bundling removal with new [flooring installation](/blog/flooring-tiles-installation-cost-breakdown-real-numbers/). Flooring companies roll the removal cost into the overall project and often discount it because the crew is already mobilized.

Get three quotes. Ask each contractor to specify whether the price includes subfloor prep, disposal, and any additional surface work needed for the new flooring you plan to install.

## DIY Tile Removal: A Realistic Guide

Tile removal is one of the more DIY-friendly demolition projects. The work is not complex — it is physical, dusty, and loud. If you can handle that, the savings are significant.

### Tools You Need

**Essential:**
- SDS rotary hammer with a chisel bit ($40 to $60 per day rental, or $150 to $250 to buy)
- 6-inch floor scraper with long handle ($25 to $40)
- Heavy-duty garbage bags or a rented dumpster
- Safety equipment: N95 respirator, safety glasses, hearing protection, heavy gloves, knee pads, steel-toe boots
- Pry bar for baseboards
- Shop vacuum

**Optional but helpful:**
- Demolition hammer (heavier than SDS — better for large areas, $75 to $100 per day rental)
- Concrete grinder for thinset residue ($50 to $100 per day rental)
- Oscillating multi-tool for detail areas (around door frames, cabinets)

### The Process

**1. Prep the room.** Remove all furniture, fixtures, toilet (for bathroom), and anything that would be in the way. Remove baseboards by prying carefully with a flat bar — save them for reinstallation if they are in good condition. Seal doorways with plastic sheeting to contain dust.

**2. Start breaking.** Position the chisel bit of your SDS hammer at a low angle (about 30 degrees) to the floor. Start at a doorway or edge where the tile meets another surface. Break the first few tiles to create a starting point and get underneath the tile layer.

**3. Work in sections.** Once you get under the tile, work in 2 to 3 foot wide swaths across the room. The SDS hammer does the heavy lifting — let the tool do the work rather than forcing it. Scoop broken tile into buckets or bags as you go.

**4. Address the subfloor.** After the tile is up, deal with the remaining thinset or mortar. On concrete, a floor scraper handles thin layers; a concrete grinder handles thicker deposits. On plywood, a floor scraper is usually sufficient.

**5. Clean up.** Tile removal generates an astonishing amount of debris. A 100-square-foot bathroom can fill eight to twelve contractor bags. Budget for disposal — either your regular trash pickup (spread over several weeks) or a dumpster rental ($200 to $400 for a small dumpster).

### DIY Cost Breakdown

For a 150 sq ft kitchen floor removal:
- SDS hammer rental (1 day): $50
- Floor scraper: $30
- Concrete grinder rental (if needed, 1 day): $75
- Safety gear: $40
- Garbage bags: $20
- Dumpster rental (optional): $250
- **Total DIY: $165 to $465**

Compare that to $450 to $900 for professional service on the same area.

### How Long Will It Take?

A motivated DIYer with a rented SDS hammer can remove about 30 to 50 square feet of tile per hour on a concrete slab, or 50 to 80 square feet per hour when removing cement board over plywood. Thinset grinding on concrete adds another hour for every 50 to 75 square feet.

For a 150 sq ft kitchen: plan on 4 to 6 hours for tile removal plus 2 to 3 hours for thinset cleanup. A full Saturday.

## Special Situations

### Heated Floor Tile Removal

If your tile has in-floor radiant heating (electric mats or hydronic tubing), removal becomes much more complicated. Electric mats are embedded in the thinset and will be destroyed during removal — budget for replacement. Hydronic tubes are more expensive to damage, so proceed carefully or hire a pro.

### Removing Tile from Shower Walls

Wall tile removal follows the same principles as floor tile, but with cement board on walls, the easiest approach is usually removing the entire cement board panel rather than chipping tile off individually. This requires access to the studs, which means the whole wall assembly comes out. Budget for new cement board and waterproofing as part of the rebuild.

### Tile Over Tile

If someone previously tiled over existing tile, you have two layers to deal with. The cost roughly doubles for the doubled labor and material volume. The upside: the bond between the two tile layers is sometimes weaker than the original bond to the substrate, which can make the upper layer faster to remove.

### Covering Without Removing

In some situations, you can install new flooring over existing tile without removing it:

- **Luxury vinyl plank (LVP):** Can go directly over tile if the tile is flat, firmly bonded, and the height change at transitions is acceptable
- **Laminate:** Same conditions as LVP
- **New tile:** Can be bonded over existing tile if the old tile is firmly adhered, flat, and the added height does not create problems at doorways and transitions

This avoids removal costs entirely but adds 3/8 to 3/4 inch of floor height. Check [door clearances](/blog/install-ceramic-flooring-pricing-guide-for-homeowners/) and transitions to adjacent rooms before committing to this approach.

For [slate tile](/blog/slate-tile-installation-costs-and-tips/) and other natural stone removal, expect the higher end of the cost range due to the weight and hardness of the material.

The [National Wood Flooring Association](https://www.nwfa.org/) provides standards for subfloor preparation that apply regardless of what previously covered the subfloor. If you are transitioning from tile to hardwood, their installation guidelines are worth reviewing.

## The Bottom Line

Tile removal is one of those projects where the sticker price is misleading. The removal itself costs $2 to $6 per square foot, but subfloor repair, surface preparation, and disposal can equal or exceed the demolition cost. Before committing, understand the full scope: what is under the tile, what condition the subfloor will be in after removal, and what preparation the new flooring requires. That complete picture — not just the per-square-foot demolition rate — is what your budget needs to cover.
