In a typical bathroom remodel, roughly half your budget disappears before any tile is set or a single fixture is hung — that half is labor. Understanding exactly where the rest goes is the fastest way to make smarter decisions, avoid the common overspend traps, and negotiate a contractor quote with confidence.

This post breaks down a mid-range bathroom remodel — the kind that costs $10,000–$18,000 and transforms a dated space without moving walls — into every major cost category with real percentages and what a contractor invoice actually looks like line by line.

---

## The Budget Split at a Glance

The table below reflects the typical percentage split for a full mid-range bathroom gut-and-replace. The dollar ranges assume a $12,000 total project budget, which is close to the national median for a primary bathroom remodel that keeps the existing footprint.

| Category | % of Budget | $ Range (on $12K) |
|---|---|---|
| Labor (contractor + subs) | 40–50% | $4,800 – $6,000 |
| Tile (material + install) | 10–15% | $1,200 – $1,800 |
| Vanity + countertop | 8–12% | $960 – $1,440 |
| Plumbing fixtures + labor | 8–12% | $960 – $1,440 |
| Shower/tub unit or surround | 8–10% | $960 – $1,200 |
| Lighting + electrical | 4–6% | $480 – $720 |
| Drywall, backer board, waterproofing | 4–6% | $480 – $720 |
| Flooring (if separate from tile) | 3–5% | $360 – $600 |
| Paint, trim, accessories | 2–4% | $240 – $480 |
| Permits + inspections | 1–3% | $120 – $360 |
| Contingency (always needed) | 10% | $1,200 |

The math often surprises homeowners: the pretty stuff — fixtures, tile, the vanity you fell in love with — accounts for only about 30–35% of the total. The rest is labor, prep, and the unglamorous substrate work that nobody photographs.

## What a Real Contractor Invoice Looks Like

Here's a representative line-by-line breakdown for a 55-square-foot primary bathroom remodel (toilet, vanity, tub/shower combo replaced; tile floor and shower walls; new lighting):

**Demo and disposal**
Removing the old tile, vanity, toilet, and tub surround, plus dumpster or haul-away fees: **$600–$900**

**Rough plumbing**
Shutoffs, supply line updates, drain repositioning if needed: **$400–$800**. If you're not moving anything, this stays at the low end.

**Backer board and waterproofing membrane**
This is where contractors catch homeowners off guard. A proper shower needs cement board or a waterproofing system like Schluter Kerdi, installed per the [Tile Council of North America (TCNA) Handbook](https://www.tcnatile.com/) specifications. Material plus install: **$350–$700**.

**Tile — shower walls**
A 3×6 subway tile in a 36"×36" shower stall: tile material runs $2–$5/sq ft, but installation (including the mortar, grout, and labor) runs $10–$20/sq ft. Total for shower walls: **$800–$1,400**.

**Tile — floor**
Porcelain floor tile at 55 sq ft, installed: **$600–$1,100** depending on pattern complexity. Herringbone costs more than straight lay; both cost more than vinyl plank. For a full breakdown by tile type, see our [bathroom tile installation cost](/blog/bathroom-tile-installation-cost/) guide.

**Vanity (supply only)**
A 36" vanity with a pre-mounted countertop from a supplier: **$400–$1,200**. The contractor installs it and typically charges a flat install fee of $150–$300 on top.

**Plumbing fixtures (faucet, showerhead, toilet)**
Mid-range set from a brand like Moen or Kohler: **$400–$800 supply only**. Budget fixture installs add another **$200–$400 in labor**.

**Lighting and exhaust fan**
Replacing a vanity light bar and adding a code-compliant exhaust fan: **$300–$600 installed**, including the electrician's time.

**Drywall and paint**
Patching, priming, and painting walls (not the shower area): **$300–$500**.

**Accessories and trim**
Towel bars, toilet paper holder, mirror, door hardware: **$150–$400 supply**. Contractors usually install these as part of punch-out.

**Contractor overhead and margin**
Most general contractors add 15–25% overhead and profit on top of their subcontractor costs and materials. This is legitimate — it covers their time managing the job, insurance, and warranty. On a $10,000 project in subs and materials, expect $1,500–$2,500 in GC margin.

**Total on this example: $10,800–$17,000** depending on fixture choices and local labor rates.

## Where People Overspend (and Usually Regret It)

**1. Tile choices that compound**

The tile itself is rarely the expensive part — it's everything that surrounds it. When a homeowner upgrades from $3/sq ft ceramic to $14/sq ft large-format porcelain, they also inherit higher installation costs (more cuts, heavier material, specific mortar requirements), and potentially extra structural reinforcement if the subfloor can't handle the weight. The tile budget balloons from $800 to $2,400 without the homeowner quite knowing how it happened.

The smarter move: choose a mid-range tile with a simple layout (straight or offset, not herringbone or diagonal) and put the savings into better grout and a quality waterproofing membrane.

**2. Moving plumbing**

Every plumber will tell you the same thing: if you don't move the drain, you save thousands. Repositioning a toilet or relocating a shower drain in a slab foundation can add $1,500–$4,000 to the rough plumbing bill — plus the concrete saw work. Keep the footprint.

**3. Scope creep after demo**

Demo day has a way of expanding. The contractor pulls the old tile and finds rotted backer board. They remove the vanity and discover the subfloor is soft. Each discovery is legitimate, but collectively they can add $1,000–$2,500 to a project that had a firm quote. The defense is a 10% contingency from day one and a clear conversation before demo about what happens when they find something unexpected.

**4. Premium fixtures in a renter-quality space**

Installing a $900 Kohler toilet in a bathroom with $1/sq ft floor tile creates a mismatch that won't impress buyers and doesn't improve daily life. Allocate fixture budget in proportion — don't put $400 in a faucet when you're tiling with the cheapest option at the big-box store.

## Where You Can Cut Without Sacrificing Quality

**Buy fixtures yourself.** Contractors typically mark up materials 15–30%. If you can order the vanity, faucet, and accessories directly from a supplier and have them arrive before the crew needs them, you'll save $200–$600 on a typical project. Coordinate timing carefully — a contractor who has to wait for your late delivery will charge you for it.

**Keep the layout.** This is the single highest-impact cost reduction available. Not moving the toilet, shower, or vanity saves thousands in rough plumbing and potentially structural work. The new layout you're imagining rarely adds as much functional value as it costs.

**Choose simpler tile patterns.** Straight or offset tile installation costs 20–30% less than diagonal or herringbone. The tile is the same price; the labor is what changes.

**Skip the full gut on a structurally sound bathroom.** If the existing tile is solid and the grout just needs work, a resurface or skim coat on the tub surround plus a new vanity and flooring can transform the look for $3,000–$5,000 instead of $12,000+. See our [DIY bathroom remodel cost guide](/blog/diy-bathroom-remodel-cost/) for where the DIY line makes sense.

**Use stock cabinet vanities instead of custom.** A stock 36" vanity from a home center with an aftermarket stone top looks virtually identical to a semi-custom piece at a third of the price. Custom cabinetry makes sense in a kitchen where you look at it every day in natural light; in a bathroom, few people notice.

## Getting an Accurate Quote

When a contractor gives you a quote, it should itemize at minimum: demo, rough plumbing, rough electrical, tile (material and labor separately), fixtures (material and labor separately), vanity, drywall/paint, and their overhead. Any quote that's just a single number without line items is a sign that surprises are coming.

Ask specifically:
- What triggers a change order, and what's the approval process?
- Are permits included, and who pulls them?
- What's your policy if demo reveals unexpected rot or damage?
- Do you mark up materials, and can I supply my own?

A contractor who won't answer those questions clearly before the job starts will be difficult to work with when something goes wrong mid-project.

The goal isn't to squeeze the contractor — good work at fair wages is how you get a bathroom that holds up. The goal is to know where your money is going so you can make intentional tradeoffs instead of discovering them on the invoice.
