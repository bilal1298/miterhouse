A mid-range bathroom remodel runs $10,000–$18,000, and roughly half of that budget is gone before any tile is set. That half is labor — and once you understand the full breakdown, you'll negotiate quotes better, make smarter material choices, and stop getting surprised by contractor invoices.

What follows is the line-by-line cost breakdown for a $12,000 primary bathroom gut-and-replace: the kind that swaps everything out without moving walls. These numbers reflect real contractor invoices, not ballpark estimates, and they match what you'll find when you [dig into the full 2026 cost picture](/blog/bathroom-remodel-cost-estimate/).

## The Budget Split at a Glance

| Category | % of Budget | $ Range (on $12K) |
|---|---|---|
| Labor (contractor + subs) | 40–50% | $4,800 – $6,000 |
| Tile (material + install) | 10–15% | $1,200 – $1,800 |
| Vanity + countertop | 8–12% | $960 – $1,440 |
| Plumbing fixtures + labor | 8–12% | $960 – $1,440 |
| Shower/tub unit or surround | 8–10% | $960 – $1,200 |
| Lighting + electrical | 4–6% | $480 – $720 |
| Drywall, backer board, waterproofing | 4–6% | $480 – $720 |
| Flooring (if separate from tile) | 3–5% | $360 – $600 |
| Paint, trim, accessories | 2–4% | $240 – $480 |
| Permits + inspections | 1–3% | $120 – $360 |
| Contingency (always needed) | 10% | $1,200 |

Here's the number that shocks most people: the pretty stuff — fixtures, tile, the vanity you've been saving in your photos app — accounts for only about 30–35% of the total. The rest is labor, prep, and substrate work that nobody photographs but that determines whether your bathroom lasts 5 years or 25.

## Line-by-Line: What a Real Invoice Looks Like

This breakdown covers a 55-square-foot primary bathroom (toilet, vanity, and tub/shower combo replaced; tile floor and shower walls; new lighting). No walls moved, no footprint changes.

**Demo and disposal: $600–$900**

Your contractor hauls out the old tile, vanity, toilet, and tub surround, plus rents or arranges a dumpster or haul-away service. Don't skip this line item when comparing quotes — some low-bid contractors bury disposal costs as a surprise on the back end.

**Rough plumbing: $400–$800**

Shutoffs, supply line updates, minor drain work. If you don't move anything — and you shouldn't, which we'll get to — this stays at the low end. The moment you say "can we just shift the toilet six inches?" this number jumps by $800–$2,000.

**Backer board and waterproofing membrane: $350–$700**

This is the line item that separates contractors who do good work from ones who don't. A proper shower surround needs cement board or a dedicated waterproofing system — Schluter Kerdi is the industry standard most professionals use. Without it, water intrudes behind the tile within a few years. The Tile Council of North America's installation specs exist for a reason; insist that your contractor follows them.

**Tile — shower walls: $800–$1,400**

A 3×6 subway tile in a standard 36"×36" shower stall costs $2–$5 per square foot for the material. Installation — mortar, grout, and labor — runs $10–$20 per square foot on top of that. So you're not buying an $80 box of tile. You're buying a $1,100 finished wall. The full picture of what drives that number is in our [bathroom tile installation cost breakdown](/blog/bathroom-tile-installation-cost/).

**Tile — floor: $600–$1,100**

Porcelain floor tile across 55 square feet, fully installed. The range is that wide because pattern matters. Straight or offset lay is on the low end. Herringbone or diagonal adds 20–30% in labor because every cut is different. The tile doesn't care about the pattern; your contractor's time does.

**Vanity (supply only): $400–$1,200**

A 36" vanity with a pre-mounted top from a supplier like Home Depot, Lowe's, or Build.com. Contractor install adds $150–$300 on top. If you're buying your own vanity — which you should — read our [bathroom vanity installation cost guide](/blog/bathroom-vanity-installation-cost/) before you order, because sink hole placement and plumbing rough-in location need to match.

**Plumbing fixtures — faucet, showerhead, toilet: $400–$800 supply only**

A mid-range set from Moen, Kohler, or Delta. Labor to install adds $200–$400. Don't cheap out on the toilet flapper mechanism; don't overspend on a showerhead that your water pressure can't support anyway.

**Lighting and exhaust fan: $300–$600 installed**

A vanity light bar replacement plus a code-compliant exhaust fan, including the electrician's time. Your exhaust fan needs to be sized for the room (CFM rating) and vented to the exterior — not into the attic, which is a moisture disaster waiting to happen and a code violation.

**Drywall and paint: $300–$500**

Patching everything that got disrupted, priming, and painting the non-shower walls. Not glamorous, but it's what makes the finished room look intentional.

**Accessories and trim: $150–$400**

Towel bars, toilet paper holder, mirror, door hardware. Contractors install these at punch-out (the final walk-through visit) and it's usually included in their overhead — but confirm that when you're reviewing the quote.

**Contractor overhead and margin: $1,500–$2,500 on a $10K project**

Most general contractors add 15–25% on top of their subcontractor costs and materials. This is not a scam — it covers job management, insurance, and the warranty on their work. What you don't want to pay is a GC who marks up materials 30% AND charges overhead on top of that. Ask directly: do you mark up materials, and by how much?

**Total on this example: $10,800–$17,000** depending on fixture choices and local labor rates.

## Where People Waste Money (and Usually Regret It)

**Tile upgrades that cascade**

When you upgrade from $3/sq ft ceramic to $14/sq ft large-format porcelain, you don't just pay more for the tile. Large-format tiles require specific mortar, more precise subfloor prep, and heavier lifting — installation costs go up $4–$8 per square foot. And if your subfloor deflects more than 1/360th of the span, you need additional structural reinforcement before the tile can go down. A $500 tile upgrade can quietly become a $1,800 total cost bump by the time the contractor is done.

The smarter move: pick a mid-range tile with a straight or offset layout, then put the savings into a better waterproofing membrane and quality grout. Schluter Ditra over Schluter Kerdi is a reasonable trade-up that actually matters for longevity.

**Moving plumbing**

Every experienced plumber will give you the same advice: don't move the drain. Repositioning a toilet or relocating a shower drain — especially on a concrete slab — adds $1,500–$4,000 to the rough plumbing bill plus concrete saw work. That "better flow" of the room you're imagining rarely delivers $3,000 worth of value. Keep the footprint, spend the money on materials.

**Scope creep after demo**

Demo reveals things. The contractor pulls old tile and finds rotted backer board. They remove the vanity and find a soft subfloor patch. These are legitimate discoveries, not contractor tricks — water damage hides well behind surfaces. The defense is a 10% contingency from day one and a clear conversation before demo starts about what the change-order process looks like and who approves what before work continues.

**Mismatched quality across the room**

A $900 Kohler Numi toilet sitting on $1/sq ft peel-and-stick floor tile is a waste of money on both ends. The mismatch won't impress buyers and doesn't make the room more useful. Allocate quality proportionally: mid-range everything, or upgrade selectively in things that wear (the toilet seat mechanism, the faucet cartridge), not in things that are just decorative.

## Where You Can Cut Without Sacrificing Quality

**Buy your own fixtures.** Contractors mark up materials 15–30%. Order the vanity, faucet, showerhead, and accessories directly from Build.com, Wayfair, or your local plumbing supply house and have them on-site before the crew needs them. The coordination takes effort, but the savings — $200–$600 on a typical project — are real. Coordinate timing carefully; making a contractor's crew wait costs you money.

**Don't move anything.** Keeping the toilet, shower, and vanity where they are is the single highest-impact cost reduction available. The new layout you're envisioning probably doesn't add $3,000 worth of functional value.

**Choose a straight tile pattern.** Same tile, 20–30% less labor. Herringbone looks great in magazine photos. It doesn't look $600 better in your actual bathroom.

**Use stock vanities, not custom.** A stock 36" vanity from the home center with an aftermarket quartz top looks nearly identical to semi-custom cabinetry at a third of the price. Custom cabinetry makes sense in a kitchen where you're staring at it in daylight every day. In a bathroom, almost nobody notices.

**Consider a partial remodel.** If your existing tile is structurally sound and you just hate how it looks, a surround resurface plus new vanity and flooring can transform the room for $3,000–$5,000 instead of $12,000. ## How to Read a Contractor Quote

Any quote that's a single number without line items is a quote that hides surprises. A legitimate bid itemizes at minimum: demo, rough plumbing, rough electrical, tile material and labor separately, fixture material and labor separately, vanity, drywall, paint, and the contractor's overhead rate.

Before you sign, ask four questions:

1. What triggers a change order, and who has to approve it before work continues?
2. Are permits included, and who pulls them? (Whoever pulls the permit is legally responsible for the work meeting code.)
3. What's your policy when demo reveals unexpected damage — do you stop work and show me before proceeding?
4. Do you mark up materials, and can I supply my own?

A contractor who won't answer those clearly is a contractor who will be difficult when something goes wrong at the 30-foot mark.

One more thing: get the permit. The permit exists so an inspector verifies that the electrical and plumbing were done correctly. Skipping it saves a few hundred dollars and creates a problem when you sell the house or file an insurance claim. It's not worth it.
