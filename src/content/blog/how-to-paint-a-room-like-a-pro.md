Bad prep is the only reason a paint job looks amateur. Not the roller brand, not the paint sheen — the four hours of wall work you decided to skip because you wanted to start painting.

Get that part right, and the brushwork almost doesn't matter. Here's the whole process, from empty room to pulled tape, with the products that actually work and the shortcuts that will cost you.

---

## Clear the Room and Protect What Stays

Move furniture to the center or out entirely. Canvas drop cloths on the floor — not the thin plastic ones that slide and bunch. A 9×12 canvas cloth from any hardware store runs about $15, won't slip under your feet, and lasts for years. Plastic sheeting on furniture is fine; canvas on floors.

Remove outlet covers, switch plates, and any light fixtures you can get down safely. Bag the hardware and tape it to the back of each cover. You'll thank yourself during cleanup.

Shut off the HVAC if you can. Moving air deposits dust on wet paint, and you'll see every particle once the sheen catches the light.

---

## Patch Everything — Don't Rationalize the Small Stuff

Every nail hole, every hairline crack at the ceiling, every ding you've been walking past for two years — they all need to go. Fresh paint doesn't hide surface flaws; it highlights them, especially in raking light from a window or lamp at the end of the day.

**Nail holes and small dings:** Use DAP DryDex Spackling. It goes on pink and dries white, so you know when it's actually ready — not just "looks dry." Press it in with a 3" putty knife, slightly overfill, and don't touch it again until it's fully white (30–60 minutes for pinhole-sized repairs).

**Cracks along the ceiling line or wall corners:** These flex with the house. Use a paintable flexible caulk — DAP Alex Plus is the standard. Run a thin bead, tool it smooth with a wet fingertip, and let it skin over for 30 minutes before painting over it. Rigid spackle in these spots will just crack again.

**Larger holes:** A mesh patch kit with setting-type joint compound (not the pre-mixed stuff that shrinks) handled in two thin coats. Let each coat dry completely. Rushing produces shrinkage cracks that read through your finish coat.

Once everything's dry, sand all patches with 120-grit to knock down the high spots, then 220-grit to blend the edges. Run your hand flat across each repair — you shouldn't feel the transition. Wipe the sanded areas with a barely damp cloth to clear the dust before you tape anything.

You should be spending more time here than anywhere else. This is the job.

---

## Wipe Down the Walls

Painting over dirty walls is an invisible mistake that shows up months later — peeling sections, blotchy sheen, areas where the paint just looks different. Grease, skin oils, and dust all compromise adhesion.

Wipe every wall with a damp sponge and a small amount of TSP substitute (around $8 at any hardware store). Pay extra attention to the area around light switches and doors — those spots get touched constantly. Let the walls dry completely before you tape.

---

## Tape the Trim — and Do It Properly

Use ScotchBlue Original for smooth trim surfaces. If your trim has gaps where it meets the wall, or if your walls have any texture, use ScotchBlue with Edge-Lock — the extra layer of adhesive along the edge actually seals instead of just masking.

Press the tape edge down firmly with a putty knife or your thumbnail. A tape edge you press on casually will let paint bleed underneath, and you'll spend 20 minutes touching up the trim after.

Tape baseboards, door trim, window trim, and the ceiling line if you're not painting the ceiling. Don't leave the tape on longer than necessary — 24 to 48 hours after painting in a warm room is the safe window. Left longer, the adhesive bonds and pulls your fresh paint off with it.

---

## Prime — This Is Not Optional

Primer is where most DIYers negotiate with themselves and lose. Here's when you genuinely can't skip it:

**Over patches and bare drywall:** Joint compound and spackle are porous. They'll absorb your finish coat unevenly and leave dull, flat spots called flashing. Use a PVA drywall primer — Zinsser's PVA or Kilz PVA. One coat, 30–60 minutes of dry time, done.

**Big color changes:** Going dark-to-light or light-to-dark? Have your paint store tint the primer 50% toward your finish color. It cuts your topcoats from three down to two and saves you a full gallon of expensive paint.

**Stains — water, smoke, marker, anything:** Regular latex primer will not block a water stain. It will bleed through every single coat you put over it. Use Zinsser BIN (shellac-based, fast-drying, and excellent) or Kilz Original. Open windows — BIN off-gases aggressively.

Apply primer with the same brush-and-roller method you're about to use for paint. One coat is enough. Let it dry fully before you touch the walls again.

---

## Pick the Right Paint for the Room

This decision matters more than most people think, and it's easy to get right.

**Bedrooms and living rooms:** Eggshell. It has just enough sheen to wipe down and not enough to highlight wall imperfections. Benjamin Moore Regal Select Eggshell is the benchmark — it covers beautifully in two coats and holds its color for years. Sherwin-Williams Emerald in eggshell is equally good. Both run $55–$65/gallon, and both are worth it over the $28 store brands that need three coats and look flat in a year.

**Kitchens, bathrooms, hallways, kids' rooms:** Satin. More durable, cleans up with a damp cloth, handles moisture without growing mildew. For bathrooms specifically, look for a product with mildew-resistant additives — Behr Premium Plus Ultra Bath has this built in and is a solid value at around $38/gallon.

**Ceilings:** Flat white ceiling paint, full stop. Don't use wall paint on the ceiling. The finish reads differently when viewed at an overhead angle and shows every roller stroke.

**Trim:** Semi-gloss or gloss. Trim takes abuse and needs to be wipeable. Sherwin-Williams Emerald Urethane Trim Enamel is expensive at around $75/gallon but it cures to a genuinely hard finish that won't scratch like standard latex.

One gallon covers 350–400 square feet. A 12×12 room with 8-foot ceilings has roughly 350 square feet of wall area after subtracting doors and windows — so two gallons gets you two solid coats. ## Cut In Before You Roll

Cutting in means painting the edges — ceiling line, corners, baseboards, and around trim — with a brush before you bring the roller anywhere near the wall. Always cut in first. Rolling first and then trying to cut in creates visible overlap lines that you can't fix.

The only brush worth using for cut-in work is a good angled sash brush. The Purdy XL Elite 2.5" is the one to buy — it holds enough paint in the ferrule to give you real control, and the bristles don't shed. The Wooster Shortcut is another excellent option if you prefer a bent handle. A $4 chip brush will leave bristles in your paint and frustrate you for two hours.

**Load it right:** Dip about an inch of bristle into the paint and tap (don't wipe) lightly against the inside of the can. Wiping removes too much paint and you'll run dry halfway through a stroke.

**Technique:** Start an inch from the edge and work back toward it in one smooth stroke. Keep a wet edge — don't let the cut-in line dry before you roll next to it or you'll see a visible seam. Work one wall completely before moving to the next.

Cut in 2–3 inches wide so the roller can get close without crowding into the taped area and leaving ridges.

---

## Roll the Open Walls

**Nap thickness:** 3/8" nap for smooth to lightly textured walls. 1/2" nap for orange-peel or knockdown texture. Wooster Pro/Doo-Z 1/2" covers are the everyday standard — they hold paint well, leave minimal texture of their own, and rinse clean. Purdy White Dove covers are equally good and slightly lower-lint.

**Load the roller:** Fill the tray about a third full. Roll the cover through the paint, then work it up and down on the ridged part of the tray until it's evenly saturated but not dripping off. An overloaded roller splatters. A dry roller leaves streaks.

**The W method:** Roll a loose W or M shape on the wall about a foot from the corner, without pressing hard. Fill in the shape without lifting the roller, then finish with light full-length vertical strokes from top to bottom — this is called "laying off" and it evens out the stipple. Work in 3-foot sections and keep a wet edge between them so everything blends.

Don't go back over an area that's started to tack up. You'll drag and streak. Keep moving forward.

---

## Second Coat — Wait for Real

Let the first coat dry to the touch before going back in. That's 2–4 hours with latex paint at 65–75°F and normal humidity. In a cold or humid room, wait longer — 4–6 hours minimum. If you're not sure, wait.

A second coat applied too early traps moisture. The surface will look fine at first, then bubble, dent easily, and never cure properly. Don't do it.

The second coat goes on exactly the same way: cut in first, then roll. You'll be faster this time, and the coverage will look noticeably more even by the end.

**Full cure time:** Paint feels dry within hours but doesn't fully harden for 2–4 weeks. During that window, don't scrub the walls or push furniture up against them.

---

## Pull Tape and Clean Up

Remove painter's tape while the final coat is slightly tacky — 1–2 hours after you finish rolling. Pull slowly at a 45-degree angle back over the taped surface. If the paint film cracks along the tape edge, score it first with a sharp razor.

**Brush care:** Rinse latex paint out immediately with warm water, working the bristles against your palm until the rinse runs completely clear. Reshape the brush and hang it bristle-down to dry. A Purdy or Wooster brush cleaned right after every use lasts five or more years. Left to dry in paint, it's a doorstop.

**Roller cleanup:** Roll the excess back into the tray, then rinse thoroughly. A roller spinner (about $10) pulls the water out in seconds. Let covers dry completely before storing.

---

## The Mistakes That Wreck an Otherwise Good Job

**Skipping patching on the theory it'll blend in:** It won't. Light from windows and lamps catches every surface irregularity under fresh paint. Fix it first.

**Not stirring the paint:** Pigment settles to the bottom of the can. Have the store shake it when you buy it, then stir it yourself before each session. Unstirred paint gives you uneven sheen and color inconsistencies.

**Overloading the roller:** Excess paint drips from the bottom of the roller on your first strokes. Work the loaded cover against the tray ridges until dripping stops.

**Painting under bad light:** Your overhead ceiling fixture flattens everything. Bring in a work light or a floor lamp and position it to rake across the wall at a low angle. It shows you every lap mark and missed spot in real time.

**Rolling too fast:** You'll get spattering and an uneven texture. Steady, medium pressure with deliberate strokes is faster in the end because you're not fixing spatters.
