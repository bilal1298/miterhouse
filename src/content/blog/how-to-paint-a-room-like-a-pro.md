The fastest way to a professional paint job is not a better brush — it's better prep. Spend 60% of your time getting the room ready, and the actual painting takes care of itself. Here's how to do it right, start to finish.

## What You'll Need (and What It Costs)

Before you touch a wall, get everything staged. Stopping mid-job to run to the hardware store is how you end up with lap marks and dried edges.

## Step 1: Clear and Protect the Room

Move furniture to the center of the room or out entirely. Cover it with plastic sheeting — canvas drop cloths protect floors better and don't slide around the way plastic does. Remove outlet covers, switch plates, and any light fixtures you can safely take down. Bag the screws and tape them to the back of the covers so they don't disappear.

Turn off the HVAC if you can. Circulating air picks up dust and deposits it on wet paint.

---

## Step 2: Patch Every Hole and Imperfection

This is where most DIYers shortchange themselves. Every nail hole, ding, and hairline crack that you skip will be visible under fresh paint — especially in raking light from a window or lamp.

**For nail holes and small dings:** Use DAP DryDex Spackling. It goes on pink and dries white, so you know when it's ready. Press it in with a putty knife, slightly overfill, and let it dry completely (usually 30–60 minutes for small holes).

**For cracks at corners or where walls meet ceilings:** Use a flexible paintable caulk (DAP Alex Plus works). Run a bead, tool it smooth with a wet finger, let it skin over for 30 minutes before painting.

**For larger holes (golf ball-sized or bigger):** Use a mesh patch kit with joint compound. Apply compound in two thin coats, letting each dry fully before the next. Rushing this creates shrinkage cracks.

Once everything is dry, sand all patches smooth with 120-grit, then finish with 220-grit. Run your hand flat across each patch — you should barely feel the transition. Wipe down sanded areas with a slightly damp cloth to pull up the dust.

---

## Step 3: Clean the Walls

Painting over dirty walls is a common and invisible mistake. Grease, dust, and skin oils prevent paint adhesion, and you won't see it fail until months later when sections start peeling or you notice the sheen looks different.

Wipe walls down with a damp cloth or a sponge with a little TSP substitute (trisodium phosphate alternative, available at any hardware store for around $8). If you're working in a home built before 1978, the [EPA's Renovation, Repair, and Painting (RRP) Rule](https://www.epa.gov/lead/renovation-repair-and-painting-program-contractors) requires lead-safe practices when disturbing painted surfaces — even for homeowners doing their own work, testing first is strongly recommended. Pay extra attention to areas near light switches, door frames, and any walls near the kitchen. Let the walls dry completely before taping.

---

## Step 4: Tape and Protect Trim

Use ScotchBlue Original (blue tape) for general surfaces, or ScotchBlue with Edge-Lock if you're working next to textured walls or have trim with gaps. Press the tape down firmly along the edge with a putty knife or your thumbnail — a loose tape edge is how paint bleeds under.

Tape off:
- Baseboards and door/window trim
- The ceiling line (if you're not painting the ceiling the same color)
- Any hardware or outlets you couldn't remove

Don't leave tape on for more than the manufacturer's recommended time — typically 14 days max, but in a warm room, 24–48 hours is safer. Tape left too long can pull paint off with it when removed.

---

## Step 5: Prime (Don't Skip This)

A lot of people treat primer as optional. It isn't, in these situations:

- **Raw or patched drywall:** Unprimed joint compound is porous and will absorb paint unevenly, creating dull spots called "flashing." Use a PVA drywall primer (Zinsser's PVA or Kilz PVA are both good).
- **Big color changes:** Going from dark to light or light to dark? A tinted primer (ask the paint store to tint it 50% toward your finish color) cuts down the number of topcoats you'll need.
- **Stains:** Water stains, smoke, marker — use a stain-blocking primer like Zinsser BIN (shellac-based) or Kilz Original. Regular latex primer will not block stains; they'll bleed through.

Apply primer with the same brush-and-roller technique you'll use for paint (described below). One coat is usually enough. Let it dry fully — PVA primer dries in 30–60 minutes; shellac-based primers take 45 minutes but off-gas more aggressively, so ventilate well.

---

## Step 6: Choose the Right Paint

**For bedrooms and living rooms:** Eggshell finish. It hides minor wall imperfections, has a barely-there sheen, and is easy to touch up. Benjamin Moore Regal Select and Sherwin-Williams Emerald are both excellent. They're pricier (~$50–$65/gallon) but cover in fewer coats and hold their color.

**For kitchens, bathrooms, hallways, and kids' rooms:** Satin finish. More durable, cleans up better, resists moisture. For bathrooms specifically, look for a product labeled "bath and spa" or with mildew-resistant additives. If indoor air quality is a concern, the [EPA's guide to VOCs](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality) explains what to look for on paint labels — low-VOC and zero-VOC formulas are widely available at comparable prices.

**For ceilings:** Flat white ceiling paint. It hides roller texture and doesn't reflect light at unflattering angles. Don't use wall paint on ceilings — the finish reads differently overhead.

**For trim:** Semi-gloss or gloss. Trim takes abuse and needs to wipe clean. Sherwin-Williams Emerald Urethane Trim Enamel is worth the premium for a hard, durable finish.

One gallon covers roughly 350–400 square feet. A 12×12 room with 8-foot ceilings has about 350 sq ft of wall area (minus doors and windows), so two gallons for two coats is the right call.

---

## Step 7: Cut In First

"Cutting in" means painting the edges — along the ceiling, corners, baseboards, and trim — with a brush before rolling the open wall area. Do this before rolling, not after.

Use a quality 2.5" angled sash brush. The Purdy XL Elite is worth the $20. Cheap brushes shed bristles and don't hold enough paint in the ferrule to give you control.

**Technique:**
1. Load the brush by dipping about 1" of the bristles into the paint and tapping (not wiping) lightly against the inside of the can.
2. Start about an inch away from the edge, then work back toward it in a smooth, controlled stroke.
3. Keep a wet edge — don't let your cut-in line dry before you roll next to it. Work one wall at a time.
4. A steady hand beats painter's tape for the ceiling line once you've done it a few times. But tape the trim until you're comfortable.

Cut in about 2–3 inches wide — wide enough that the roller won't get into the taped area and cause ridges.

---

## Step 8: Roll the Walls

**Roller nap:** For smooth to slightly textured walls, use a 3/8" nap cover. For heavier textures (orange peel, knockdown), use 1/2" nap. The ½" Wooster Pro/Doo-Z covers or Purdy White Dove are reliable and don't leave too much texture of their own.

**Load the roller correctly:** Fill your tray about a third full. Roll the cover through the paint, then work it back and forth on the ridged upper part of the tray until it's evenly loaded but not dripping.

**Rolling technique — the W method:**
1. Start about a foot from a corner and roll a large, loose "W" or "M" shape on the wall — don't press hard.
2. Fill in the W without lifting the roller, using overlapping strokes.
3. Finish with light, full-length vertical strokes from top to bottom (called "laying off") to even out the stipple pattern.
4. Work in 3-foot-wide sections and keep a wet edge so sections blend.

Don't roll over an area that's started to dry — you'll lift and streak the paint. Keep moving.

---

## Step 9: Second Coat and Drying Times

Let the first coat dry to the touch before the second coat. Latex paint typically needs 2–4 hours between coats in normal conditions (65–75°F, 50% humidity). In a cold basement or humid room, that stretches to 4–6 hours.

Don't rush this. A second coat applied too early traps moisture, causes bubbling, and creates a surface that dents easily for weeks.

The second coat goes on the same way as the first — cut in, then roll. By the second coat, you'll be faster and the coverage will be better.

**Full cure time:** Paint is dry to the touch in hours, but it doesn't fully cure (harden) for 2–4 weeks. During that window, avoid scrubbing the walls or pressing furniture against them.

---

## Step 10: Pull the Tape and Clean Up

Remove painter's tape while the paint is still slightly tacky — not wet, not fully dry. The sweet spot is usually 1–2 hours after the final coat. Pull at a 45-degree angle back over itself, slowly. If the paint cracks or tears, score the tape edge lightly with a razor before pulling.

**Brush cleanup:** Rinse latex paint out immediately with warm water. Work the bristles against your palm until the water runs clear, then reshape and hang to dry (don't let brushes sit on their bristles). A brush cleaned properly after every job lasts years.

**Roller cleanup:** Roll excess paint back into the tray, then rinse with warm water. Use a roller spinner tool (around $10) if you have one — it removes water in seconds instead of minutes.

Wash out paint trays and let them dry. Remove drop cloths and shake them outside before storing.

---

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

**Skipping prep:** The single biggest mistake. Paint reveals every flaw it finds. Fix it before it's sealed under two coats.

**Not stirring paint:** Pigment settles. Stir thoroughly — at the store, ask them to shake it, then stir it yourself before each use.

**Overloading the roller:** Drips happen when the roller is too saturated. Work the loaded roller on the tray ridges until it stops dripping.

**Painting in bad light:** Set up a work light or bring in a lamp. Overhead lighting misses laps and missed spots. Side-light reveals everything.

**Rolling too fast:** Speed creates spattering and uneven texture. Steady, medium pressure with deliberate strokes.

## Wrapping Up

A properly painted room is one of the highest-ROI home improvement projects you can do — it transforms a space completely for a few hundred dollars and a weekend of work. The difference between a paint job that looks good for a decade and one that looks tired in a year almost always comes down to the prep steps most people skip.

If you're in the middle of a larger renovation, this kind of finish work tends to come at the end of a longer timeline.
