Hiring a contractor for drywall repair costs $50 to $85 per hour for labor, or $150 to $800 per repair depending on size and complexity. Most contractors set a minimum service charge of $150 to $250 regardless of how small the job is — that trip charge alone can exceed the cost of materials for small patches by five to ten times. Understanding what contractors actually do, how they price the work, and when it makes sense to call one versus handling it yourself saves both money and frustration.

This guide covers the contractor side of drywall repair: finding the right person, understanding the quote, and knowing what quality work looks like when it is finished.

## When You Genuinely Need a Contractor

Not every drywall problem needs a pro. But some situations genuinely do:

**Ceiling repairs.** Working overhead with heavy sheets of drywall is physically demanding and technically unforgiving. A seam that is slightly off on a wall is hard to see. The same seam on a ceiling, lit by recessed lights, casts a shadow you will notice every day. Pros have the equipment (drywall lifts, stilts, scaffolding) and the muscle memory to get ceiling work right.

**Water damage with potential mold.** When drywall has been wet — from a roof leak, burst pipe, or flooding — what you see on the surface is rarely the full story. Mold can grow inside the drywall core and on the framing behind it within 24 to 48 hours of sustained moisture. A contractor knows how far to cut back, how to assess framing condition, and when to bring in a mold remediation specialist. Homes built before 1978 also need a [lead paint assessment](https://www.epa.gov/lead) before any demolition work.

**Matching existing textures.** If your walls have orange peel, knockdown, skip trowel, or any other applied texture, replicating it on a repair patch is one of the hardest skills in drywall finishing. A good contractor has the spray equipment and experience to make patches blend seamlessly with surrounding surfaces. A first-time DIYer attempting texture matching on a visible wall will almost certainly be disappointed.

**Large-scale damage.** Anything involving more than one or two sheets of drywall — a room damaged by fire, extensive termite damage, or a remodel that exposed walls — is contractor territory by volume alone.

**Structural cracks that keep coming back.** If you have patched the same crack twice and it keeps reappearing, the problem is not the drywall. Something is moving — foundation settling, truss uplift, inadequate framing. A contractor can diagnose the source and address it structurally before patching.

## Types of Drywall Contractors

The person you call depends on the scope:

### General Handyman

**Best for:** Small repairs — nail pops, doorknob holes, minor cracks. A few patches at a time.

**Typical rate:** $50 to $75 per hour, $150 to $300 minimum.

**Pros:** Available for small jobs that dedicated drywall contractors may decline. Often handles the painting too, saving a second trade call.

**Cons:** Skill level varies widely. Some handymen produce invisible repairs. Others leave visible ridges and mismatched texture. Ask for photos of finished drywall work before hiring.

### Drywall Finishing Contractor

**Best for:** Large patches, full sheet replacement, ceiling work, texture matching, and new drywall installations.

**Typical rate:** $60 to $85 per hour, or per-patch pricing for repairs.

**Pros:** This is all they do, and the quality difference shows. A dedicated finisher has the specific tools (automatic taping tools, hopper guns, sanding vacuums) and the refined technique that comes from doing thousands of repairs.

**Cons:** May not be interested in one small patch. Minimum charges are higher.

### Painting Contractor with Drywall Capabilities

**Best for:** Repairs followed by painting. If you are repainting a room and it needs drywall work first, a painting contractor who handles both eliminates the coordination of two separate trades.

**Typical rate:** Bundled into the painting quote, often at a discount compared to standalone drywall work.

**Pros:** Single point of contact, seamless transition from repair to finish. **Cons:** Not all painting contractors are skilled at drywall beyond basic patching.

## Understanding the Quote

A drywall repair quote should include:

**Scope description.** Each repair listed individually with approximate size and location. "Fix three holes in the living room" is not detailed enough. "Patch one 4-inch hole behind the couch (doorknob damage), re-tape two 6-inch ceiling cracks near the east window, re-fasten and skim-coat a 2x3-foot sagging section above the kitchen entry" tells you the contractor actually assessed each item.

**Material specification.** The type of compound (lightweight all-purpose for most repairs, setting-type for areas needing rigidity), tape type (paper or mesh), and any specialty products (mold-resistant compound for bathroom repairs, fire-rated compound for garage ceilings).

**Finish level.** The drywall industry defines five levels of finish (Level 0 through Level 5). Most repair work should be Level 4 (all joints taped, compound applied, sanded smooth, and primed). Level 5 adds a skim coat over the entire surface and is specified for high-gloss paint or critical lighting conditions. Ask which level the quote includes.

**Texture matching.** If applicable, the quote should state whether texture application is included and what method will be used.

**Priming.** A repair is not complete until the new compound is primed. Unprimed compound absorbs paint differently than the surrounding wall, creating visible patches called "flashing" — matte spots on an otherwise uniform surface. Priming should be listed in the quote.

**Timeline and coats.** A proper repair takes three coats of compound with drying time between each. Beware of quotes that promise same-day completion for anything larger than a small patch — they are either using setting-type compound (legitimate but requires skill) or cutting corners on coat count.

## Evaluating Contractor Quality

### Before They Start

Ask to see photos of previous drywall repair work — specifically repairs, not new construction. New drywall is easier to finish than patches. A contractor who has repair photos on their phone is proud of their work and does enough of it to stay skilled.

Check reviews specifically mentioning drywall quality. Generic "they were on time" reviews tell you nothing about whether you will see the patch after painting.

### While They Work

Good drywall contractors share a few habits:

- They apply thin coats and widen each successive coat beyond the previous one, feathering gradually into the surrounding surface
- They sand between coats with fine-grit paper (150+), not coarse
- They check their work with a raking light (a flashlight held at a steep angle against the wall) to spot imperfections invisible under normal lighting
- They protect your floors and furniture — drywall dust gets everywhere and is abrasive enough to scratch hardwood floors

### After Completion

The test of a good drywall repair is invisibility. After priming (before paint), examine the repair area in both direct and raking light. You should not be able to feel the transition from compound to existing drywall with your fingertips. There should be no ridges, humps, or depressions.

If you can see the repair outline after priming, speak up before painting. It is far easier (and cheaper) to add a skim coat and re-prime than to repaint after discovering the patch shows through a finished coat.

## Safety Considerations

Drywall repair generates fine dust that is irritating to lungs. While gypsum dust is not classified as toxic, prolonged exposure without protection is unwise. The [EPA's indoor air quality guidelines](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality) recommend ventilation and dust containment during any sanding work. Additionally, [OSHA standards](https://www.osha.gov/) require respiratory protection for workers exposed to nuisance dust in enclosed spaces.

A professional contractor should:
- Use a dustless sanding system (vacuum-attached sander) or contain the work area with plastic sheeting
- Wear a dust mask or respirator
- Clean up thoroughly using a HEPA-rated vacuum, not a broom (which just redistributes fine dust into the air)

If you have household members with respiratory conditions, confirm the contractor's dust-management approach before work begins.

## Pricing Pitfalls to Watch For

**"We'll see when we open it up."** Some uncertainty is reasonable — you cannot fully assess water damage until you cut out the affected drywall. But a reputable contractor provides a range (not a blank check) and communicates before exceeding it.

**Low-ball quotes with upcharges.** A quote of $100 for a "simple repair" that somehow becomes $450 when they discover it "needs extra work." Get a detailed scope upfront and a commitment to communicate before adding charges.

**Per-coat charges.** Some contractors quote a per-coat price knowing three coats are needed but only quoting one. The total is always three coats of compound plus primer. If the quote only mentions one coat, ask what the all-in price is for a paint-ready finish.

**No warranty.** Drywall repairs should hold up for the life of the wall. A contractor who will not guarantee their work for at least a year is either cutting corners on materials or technique.

The best approach for most homeowners: batch your drywall repairs. Accumulate small damage over 6 to 12 months, then have a contractor address everything in one visit. The trip charge and setup time get amortized across multiple repairs, bringing the per-patch cost down significantly. Combine the visit with painting if possible — one trade call, one mess, one cleanup, and walls that look brand new when they leave.
