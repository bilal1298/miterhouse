Installing a standing seam metal roof costs between $10 and $18 per square foot for materials and professional labor, putting a typical 2,000-square-foot roof project at $20,000 to $36,000. That is roughly two to three times the cost of asphalt shingles, but the payoff is a roof that lasts 40 to 70 years, sheds snow better than any other residential roofing material, and requires almost no maintenance.

This guide covers the installation process, what drives costs up or down, and a frank assessment of whether this is a viable DIY project (for most homeowners, it is not).

## What Makes Standing Seam Different

Standing seam is a specific metal roofing profile where long panels run vertically from ridge to eave, connected by raised seams that stand 1 to 2 inches above the panel surface. The seams are either mechanically locked (folded together with a seaming tool) or snap-locked (the panels clip together).

The critical feature: **no exposed fasteners.** Every screw and clip is hidden beneath the seam, which means there are no penetrations through the panel surface where water could eventually enter. This is the primary advantage over other metal roofing types (like corrugated or R-panel) that use exposed screws through the panel face.

The hidden fastener design also allows the panels to expand and contract with temperature changes without stressing the fasteners. Metal roofing expands significantly — a 20-foot panel can grow 1/4 inch between winter and summer. Standing seam accommodates this through clips that allow longitudinal movement.

## Material Options and Costs

### Galvalume Steel (Most Common)
- **Cost:** $4 to $7 per square foot for material
- **Gauge:** 24-gauge is standard residential, 26-gauge is a budget option
- **Finish:** Factory-applied Kynar/PVDF paint finish lasts 30 to 40 years
- **Lifespan:** 40 to 60 years
- **Best for:** Most residential applications. Best balance of cost and performance.

### Aluminum
- **Cost:** $6 to $10 per square foot for material
- **Pros:** Will not rust even in coastal/salt air environments, lighter weight
- **Cons:** Softer than steel (dents more easily), more expensive
- **Lifespan:** 40 to 60 years
- **Best for:** Coastal homes where salt corrosion is a concern.

### Copper
- **Cost:** $15 to $30 per square foot for material
- **Finish:** Develops green patina over time
- **Lifespan:** 80 to 100+ years
- **Best for:** Historic properties, high-end homes where aesthetic and longevity justify the premium.

### Zinc
- **Cost:** $12 to $20 per square foot for material
- **Finish:** Self-healing — scratches develop a protective patina naturally
- **Lifespan:** 80 to 100+ years
- **Best for:** Architecturally distinctive homes in moderate climates.

## The Installation Process

### Step 1: Tear-Off or Overlay Decision

Most building codes allow one layer of roofing over existing material. However, standing seam performs best over a clean deck because it requires a flat substrate. Uneven surfaces beneath the panels can telegraph through and create waviness that is visible — and unsightly — on a smooth metal surface.

The standard recommendation: tear off existing roofing down to the deck. This adds $1 to $2 per square foot but ensures a clean, inspectable substrate.

### Step 2: Deck Inspection and Repair

With the old roofing removed, the deck (typically plywood or OSB sheathing) is inspected. Any soft, rotted, or damaged sections are replaced. This is the only opportunity to address deck issues for the next 40 to 70 years, so thorough inspection is critical.

### Step 3: Underlayment

A high-temperature underlayment is applied over the entire deck. Standard asphalt felt is not recommended under metal roofing — the metal gets hot enough to degrade standard felt. Synthetic underlayment rated for metal roofing is the standard choice. In cold climates, ice and water shield membrane is applied at eaves, valleys, and around penetrations.

### Step 4: Eave Trim and Starter

Metal drip edge and eave trim are installed along the bottom edge. This creates a clean termination point and directs water into the gutter. The trim must be level and straight — any waves here will telegraph through the panel edges.

### Step 5: Panel Layout and Cutting

Panels are measured and cut to length. For residential work, panels are typically ordered pre-cut to the exact rafter length or formed on-site using a portable roll-forming machine. On-site forming produces seamless panels in any length, eliminating horizontal joints.

Panel width is typically 12 to 18 inches. The layout is planned so that panels are symmetrical on the roof face — a narrow sliver at one rake and a full panel at the other looks unfinished.

### Step 6: Clip Installation

Clips are screwed to the roof deck at specified intervals (typically 24 inches on center for standard wind loads, 12 inches in high-wind zones). The clips are the connection between the panel and the structure — they must be positioned precisely and fastened securely.

Two types of clips are used: **fixed clips** at the ridge or eave (anchoring the panel at one point) and **floating clips** along the rest of the panel length (allowing the panel to slide as it expands and contracts). Getting this right is essential for long-term performance.

### Step 7: Panel Installation

Starting from one rake (side edge), panels are set into the clips and snapped or mechanically seamed to the adjacent panel. Each panel must be plumb (perfectly vertical) — even a small deviation compounds across the roof width and results in panels that do not line up at the opposite rake.

### Step 8: Seaming

For mechanically seamed systems, a seaming machine (either manual or electric) is run along each seam to fold the metal into a watertight lock. This requires specialized equipment that most homeowners do not own.

Snap-lock systems are easier — the panels simply press together — but are generally rated for lower wind loads than mechanically seamed systems.

### Step 9: Flashing and Trim

Ridge caps, rake trim, valley flashing, and penetration flashings are installed last. Each piece interlocks with the panels and is sealed to prevent water entry. Pipe boots and vent flashings for metal roofing are different from those used on shingle roofs — they must accommodate the raised seam profile.

## Can You DIY a Standing Seam Metal Roof?

The honest answer: probably not for your primary residence. Here is why.

**Specialized tools.** Seaming machines, metal brakes (for bending trim), and sometimes portable roll-forming machines are required. Renting these is possible but adds significant cost and a learning curve.

**Precision requirements.** Metal roofing is unforgiving. A shingle that is slightly crooked is invisible. A metal panel that is 1/8 inch out of plumb at the eave will be 1/2 inch off at the ridge. You cannot fudge metal.

**Safety.** Metal panels are large, awkward, and slippery. Working on a roof with large panels in any wind is dangerous. The panels act as sails.

**Warranty implications.** Many manufacturer warranties require installation by a certified contractor. DIY installation may void the material warranty.

Where DIY standing seam makes sense: a small outbuilding, shed, or covered patio where the stakes are lower, the area is manageable, and the roof pitch is moderate. For a primary residence, hire a contractor who specializes in metal roofing — not a general roofer who occasionally does metal.

## Cost Variables

| Factor | Impact on Cost |
|---|---|
| Roof pitch (steeper = harder) | +10% to +25% for steep pitches |
| Roof complexity (dormers, valleys) | +15% to +40% for complex rooflines |
| Story height | +10% to +20% for 2+ stories |
| Panel profile choice | Varies by manufacturer |
| Snow guards (cold climates) | +$1–$3/sq ft |
| Insulation (if adding) | +$2–$5/sq ft |

The [American Wood Council](https://www.awc.org/) provides structural guidelines relevant to roof framing that supports metal roofing. The [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) governs roofing installations including wind uplift requirements and fastening schedules specific to metal.

Permits are typically required for a full re-roof. Check with your local building department before starting. Some jurisdictions require engineered drawings for metal roofing installations, particularly in high-wind zones.
