Getting a water heater repaired on short notice is one of those homeowner experiences that separates the prepared from the panicked. The good news: most water heater problems have straightforward fixes. The bad news: figuring out which problem you have at midnight, with no hot water and a house full of unhappy people, requires a level head.

Here is what you actually need to know before you call anyone or start unscrewing things yourself.

## Understanding Your Water Heater Type

The repair approach changes dramatically based on what you have. Open the utility closet and figure out which one is staring back at you.

**Conventional tank water heaters** are the most common. A big insulated cylinder, 40 to 80 gallons, heated by either a gas burner at the bottom or electric elements inside the tank. These are the easiest to troubleshoot and the cheapest to repair.

**Tankless (on-demand) water heaters** heat water as it flows through the unit. More efficient, but the internals are more complex. Error codes on the digital display can point you toward the problem, but the repairs often require specialized knowledge.

**Heat pump water heaters** pull heat from the surrounding air. They have both a refrigeration system and electric backup elements. Repair complexity is higher, and parts availability can be limited.

**Solar water heaters** use roof-mounted collectors. Problems can be in the collectors, the pump, the storage tank, or the plumbing between them. These almost always need a specialist.

For this guide, we are mostly talking about conventional tank heaters, since they represent roughly 80% of residential installations.

## The Diagnostic Checklist

Before spending a dime, run through these checks in order.

### For Gas Water Heaters

1. **Is the gas supply on?** Check the valve on the gas line feeding the heater. It should be parallel to the pipe, not perpendicular. Also verify that other gas appliances (stove, furnace) are working.
2. **Is the pilot light lit?** Look through the small viewport at the bottom of the unit. If you see no flame, follow the relighting instructions on the label. Our guide on [how to turn on a gas water heater](/blog/turn-on-gas-water-heater-honest-advice-for-your-home/) walks you through it step by step.
3. **Is the thermostat set correctly?** The dial on the gas valve should be set between 120 and 140 degrees F. The [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/water-heating) recommends 120 degrees F for most households.
4. **Do you smell gas?** If yes, do not touch anything electrical. Do not flip switches. Leave the house and call your gas company's emergency line. This is the one scenario where you never attempt a DIY fix.

### For Electric Water Heaters

1. **Check the breaker.** Electric water heaters usually have a dedicated 30-amp double-pole breaker. If it is tripped, reset it once. If it trips again immediately, there is an electrical fault --- stop and call an electrician.
2. **Check the high-temperature cutoff.** Most electric heaters have a red reset button on the upper thermostat behind the access panel. Press it firmly. If the unit starts heating, the cutoff tripped due to overheating. If it trips again, the thermostat or element is failing.
3. **Test for power at the thermostat.** If you own a multimeter and know how to use it safely, you can test for 240 volts at the upper thermostat. No power means a wiring problem upstream. Power but no heat means the thermostat or element is dead.

### For Both Types

- **Check the [water supply](/blog/water-heater-pipe-size-what-homeowners-should-know/).** Make sure the cold water inlet valve on top of the heater is fully open.
- **Check for leaks.** Inspect the connections at the top, the T&P valve on the side, and the drain valve at the bottom. Also look for water pooling around the base.
- **Note the age.** The serial number contains the manufacture date. Most manufacturers encode the month and year in the first four characters. A 15-year-old unit with a major failure is telling you it is time for replacement.

## Repair Costs by Problem Type

Here is what you can realistically expect to pay, organized by urgency and complexity.

### Quick Fixes (Under $200)

| Problem | DIY Cost | Pro Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Pilot light will not stay lit (thermocouple) | $10 - $30 | $150 - $250 |
| Tripped high-temperature cutoff | $0 (reset button) | $100 - $150 |
| Thermostat adjustment | $0 | $75 - $150 |
| Sediment-related noise | $0 (flush it) | $100 - $200 |

### Moderate Repairs ($200 - $500)

| Problem | DIY Cost | Pro Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Electric element replacement | $20 - $40 | $200 - $350 |
| Gas valve replacement | Not recommended DIY | $300 - $500 |
| Anode rod replacement | $20 - $50 | $150 - $300 |
| T&P valve replacement | $15 - $30 | $150 - $250 |

### Major Repairs or Replacement ($500+)

| Problem | Typical Pro Cost |
|---|---|
| Tank replacement (40-50 gallon gas) | $1,200 - $2,200 |
| Tank replacement (40-50 gallon electric) | $1,000 - $1,800 |
| Tankless unit replacement | $2,500 - $5,000 |
| Gas line repair or rerouting | $500 - $1,500 |

Add $100 to $300 for after-hours emergency service on top of any of these numbers.

## Code Requirements You Should Know About

Water heater installations and certain repairs are regulated by local building codes, typically based on the [International Residential Code](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70). Here is what matters for repairs:

**Permits.** Most jurisdictions require a permit for water heater replacement but not for component repairs like thermocouples, elements, or thermostats. Call your local building department to verify. The permit typically costs $50 to $150.

**Expansion tanks.** Many jurisdictions now require a thermal expansion tank on closed-loop water systems. If you are replacing a unit, the inspector may require you to add one. Parts cost $30 to $50; installation adds $100 to $200 if you hire it out.

**Earthquake straps.** Required in seismic zones (California, Oregon, Washington, parts of the Midwest). Two straps, upper and lower third of the tank. About $15 for a kit.

**Drain pans.** Required when the heater is installed above living space or where a leak could cause damage. A galvanized or plastic pan with a drain line costs $20 to $40.

**Venting.** Gas water heaters must be properly vented. Natural draft units use a metal flue; power-vent units use PVC or CPVC. Never modify venting without understanding the combustion air requirements.

## Finding a Legitimate 24-Hour Service

The emergency plumbing market has its share of overchargers and underqualified operators. Protect yourself:

**Verify licensing before they arrive.** Every state has an online contractor license lookup. A plumber working on gas lines must hold a valid plumbing license in your state. The [EPA's WaterSense program](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) can also help you find certified professionals.

**Ask about flat-rate vs. time-and-materials pricing.** Flat-rate is usually better for the homeowner because you know the cost upfront. Time-and-materials can spiral, especially at after-hours rates.

**Beware the upsell.** A common tactic: the tech arrives, diagnoses a minor problem, then recommends full replacement. Get a second opinion during business hours if the quote seems excessive for the symptom.

**Check if your home warranty covers it.** Many home warranty plans include water heater repair. The service call fee is typically $75 to $125, which can be less than a plumber's diagnostic fee.

## Temporary Measures to Get Through the Night

Not every water heater failure needs an immediate fix. These stopgaps buy you time to schedule a proper repair.

**Boil water on the stove** for dishes and basic cleanup. It sounds primitive, but it works.

**Use the gym or a neighbor's shower.** Swallow your pride for one morning.

**If you have a small leak,** place a bucket and towel underneath and shut off the cold water inlet to the heater. This stops the leak by stopping the water supply. You will have no hot water, but you will also not have water damage.

**If the water is lukewarm,** turn up the thermostat slightly and give it 30 minutes. If the temperature improves, you may be dealing with a heating element that is on its way out but still partially functional.

## Prevention: Avoiding the Midnight Emergency

The best emergency repair is the one that never happens.

- **Flush the tank annually.** Sediment buildup is the leading cause of premature failure.
- **Test the T&P valve yearly.** Lift the lever briefly. Water should flow freely and stop when you release it. If not, replace the valve.
- **Replace the anode rod every 3 to 5 years.** This sacrificial rod protects the tank from corrosion. Once it is consumed, the tank itself starts corroding.
- **Know the age of your unit.** Start budgeting for replacement when it hits 10 years. Proactive replacement during business hours is always cheaper and less stressful than emergency service at midnight.
- **Inspect for [unusual noises](/blog/the-real-deal-on-why-is-water-heater-making-noise/) periodically.** Popping or rumbling sounds are early warnings of sediment problems.

Taking 30 minutes once a year to maintain your water heater can save you hundreds in emergency calls and thousands in premature replacements. That is about the best return on time investment you will find in home maintenance.
