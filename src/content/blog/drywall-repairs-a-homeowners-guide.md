Drywall repair is one of the most common home improvement tasks you will face as a homeowner, and the costs range from under $50 for a small patch to $500 or more for large-scale repairs involving multiple rooms. The good news is that most drywall damage falls into a handful of categories, each with well-established repair methods that a patient DIYer can handle.

Whether you are dealing with nail pops, hairline cracks, small holes from doorknobs, or water-damaged sections that need full replacement, this guide walks through the real costs, the techniques that actually work, and when it makes sense to call a professional instead.

## Types of Drywall Damage and What They Cost to Fix

Not all drywall damage is equal. A thumbnail-sized hole from a wall anchor is a completely different project than a 2-foot section of water-damaged ceiling. Here is what you are likely dealing with:

**Small holes (under 3 inches):** These are the most common — nail holes, anchor holes, doorknob impacts. A mesh patch kit from any hardware store costs $5 to $12 and handles these easily. If you hire a handyman, expect to pay $75 to $150 per patch, often with a minimum service charge.

**Medium holes (3 to 6 inches):** These typically need a California patch or a backer board method. Materials run $10 to $25. A professional will charge $100 to $250 per hole depending on location and accessibility.

**Large holes or damaged sections:** When you need to cut out and replace a section of drywall — say from a plumbing leak or accidental damage — the materials cost $20 to $60 (a sheet of 4x8 drywall is roughly $12 to $18), but the labor is where costs climb. Professional repairs for large sections typically run $250 to $500 or more, particularly if texturing or extensive taping is needed.

**Cracks:** Hairline cracks along seams are usually structural settling and cost $75 to $200 for a pro to repair properly. Recurring cracks in the same spot may indicate a framing issue that needs addressing first.

## The DIY Approach: Step by Step for Small to Medium Repairs

For holes under 6 inches, a DIY repair is genuinely straightforward. Here is the process that gives you professional-looking results.

### Gather Your Materials

You will need joint compound (a pre-mixed "all-purpose" tub works fine for patches — around $8 to $15 for a quart), a 6-inch and 10-inch taping knife, 150-grit sandpaper or a sanding sponge, a self-adhesive mesh patch for small holes, primer, and paint. Total material cost for a typical repair: $20 to $40.

### Prep the Damaged Area

For small holes, peel away any loose paper or debris around the edges. For larger holes, use a drywall saw to cut back to a clean rectangle — it is much easier to patch a neat shape than a ragged hole. If the hole is big enough to need a backer board, cut a piece of scrap drywall or plywood slightly larger than the hole, slide it behind the opening, and secure it with drywall screws through the existing wall.

### Apply the Patch and Mud

Press a mesh patch over small holes. For larger repairs with a backer board, screw in your new drywall piece and tape the seams with paper or mesh tape. Then apply the first coat of joint compound with your 6-inch knife. The goal is not perfection here — it is coverage.

Let it dry fully. In a heated, ventilated room, this takes 12 to 24 hours. Do not rush it. Damp compound sands badly and shrinks unevenly.

### Sand, Second Coat, Sand Again

Lightly sand the first coat to knock down ridges and high spots. Apply a second, thinner coat with your wider knife, feathering the edges out 2 to 3 inches beyond the first coat. This feathering is what makes the repair invisible. Let dry, sand lightly again.

Most patches need two coats. Larger repairs or deep fills may need three. Each coat should be thinner than the last and spread wider.

### Prime and Paint

This is the step people skip, and it always shows. Bare joint compound absorbs paint differently than the surrounding wall, creating a visible "flash" mark. Apply a coat of PVA primer (around $10 a quart) over the patch and at least 6 inches beyond it. Once dry, paint to match.

If your wall paint has been on for more than a year or two, you may need to repaint the entire wall to get a seamless match. Paint fades more than you realize.

## When to Hire a Professional

There are specific situations where calling a pro is the right move, not a shortfall.

**Water damage:** If drywall has been wet, you need to identify and fix the moisture source first. Wet drywall can harbor [mold](https://www.epa.gov/lead) behind the surface, and a pro can assess whether the framing and insulation behind the wall are compromised.

**Ceiling repairs:** Working overhead with joint compound is significantly harder than wall repairs. The compound is heavy, drips, and is difficult to feather smoothly against gravity. Ceiling texture matching is its own skill.

**Texture matching:** If your walls have knockdown, orange peel, or skip-trowel texture, matching it invisibly is genuinely difficult. Pros have spray rigs and decades of muscle memory for this. A mismatched texture is more visible than a slight color difference.

**Large areas (more than 4 feet in any direction):** At this scale, you are doing taping work, not patching. Getting long seams flat and invisible requires technique that takes time to develop.

Professional drywall repair typically costs $250 to $750 for most residential jobs. Some contractors charge by the hour ($60 to $85 per hour is typical), while others price by the patch or by the square footage of affected area.

## Cost Comparison: DIY vs. Professional

| Repair Type | DIY Cost | Professional Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Small hole (nail/anchor) | $5–$15 | $75–$150 |
| Medium hole (3–6 in) | $15–$30 | $150–$250 |
| Large section replacement | $30–$60 | $300–$500+ |
| Ceiling crack repair | $15–$25 | $150–$300 |
| Full room retaping | $50–$100 | $500–$1,200 |

These numbers assume you already own basic tools. If you need to buy a mud pan, taping knives, and a sanding sponge, add $25 to $40 to the DIY column.

## Common Mistakes That Make Repairs Worse

After patching a lot of walls over the years, these are the errors I see most often:

**Applying compound too thick.** It shrinks as it dries, and thick layers crack. Multiple thin coats always beat one heavy coat.

**Not feathering the edges.** If you can feel a ridge where the patch meets the original wall, it will be visible after painting. Feather out wide — wider than you think you need to.

**Using hot mud when you do not need to.** Setting-type compound (the powder you mix) is great for deep fills and first coats, but it is harder to sand than pre-mixed all-purpose. Save it for situations where you need fast drying or deep fills.

**Sanding too aggressively.** You are smoothing, not removing. Light pressure with 150-grit is all you need. Over-sanding gouges the paper face of the surrounding drywall.

## Drywall Repair and Lead Paint Concerns

If your home was built before 1978, any repair that disturbs painted surfaces may involve lead paint. The [EPA's Renovation, Repair, and Painting Program](https://www.epa.gov/lead/renovation-repair-and-painting-program) requires certified contractors for work in pre-1978 homes. For DIYers in older homes, a $10 lead test kit from the hardware store is a smart first step.

The [Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/) also maintains safety guidelines for home repair materials, which is worth checking if you are using products you are unfamiliar with.

## Matching Existing Wall Texture

The hardest part of drywall repair is not the patching — it is making the patch disappear. Smooth walls are the easiest: sand flat, prime, paint. But textured walls require matching the existing pattern.

For **orange peel** texture, you can buy aerosol texture cans ($8 to $14) that produce a reasonable match. Practice on cardboard first. Adjust the nozzle distance and pressure until the pattern is close.

For **knockdown** texture, apply joint compound through a hopper gun or with a roller, let it set for 10 to 15 minutes, then lightly flatten the peaks with a wide knife. Timing is everything — too wet and you smear it, too dry and you tear it.

For **popcorn** texture on ceilings, matching is genuinely difficult. Spray-on products exist but rarely look identical to the original. If the repair area is small, you may get away with it. For larger areas, consider scraping the entire ceiling and applying a new texture uniformly.

## When Drywall Repair Signals a Bigger Problem

Sometimes drywall damage is a symptom, not the disease. Cracks that reappear after repair may indicate foundation settling. Bubbling or staining often points to active moisture. Sagging ceilings can mean failed fasteners, an overloaded attic, or water infiltration.

Before repairing, ask yourself: why did this happen? If the answer involves water, structural movement, or recurring impacts, fix the cause first. Otherwise you are just patching over a problem that will come back.
