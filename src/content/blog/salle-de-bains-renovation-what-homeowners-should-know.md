A bathroom renovation — sometimes called a "salle de bains renovation" in design circles influenced by European aesthetics — typically costs between $5,000 and $25,000 for a full remodel of a standard bathroom. The cost swings wildly based on three things: whether you are changing the layout (moving plumbing), the quality of fixtures and finishes you choose, and whether the work uncovers hidden problems like water damage or outdated wiring.

If you are planning a bathroom overhaul, understanding the real scope and cost before you start is the difference between a project that goes smoothly and one that spirals.

## Defining the Scope: Refresh vs. Remodel vs. Gut

The word "renovation" covers a huge range of work, and clarifying what you actually need prevents budget mismatches:

**Cosmetic refresh ($2,000-$6,000):** New paint, updated light fixtures, a new mirror, fresh caulk, re-grouted tile, and maybe a new faucet or showerhead. The layout stays the same. Existing tile, vanity, and tub/shower stay in place. This is the best bang for your buck if the bones are solid.

**Mid-range remodel ($6,000-$15,000):** Replace the vanity, toilet, and shower/tub fixtures. New tile on the floor and possibly the shower surround. Updated lighting and ventilation fan. Plumbing stays in the same locations. This covers most standard bathroom upgrades.

**Full gut renovation ($15,000-$35,000+):** Strip the room to studs, address any structural or moisture damage, potentially move plumbing to change the layout, install all new everything. This is warranted when there is water damage behind the walls, the layout is fundamentally bad, or you are combining two small bathrooms into one.

Most homeowners end up in the mid-range category, and most of the advice here applies to that level of work.

## Where the Money Goes

The cost split for a mid-range bathroom renovation looks roughly like this:

| Category | % of Budget | On a $12,000 Project |
|---|---|---|
| Labor (all trades) | 40-50% | $4,800-$6,000 |
| Tile (floor + shower) | 12-18% | $1,440-$2,160 |
| Vanity + countertop | 10-15% | $1,200-$1,800 |
| Shower/tub fixtures | 8-12% | $960-$1,440 |
| Plumbing (fixtures + connections) | 6-10% | $720-$1,200 |
| Electrical + lighting | 4-6% | $480-$720 |
| Ventilation | 2-3% | $240-$360 |
| Permits | 1-2% | $120-$240 |
| Contingency | 10-15% | $1,200-$1,800 |

That contingency line is not optional. Bathrooms hide problems — water damage behind tile, rot in the subfloor, outdated plumbing that does not meet current code. The [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) has specific requirements for bathroom ventilation, electrical circuits, and waterproofing that may require upgrades in older homes.

## Tile Selection: The Make-or-Break Decision

Tile accounts for more of the bathroom's visual impact than any other element, and the cost range is enormous. Understanding the difference helps you allocate budget wisely.

**Floor tile considerations:**
- Porcelain tile ($3-$10/sq ft material, $8-$15/sq ft installed) is the standard choice — durable, water-resistant, and available in countless styles
- Large-format tiles (12x24 and bigger) look great but require a perfectly level subfloor. Lippage on large tiles is immediately visible and looks terrible
- Smaller mosaic tiles hide subfloor imperfections better but cost more in labor because of the increased grout work

**Shower tile considerations:**
- The shower surround is where waterproofing matters most. The tile is the finish layer; the waterproofing system behind it (Schluter Kerdi, RedGard, or similar) is what actually keeps water out of the walls
- Subway tile ($2-$5/sq ft) remains popular because it is inexpensive, looks clean, and is readily available. The real cost is in the labor pattern — a running bond pattern is cheapest, herringbone or stacked patterns cost more in labor

**The TCNA (Tile Council of North America)** publishes the [handbook for tile installation standards](https://www.tcnatile.com/) — it is the industry reference for proper methods and is worth knowing about if you want to evaluate whether your installer is doing things right.

## What to Splurge On and What to Save On

After doing several bathroom renovations, here is where money is well-spent versus where savings are available:

**Worth spending on:**
- **Waterproofing system.** A proper waterproof membrane behind shower tile prevents the kind of hidden damage that causes entire renovations to fail within 5 years. Budget $200-$400 for materials; this is non-negotiable.
- **Exhaust fan.** A quality, quiet bath fan (80+ CFM, 1.0 sone or lower) costs $100-$250 and prevents moisture problems for years. Cheap fans are loud, move less air, and fail sooner.
- **Toilet.** A good elongated-bowl, comfort-height toilet costs $250-$500 and you will use it multiple times a day for the next 20 years. The $100 builder-grade option flushes poorly and looks cheap.
- **Shower valve.** A pressure-balancing or thermostatic valve ($150-$400) prevents scalding and delivers consistent temperature. This is inside the wall — you cannot upgrade it later without demolition.

**Fine to save on:**
- **Vanity.** A $400-$800 vanity from a home center looks good and functions well. Custom vanities ($1,500-$4,000) are rarely justified unless you have unusual space constraints.
- **Mirror.** A frameless mirror from a glass shop ($50-$100) or a simple framed mirror ($80-$200) works as well as a $500 designer mirror.
- **Accessories.** Towel bars, toilet paper holders, and robe hooks from mid-range brands ($15-$40 each) are functionally identical to premium versions ($80-$200 each).
- **Light fixtures.** Vanity lights at the $60-$150 range offer plenty of attractive options without approaching the $300+ designer price point.

## Timeline: What Actually Happens

A mid-range bathroom remodel takes 2 to 4 weeks for professional contractors. Here is the typical sequence:

**Week 1: Demolition and prep.** Tear out old fixtures, tile, and vanity. Inspect for hidden damage. Address any structural, plumbing, or electrical issues discovered during demo. This is where surprises happen.

**Week 2: Rough-in and substrate.** Plumber adjusts or replaces supply and drain lines. Electrician updates wiring and adds circuits if needed. Cement board or waterproofing membrane goes on shower walls. Subfloor is repaired or leveled.

**Week 3: Tile and fixtures.** Shower tile installation (takes 2-3 days for tile plus a day for grout). Floor tile goes down. Vanity, toilet, and shower trim get installed. Painting happens around the tile work.

**Week 4: Finish and punch list.** Final connections, caulking, hardware installation, cleanup. Walk-through to catch any items that need correction.

If you are doing portions yourself, multiply the timeline by 2 to 3. Weekend-warrior bathroom renovations commonly take 6 to 10 weeks because you are working around a day job and learning as you go.

## Working With Contractors

Getting the right contractor matters more in a bathroom than in most rooms because every trade interacts — plumbing, electrical, tile, and carpentry all need to coordinate in a small space.

**For the best results:**
- Get three detailed, itemized quotes. A single line item of "$12,000 — bathroom remodel" tells you nothing. You want labor, materials, fixtures, and contingency broken out.
- Ask who does the tile work. Some GCs sub out tile to specialists; others have their crew do it. Tile quality varies dramatically based on the installer's skill.
- Clarify what is NOT included. Fixtures you supply, permits, disposal fees, and paint touch-up in adjoining rooms are common exclusions.
- Visit a completed project. Any contractor worth hiring has recent work they can show you.

## DIY-Friendly Portions

Even if you hire out the major trades, there are portions of a bathroom renovation that save real money when done yourself:

- **Demolition.** Tearing out old tile, vanities, and fixtures saves $500-$1,000 in labor. Just be careful around plumbing connections and electrical — shut off water and power first.
- **Painting.** Walls and ceiling painting after tile work saves $200-$400.
- **Hardware and accessories.** Installing towel bars, TP holders, mirrors, and medicine cabinets saves $100-$300 in labor.
- **Vanity installation** (if it is a freestanding unit with a standard faucet) is manageable for a handy homeowner.

## Permits and Code Requirements

Most jurisdictions require permits for bathroom work that involves:
- Moving or adding plumbing fixtures
- Changing electrical circuits or adding GFCI protection
- Modifying structural elements (walls, subfloor)
- Adding or modifying ventilation

A simple cosmetic refresh (paint, hardware, caulk) typically does not require a permit. A mid-range remodel that replaces fixtures in the same locations may or may not require one depending on your jurisdiction — check with your local building department before starting.

Permit costs typically run $100 to $300 and include one or two inspections. Skipping the permit saves a small amount of money but creates potential problems at resale when a buyer's inspector asks whether renovations were permitted.

## Protecting the Investment

A well-executed bathroom renovation should last 15 to 25 years before the next major update. To protect that investment:

- Run the exhaust fan during and 20 minutes after every shower
- Recaulk shower corners and the tub-to-tile junction annually, or whenever caulk shows cracks or mold
- Fix dripping faucets promptly — the water damage they cause costs far more than the repair
- Use a bathroom-rated paint (semi-gloss or satin with mildew resistance)
