Painting a single room costs $200 to $800 with a professional painter, or $75 to $300 if you handle the work yourself. The exact number comes down to room size, ceiling height, wall condition, and how much prep your walls need. A standard 10x12 bedroom with 8-foot ceilings and walls in decent shape sits at the lower end of both ranges — larger living rooms or rooms needing extensive repair push toward the top.

## How to Calculate Your Specific Room Cost

Rather than guessing, you can calculate a reasonable estimate in about five minutes. Here is the math:

**Step 1: Measure the paintable wall area.** Multiply the room perimeter (sum of all wall lengths) by the ceiling height. A 10x12 room has a 44-foot perimeter. At 8 feet high, that is 352 square feet of wall area.

**Step 2: Subtract openings.** A standard door is about 21 square feet. A standard window is about 15 square feet. If your room has one door and two windows, subtract 51 square feet. Your paintable area is roughly 300 square feet.

**Step 3: Estimate paint needed.** One gallon of quality paint covers 350 to 400 square feet on smooth surfaces. For two coats on 300 square feet, you need about 1.5 gallons. Buy two gallons — you will be glad to have the extra for touch-ups.

**Step 4: Add up costs.**

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| 2 gallons of quality paint | $70–$120 |
| Primer (if needed) | $20–$40 |
| Roller covers (2–3) | $10–$15 |
| Painter's tape (2 rolls) | $10–$16 |
| Drop cloths | $10–$20 |
| Brush (if you don't have one) | $10–$20 |
| Spackle and sandpaper | $8–$15 |
| **DIY Total** | **$138–$246** |

For a pro, multiply your wall area by $1.50 to $3.50 per square foot. That 300-square-foot bedroom comes to $450 to $1,050 for a professional job, though most painters quote per room rather than per square foot.

## The Prep Nobody Talks About (But Should)

I have painted rooms where prep took longer than painting. That is not unusual — and it is not wasted time. Every hour spent on prep shows in the finished result.

**Nail holes and dents:** Fill with lightweight spackle, let dry (20 minutes for small fills), sand smooth with 150-grit. Do not skip the sanding. Even a tiny bump under paint becomes visible, especially in raking light from a window.

**Cracks:** Hairline cracks in drywall joints are common and do not indicate structural issues. Clean them out, apply mesh tape if wider than 1/16 inch, cover with joint compound, and sand smooth. Wider or recurring cracks may signal settling — worth investigating before painting over.

**Peeling paint:** Scrape loose paint back to a firm edge, feather the edge with sandpaper, and prime the bare spot. Painting over peeling paint just gives you a fresh layer that peels faster.

**Wall cleaning:** Kitchens need degreasing (TSP solution works). Bathrooms may have mildew (bleach solution, then rinse). General rooms just need a wipe with a damp cloth to remove dust. Paint does not stick to greasy or dusty surfaces.

For homes built before 1978, test for lead paint before sanding or scraping. The [EPA's RRP program](https://www.epa.gov/lead/renovation-repair-and-painting-program) outlines the rules. Home test kits cost $10 to $30 and give you results in minutes. If lead is present, certified handling is required by federal law — this is not optional.

## Picking Paint: Practical Advice

Walk into a paint store and you face hundreds of options. Here is how to cut through the noise.

**For most rooms:** 100% acrylic latex in eggshell finish. It is durable enough for daily life, hides minor wall imperfections, and cleans up with soap and water. This is the default choice for bedrooms, living rooms, dining rooms, and home offices.

**For kitchens and bathrooms:** Satin finish with mildew-resistant properties. The slight extra sheen makes it easier to wipe down, and the mildew resistance matters in humid spaces.

**For trim and doors:** Semi-gloss or satin. Trim takes a beating from vacuums, feet, and furniture, so it needs the toughest finish in the room. Use the same brand as your wall paint — the sheens and tones are designed to complement each other.

**Color selection.** Buy sample sizes (pint or quart) and paint a 2x2-foot swatch directly on the wall. Look at it in morning light, afternoon light, and under your room's artificial lighting. Colors look dramatically different depending on the light source. Gray can look blue, beige can look pink, and white can look yellow — all depending on which direction the windows face.

If you are painting adjacent rooms, look at how the colors work together through doorways. Stand in the hallway and see all the visible colors at once. This catches clashing combinations before you commit.

## A Step-by-Step Single Room Process

This is the sequence I follow every time, whether it is the first room or the thirtieth:

**Day 1 — Prep (2–4 hours for a standard bedroom)**
1. Remove all furniture from the room, or move it to the center and cover with drop cloths
2. Remove outlet covers, switch plates, curtain hardware, and wall decor
3. Fill nail holes and repair any wall damage
4. Sand repairs smooth after they dry
5. Wipe down all walls
6. Tape trim, window frames, and ceiling line

**Day 2 — Prime and first coat (3–5 hours)**
1. Prime any bare spots, stains, or dramatic color-change areas
2. Let primer dry fully (check the can — usually 1–2 hours)
3. Cut in with a brush along all edges, corners, and around trim
4. Roll the walls, working one wall at a time
5. Let first coat dry (2–4 hours depending on paint and humidity)

**Day 3 — Second coat and cleanup (2–4 hours)**
1. Inspect the first coat — touch up any misses or thin spots with the brush
2. Cut in again
3. Roll the second coat
4. Remove tape while the paint is slightly tacky
5. Reinstall outlet covers and switch plates
6. Touch up any bleeds or imperfections

Total time: 7 to 13 hours spread over two to three sessions. A professional two-person crew does the same room in 4 to 6 hours, including prep.

## Mistakes That Cost Time and Money

**Loading too much paint on the brush.** Dip only the bottom third of the bristles. Excess paint drips down the handle, splashes on the floor, and creates thick, saggy lines at cut-in edges.

**Cutting in one wall, then rolling the next.** Always cut in and roll the same wall before moving on. If the cut-in edge dries before you roll into it, you get a visible band where the brush marks meet the roller texture.

**Removing tape too late.** If paint dries completely over the tape edge, pulling the tape peels the paint with it. Remove tape when the paint is dry to the touch but still slightly soft — usually 30 to 60 minutes after the final coat.

**Ignoring ambient conditions.** Paint needs moderate temperature (60–80°F) and reasonable humidity (40–70%) to cure properly. Painting on a humid summer day with no AC running leads to slow drying, sags, and a tacky finish that takes days to harden. In winter, make sure the room is warm enough — paint applied below 50°F may never cure correctly.

## When to Upgrade Beyond DIY

If you are debating between doing it yourself and hiring someone, consider the room's difficulty level. Our guides on [how to paint a room like a pro](/blog/how-to-paint-a-room-like-a-pro/) and [interior house painting services](/blog/interior-house-painting-services-what-homeowners-should-know/) can help you calibrate.

Straightforward rooms — rectangular bedrooms, standard ceilings, minimal trim detail — are where DIY delivers the best value. Your labor is free, and the skill required is modest.

Complex rooms — cathedral ceilings, lots of built-ins, heavy trim detail, or rooms with wallpaper to remove first — are where professional skill and equipment justify the cost. A painter who does this daily works three to four times faster than a weekend DIYer on difficult rooms.

The [EPA's indoor air quality guidance](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality) recommends keeping rooms ventilated during and after painting. Open windows, run fans, and avoid sleeping in a freshly painted room for at least 24 hours.

Also keep in mind: your time has a value. If you make $40 an hour at work and a room takes you 12 hours to paint, you are effectively spending $480 in time plus $200 in materials. A pro charging $600 for the same room is not necessarily more expensive — they just shift the cost from your weekends to your wallet. There is no wrong answer; just make the choice with eyes open.
