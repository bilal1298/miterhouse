Building a deck is one of the most popular outdoor home improvement projects in the U.S., with the average cost running $15,000 to $30,000 for a professionally built 300-square-foot deck, or $5,000 to $12,000 in materials if you build it yourself. The project involves structural framing, footings, and code compliance -- it is not a casual weekend project, but it is achievable for a prepared DIYer.

This guide covers the full sequence from planning through completion, with the specific details most general deck building guides leave out.

## Planning Phase: Before You Buy a Single Board

The planning phase determines whether your deck project succeeds or becomes an expensive headache. Do not skip this.

**Check your local codes and permit requirements.** Nearly every municipality requires a building permit for a new deck. This is non-negotiable. Building without a permit creates problems at inspection, at resale, and with insurance. Permit fees typically run $100 to $500 depending on your area. Your permit application will require a site plan showing the deck's location relative to property lines (setbacks), the house, and any easements.

**Determine your footing requirements.** Your local code will specify footing depth (below the frost line in cold climates), footing diameter, and spacing. In most areas, footings must be concrete and extend at least 12 inches below the frost line. In the southern U.S., the frost line might be 6-12 inches. In the northern states, it can be 36-48 inches deep. This single factor has enormous impact on project cost and difficulty.

**Choose your decking material early.** The material choice affects framing requirements, fastener types, spans, and total cost. Here is a comparison:

| Material | Cost per sq ft (materials) | Lifespan | Maintenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure-treated lumber | $2 -- $5 | 15-20 years | Stain/seal every 2-3 years |
| Cedar | $4 -- $8 | 15-25 years | Seal every 1-2 years or let gray |
| Composite (mid-range) | $6 -- $12 | 25-30 years | Occasional cleaning |
| Composite (premium) | $10 -- $18 | 30+ years | Minimal |
| PVC/cellular | $8 -- $14 | 25-30+ years | Minimal |
| Tropical hardwood (Ipe) | $10 -- $20 | 30-50 years | Periodic oiling |

Pressure-treated pine is the most common choice for DIY builds because of its low cost and availability. Composite has become the dominant choice for professional installations due to its low maintenance. Each has trade-offs covered below.

## Foundation and Framing: Where the Real Work Lives

The visible deck boards are the smallest part of the project. The structure underneath is what matters.

**Laying out footings:**

Start by establishing the ledger board location on the house (more on this below), then use string lines and batter boards to lay out the outer footing positions. Use the 3-4-5 triangle method to confirm square corners -- measure 3 feet along one line, 4 feet along the perpendicular line, and the diagonal between those points should be exactly 5 feet. Scale up (6-8-10 or 9-12-15) for better accuracy on larger decks.

**Ledger board attachment** is the single most critical structural connection. The ledger bolts to the house rim joist and carries half the deck's load. Use 1/2-inch lag screws or through-bolts spaced per code (typically 16 inches on center, staggered). Flash the top of the ledger with metal Z-flashing tucked under the house siding to prevent water intrusion. More deck failures trace back to improper ledger attachment than any other single factor.

[Simpson Strong-Tie](https://www.strongtie.com/) publishes detailed connector specifications and span tables that most building inspectors reference. Their resources are worth bookmarking.

**Post and beam sizing** depends on your span distances and load requirements. A general reference:

- 4x4 posts: adequate for decks under 6 feet high in most codes (though 6x6 is always better)
- 6x6 posts: required for decks over 6 feet high and recommended for all decks
- Beam sizing: a doubled 2x8 typically spans 6-8 feet between posts; doubled 2x10 spans 8-10 feet; doubled 2x12 spans 10-12 feet (verify with your local code and the [IRC guidelines](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/))

**Joist installation** follows the beam. Standard joist spacing for residential decks is 16 inches on center for most materials, though some composite manufacturers require 12-inch spacing. Use joist hangers at the ledger and rim joist connections. Crown each joist (sight along the edge to find the natural bow) and install crown-up.

## Decking Installation: The Visible Part

Once framing is complete and inspected (get the framing inspection before covering it with decking), the boards go down relatively quickly.

**For wood decking:**
- Start at the house and work outward
- Leave a 1/8-inch gap between boards for drainage (use a 16d nail as a spacer)
- Pre-drill near board ends to prevent splitting
- Use deck screws rated for your lumber treatment (ACQ-compatible screws for pressure-treated lumber)
- Stagger end joints at least two joist bays apart

**For composite decking:**
- Follow the manufacturer's installation guide exactly -- warranty depends on it
- Most composites use hidden fastener systems that clip between boards
- Leave expansion gaps as specified (composites expand and contract more than wood)
- Use manufacturer-recommended screws at board ends and where clips are not possible

**Stairs and railings** are where many DIY builds struggle. Stair stringers must be cut accurately -- a consistent rise and run on every step. Most codes require a maximum 7-3/4 inch rise and 10-inch minimum run. Railings must be 36 inches high (42 inches for decks over 30 inches above grade in many jurisdictions) with balusters spaced no more than 4 inches apart.

## Common Mistakes That Cost Money

Having built two decks and helped friends with three more, here are the errors I see repeatedly:

**Not accounting for lumber dimensions.** A 2x6 is actually 1.5 inches by 5.5 inches. A 6x6 post is 5.5 x 5.5. If your plans do not account for actual dimensions, nothing will line up.

**Skipping the gravel base under footings.** Four inches of compacted gravel under your concrete footings provides drainage and prevents frost heaving in cold climates.

**Using the wrong fasteners.** Regular steel screws corrode in pressure-treated lumber within a year. Use stainless steel or ACQ-rated coated screws. This is a $30 upgrade over regular screws for a typical deck -- do not skip it.

**Insufficient diagonal bracing.** A deck frame that seems sturdy during construction can develop lateral sway under load without proper cross-bracing. Add diagonal bracing between posts and beams.

**Ignoring drainage under the ledger.** Water that pools behind the ledger board causes rot at the house connection. Proper flashing, a small gap between the ledger and siding, and drainage at the ledger bolts prevent this.

## Tools You Will Need

Deck building requires a meaningful tool collection. If you do not already own these, factor rental or purchase costs into your budget:

**Essential:**
- Circular saw (a framing blade and a fine-tooth blade for decking)
- Cordless drill/driver and impact driver
- Speed square and framing square
- 4-foot and torpedo levels
- Tape measure (25 feet minimum)
- Chalk line
- Post hole digger or power auger (rent this)
- Clamps (bar clamps and spring clamps)
- Safety gear: safety glasses, hearing protection, work gloves

**Very helpful:**
- Miter saw (for railing components and trim)
- Jigsaw (for notching around posts and obstacles)
- String line and line level or laser level
- Socket set (for lag screws)

Renting a power auger for the footings costs $50-75 per day and saves hours of hand-digging. Worth every penny, especially if you have more than four footings.

## The Permit and Inspection Process

A typical deck permit involves two or three inspections:

1. **Footing inspection** -- after holes are dug but before concrete is poured (inspector checks depth and diameter)
2. **Framing inspection** -- after the structural frame is complete but before decking is installed (inspector checks connections, lumber sizes, spans)
3. **Final inspection** -- after completion (inspector checks railings, stairs, overall compliance)

Schedule inspections in advance. Waiting a week for an inspector to show up while your project sits idle is frustrating but common. Plan for it.

The [AARP livable communities guide](https://www.aarp.org/livable-communities/housing/) has useful resources on accessibility considerations for deck design, including ramp requirements and universal design principles that make decks usable for everyone.

## Timeline Expectations

For a 300-square-foot deck with standard conditions:

| Phase | Professional Crew | DIY (weekends) |
|---|---|---|
| Permit approval | 1-4 weeks | 1-4 weeks |
| Footings | 1 day | 1-2 weekends |
| Framing | 1-2 days | 2-3 weekends |
| Decking | 1 day | 1-2 weekends |
| Stairs and railings | 1 day | 1-2 weekends |
| **Total build time** | **4-6 days** | **6-10 weekends** |

Weather delays, inspection wait times, and material delivery issues can extend both timelines. A realistic DIY schedule for a complete deck build is 2-3 months from permit application to finished project, working weekends.

## Bottom Line

Building a deck is a substantial project that rewards careful planning and proper execution. The framing and footings are structural work -- do them right or hire someone who will. The decking itself is the forgiving part. Budget more time than you think you need, get the permit, and do not cut corners on connections and fasteners. A well-built deck adds living space, increases home value, and lasts decades. A poorly built one becomes a liability.
