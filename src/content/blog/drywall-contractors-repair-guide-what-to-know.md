Hiring a drywall repair contractor sounds simple until you start getting quotes and realize the range is enormous — from $150 for a quick patch to $2,000 or more for a full room's worth of damage. The difference usually comes down to the contractor's overhead, the scope of damage, and whether texture matching is involved.

This guide covers how to find, vet, and work with drywall repair contractors so you get quality work at a fair price without the headaches that come from hiring the wrong person.

## What Drywall Repair Contractors Actually Do

A good drywall contractor handles everything from small patch repairs to full sheet replacement, taping, mudding, and texture matching. Some specialize in new construction finishing, while others focus exclusively on repair and restoration work. The repair specialists are usually who you want for residential damage.

Here is what a typical residential drywall repair job involves:

- **Assessment:** A contractor inspects the damage, checks for underlying issues (moisture, structural movement), and determines whether a patch or full replacement is needed.
- **Prep work:** Removing damaged material, cutting back to solid drywall, and prepping the surrounding area. This includes protecting floors and furniture from dust.
- **Repair or replacement:** Installing new drywall sections, taping seams, applying multiple coats of joint compound, and sanding between coats.
- **Texture matching:** Replicating the existing wall or ceiling texture to make the repair invisible.
- **Priming:** Most contractors will prime the repaired area but stop short of final painting unless you specifically ask (and pay) for it.

## How Much Do Drywall Repair Contractors Charge?

Pricing varies significantly by region, but here are the ranges you should expect:

**Hourly rates:** $60 to $90 per hour is standard for an experienced drywall contractor. Handyman services doing basic patches may charge $45 to $70. In high-cost metro areas, rates of $90 to $120 per hour are not unusual.

**Per-patch pricing:** Many contractors prefer this model for small jobs. Expect $100 to $200 per patch for standard holes, and $200 to $400 for patches that require texture matching. Most contractors have a minimum service call fee of $100 to $175, which means one small nail hole still costs you that minimum.

**Per-room or project pricing:** For larger jobs — say water damage affecting an entire wall or ceiling — contractors typically bid by the project. A single wall rebuild with taping, mudding, and texture matching runs $400 to $900. A full room with ceiling work can be $1,000 to $2,500 depending on complexity.

**Texture matching premium:** If your home has knockdown, orange peel, or popcorn texture, add 20% to 40% to the base repair cost. Matching existing texture is a genuine skill, and contractors who are good at it charge accordingly.

## Finding Qualified Drywall Contractors

The challenge with drywall repair is that the barrier to entry is low. Anyone with a truck and some taping knives can call themselves a drywall contractor. Here is how to filter for quality.

### Check Credentials and Insurance

At minimum, a drywall contractor should carry general liability insurance and workers' compensation coverage. Ask for certificate of insurance — a legitimate contractor will have this ready. In many states, drywall contractors also need a general contractor's license or a specific trade license.

The [Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA)](https://www.osha.gov/) sets workplace safety standards that apply to construction work, including drywall installation and repair. A contractor who follows OSHA guidelines is one who takes their work seriously.

### Ask the Right Questions

Before hiring, ask these specific questions:

1. **How will you match the existing texture?** A vague answer here is a red flag. A skilled contractor will describe the specific technique (hopper gun, hand texture, spray rig) and may ask to see the wall first.
2. **What is your warranty on the work?** Most reputable contractors offer at least a one-year warranty against cracks reappearing or compound failing.
3. **Will you handle dust containment?** Drywall sanding creates fine dust that gets everywhere. A professional should use plastic sheeting and, ideally, a dustless sanding system.
4. **Do you pull permits for large repairs?** In most jurisdictions, replacing large sections of drywall (especially after water damage) may require a permit, particularly if insulation or framing is involved.

### Get Multiple Quotes

Always get at least three written quotes. The quotes should specify the scope of work, materials included, timeline, and payment terms. Be wary of any contractor who wants full payment upfront — standard terms are 30% to 50% deposit with the balance on completion.

## Red Flags to Watch For

After years of seeing good and bad drywall work, these warning signs should make you keep looking:

**No physical business address.** Reputable contractors have an office or at minimum a consistent business presence. A contractor who only communicates through a personal cell phone with no website or reviews is a higher risk.

**Unusually low bids.** If one bid is 40% below the others, that contractor is either cutting corners on materials, underestimating the scope, or planning to hit you with change orders later.

**Unwillingness to provide references.** Any contractor worth hiring has past clients who will vouch for them. If they cannot produce references, they either do not have them or the references are not good.

**Rushing the timeline.** Joint compound needs proper dry time between coats — typically 24 hours in normal conditions. A contractor promising to patch, mud, texture, and prime in a single day is either using setting compound exclusively (which is harder to achieve a smooth finish) or not allowing adequate dry time.

## What Good Drywall Work Looks Like

When a contractor finishes, here is what you should inspect:

**Run your hand across the repair.** You should not feel any ridge, bump, or depression where the patch meets the original wall. The transition should be completely smooth.

**Look at it in raking light.** Hold a flashlight nearly parallel to the wall surface. Imperfections that are invisible in normal lighting show up immediately under raking light. Any competent contractor knows this test and has already used it.

**Check the texture match.** Stand back 3 to 4 feet. The repaired area should be indistinguishable from the surrounding wall. Close-up, minor texture differences are acceptable — from normal viewing distance, they should not be.

**Verify priming.** The repaired area should be primed so that when you or your painter applies the finish coat, the sheen and absorption are uniform.

## Indoor Air Quality During Repairs

Drywall work generates dust and, if older compound or paint is disturbed, potentially volatile organic compounds. The [EPA's guidance on VOCs and indoor air quality](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality) is worth reviewing, especially if anyone in the household has respiratory sensitivities.

Good contractors mitigate this with plastic sheeting over doorways, negative air pressure machines for larger jobs, and HEPA-filtered sanding equipment. If your contractor shows up with none of these for an interior job, ask about their dust control plan.

## DIY vs. Contractor: Where the Line Is

Small patches — anything under about 4 inches — are genuinely easy DIY projects. A mesh patch kit and some all-purpose joint compound will handle nail holes, small drywall anchors, and minor dents. The materials cost under $20 and the skill ceiling is low.

The line where hiring a contractor starts making sense is around medium-sized repairs (6 inches and up) or any job involving:

- Ceiling work
- Texture matching beyond smooth walls
- Water damage where the source needs investigation
- Multiple repairs across several rooms (a contractor working efficiently will be faster and cheaper than your time spent on a learning curve)

## Getting the Best Value

The smartest way to save on drywall contractors is to batch your repairs. That minimum service call fee stings when you are paying it for a single nail hole. Instead, walk through your entire home and catalog every drywall issue — the cracks above door frames, the nail pops in the hallway, the anchor holes in the bedroom. Present them all as one job.

Most contractors will give a significantly better per-repair rate when they are doing 8 to 10 patches in one visit versus making separate trips for each. A job that might cost $150 per individual patch often drops to $80 to $100 each when bundled.

Also consider timing. Drywall contractors are slowest in winter and early spring. Scheduling repair work during these off-peak months may get you better availability and, sometimes, better pricing.
