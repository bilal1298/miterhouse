Painting a room yourself costs between $100 and $300 in materials for an average-sized bedroom (10x12 feet), while hiring a professional runs $300 to $800 per room. The wide range depends on ceiling height, the number of windows and doors, wall condition, and whether you are doing a simple color refresh or a dramatic change that requires extra primer coats.

## Cost Breakdown: Room by Room

Not every room costs the same to paint. Kitchens and bathrooms require moisture-resistant formulations. High-traffic areas like hallways need more durable sheens. Larger rooms with vaulted ceilings need more paint and more time on a ladder.

| Room Type | DIY Cost | Pro Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard bedroom (10x12) | $100–$250 | $300–$700 | Simplest room to paint |
| Living room (15x20) | $200–$400 | $600–$1,200 | More wall area, often vaulted ceilings |
| Kitchen | $150–$350 | $400–$900 | Extra prep around cabinets, grease cleanup |
| Bathroom | $75–$200 | $250–$600 | Moisture-resistant paint required |
| Hallway | $100–$250 | $300–$650 | Awkward angles, lots of cutting in |
| Basement room | $200–$500 | $500–$1,200 | May need moisture barrier primer |

These numbers assume standard 8-foot ceilings and two coats of paint. Add 20 to 40 percent for ceilings above 9 feet.

## What You Actually Need to Buy

Here is a realistic shopping list for painting a single bedroom with two coats:

**Paint:** One gallon covers roughly 350 to 400 square feet on smooth drywall. A 10x12 room with 8-foot ceilings has about 350 square feet of wall space (minus windows and doors), so one gallon is tight — buy two to be safe. At $35 to $60 per gallon for quality interior paint, that is $70 to $120.

**Primer:** If you are covering a dark color, going from oil-based to latex, or painting over stains, you need a separate primer. One gallon of quality primer runs $20 to $40. If you are just refreshing a similar color on clean walls, a paint-and-primer-in-one product can work.

**Supplies:** Roller frames ($5–$10), roller covers ($5–$15 for a 3-pack of quality microfiber), a 2.5-inch angled brush ($10–$20), painter's tape ($5–$8 per roll, and you will need 2–4 rolls), drop cloths ($10–$25), a paint tray ($5–$8), and stir sticks (usually free at the paint store).

Total materials for one room: $130 to $260.

The supplies you buy for the first room carry over to the next ones, so painting multiple rooms at once brings your per-room cost down. By the third room, you are mostly just buying paint.

## Choosing the Right Paint Sheen

Sheen selection is not just about aesthetics — it directly affects durability and how easy the walls are to clean.

**Flat/matte:** Hides imperfections well but marks easily and is hard to clean. Best for adult bedrooms, formal dining rooms, and ceilings. Not suitable for kitchens, bathrooms, or kids' rooms.

**Eggshell:** Slight sheen, still hides minor imperfections, easier to wipe clean. The most popular choice for living rooms, bedrooms, and hallways. This is the all-around default for most rooms.

**Satin:** Noticeable soft sheen, good durability, easy to clean. Works well in kitchens, bathrooms, kids' rooms, and high-traffic areas. Shows wall imperfections more than eggshell.

**Semi-gloss:** Shiny, very durable, easy to clean. Standard for trim, doors, cabinets, and sometimes bathroom walls. Too reflective for large wall areas unless that is the look you want.

**High-gloss:** Maximum durability and sheen. Primarily used for trim and architectural details. Highlights every flaw in the surface underneath.

A common mistake is using flat paint in a kids' room or hallway because it looks nice on the swatch. Within six months, you will have permanent handprints and scuff marks that no amount of scrubbing will remove. Use satin or eggshell in any room that sees real life.

## Prep Work That Actually Matters

Professional painters spend half their time on prep, and there is a good reason. Paint does not hide problems — it highlights them. The shinier the paint, the worse unrepaired walls look.

**Cleaning the walls.** Dust, cobwebs, and kitchen grease prevent paint from adhering properly. Wipe walls down with a damp cloth or use TSP (trisodium phosphate) for greasy kitchen walls. Let everything dry completely.

**Filling holes and cracks.** Use lightweight spackle for nail holes and small dings. For cracks wider than a hairline, use setting-type joint compound — it shrinks less. Sand everything smooth once dry. A 150-grit sanding sponge works well for this.

**Sanding glossy surfaces.** If the existing paint has any sheen, scuff it lightly with 150-grit sandpaper or a sanding sponge. This gives the new paint something to grip. Skipping this step on semi-gloss or gloss surfaces almost guarantees adhesion failure.

**Taping.** Apply painter's tape to trim, baseboards, window frames, and the ceiling line. Press the edge of the tape firmly with a putty knife or credit card to prevent paint from bleeding underneath. Remove the tape while the final coat is still slightly tacky for the cleanest line.

The VOCs (volatile organic compounds) in some paints can affect indoor air quality. Look for low-VOC or zero-VOC paints, especially for bedrooms and nurseries. The [EPA has guidance on VOCs and indoor air quality](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality) worth reviewing before you buy.

## Technique Tips That Save Time and Money

**Cut in before you roll.** Use your angled brush to paint a 2 to 3-inch band along all edges — ceiling line, corners, around trim, outlets, and switches. Then fill in the large areas with a roller. Work one wall at a time so the cut-in edges are still wet when you roll into them, avoiding visible lap marks.

**Use the right roller nap.** For smooth drywall, use a 3/8-inch nap roller cover. For textured walls, go with 1/2-inch or 3/4-inch nap. Using too short a nap on textured walls leaves the valleys unpainted. Using too long a nap on smooth walls creates unwanted texture.

**Maintain a wet edge.** Roll in a "W" or "N" pattern, then fill in with even, overlapping strokes. Never let the leading edge of your painted area dry before you get back to it — that is what causes roller marks and visible seams.

**Do not overload the roller.** Dip, roll off the excess on the tray's ramp, then apply. An overloaded roller drips, splatters, and leaves an uneven coat. It feels slower to use less paint per pass, but you will get a smoother finish and waste less paint.

**Two thin coats beat one thick coat every time.** A thick coat sags, takes forever to dry, and often cracks. Two thin, even coats give you better coverage, a smoother finish, and longer-lasting results.

## When Hiring a Pro Makes Sense

DIY painting is one of the most accessible home improvement projects, but certain situations genuinely call for a professional:

- **High ceilings or stairwells.** Painting a two-story foyer or a stairwell requires scaffolding or extension ladders in awkward positions. This is both physically difficult and legitimately dangerous for a homeowner without experience.
- **Extensive wall repair.** If the drywall has significant damage — large holes, widespread cracking, or water damage — a pro can repair and skim-coat the walls as part of the painting job. See our guide to [professional painting costs](/blog/professional-painting-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/) for what that adds to the bill.
- **Lead paint.** Homes built before 1978 may have [lead-based paint](https://www.epa.gov/lead). Disturbing lead paint through sanding or scraping creates hazardous dust. Professionals certified in lead-safe work practices know how to handle this safely.
- **Time pressure.** A professional crew of two or three painters can knock out a room in a day, including prep. A solo DIYer typically needs two to three days per room, working evenings and weekends.
- **Whole-house repaints.** Painting one room yourself is a weekend project. Painting eight rooms is a multi-week commitment that tests your patience. For whole-house jobs, the pro cost often makes more sense when you factor in your time.

## Saving Money Without Cutting Corners

If you are painting multiple rooms, buy paint in 5-gallon buckets rather than individual gallons — you will save 15 to 25 percent per gallon. This also ensures color consistency across rooms, since there can be slight batch variations between individual gallons.

Skip the cheapest rollers and brushes. A $15 brush lasts through dozens of projects if you clean it properly. A $3 brush leaves bristles in your paint and falls apart after one room.

The [Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/) has resources on safe practices for home improvement projects, including proper ventilation requirements when painting. Keep windows open and fans running, especially with oil-based products.

Do not buy more painter's tape than you need — it is surprisingly expensive and most people over-buy. Measure your trim and window perimeters before you go to the store. Two rolls of 1.88-inch tape usually handles a standard bedroom.

Finally, timing your purchase matters. Paint goes on sale regularly — Memorial Day, Labor Day, and Fourth of July weekends are reliably discounted at the big-box stores. Buying during a sale can save you $10 to $15 per gallon, which adds up fast on a multi-room project.
