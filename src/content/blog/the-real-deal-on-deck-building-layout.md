Getting the deck layout right before cutting a single board saves more time and money than any other step in the build process. A well-planned layout accounts for footing locations, joist spans, traffic flow, furniture placement, and how the deck connects to the house. Rushing through this phase leads to footings in the wrong spots, framing that does not line up, and a finished deck that does not function the way you imagined.

Here is how to lay out a deck properly, from establishing the first reference line to marking footing locations.

## Start With the Ledger Board Location

Every attached deck starts from the ledger board -- the horizontal member that bolts to the house and carries one side of the deck's load. Your ledger location establishes the deck height and alignment for everything else.

**Determining deck height:** The finished deck surface should sit just below the threshold of the door providing access. A 1-inch step down from the door threshold to the deck surface prevents water from pooling against the door frame and entering the house. This means the top of the ledger board sits about 2.5 to 3 inches below the door threshold (accounting for the thickness of the decking boards and any spacer or flashing).

**Marking the ledger line:** Snap a chalk line across the house wall at your calculated ledger height. This line should be level -- use a 4-foot level or, better yet, a laser level for longer spans. The ledger height determines the height of every post, beam, and footing in the project, so accuracy here is critical.

**Account for the siding:** If your house has lap siding, vinyl, or any non-flat exterior, the ledger cannot mount directly over it. You need to either cut away the siding where the ledger attaches (preferred method) or use standoff spacers that create a drainage gap. Most building codes require flashing above and behind the ledger regardless of approach.

## Establishing Square and Parallel Lines

Once the ledger position is marked, you need to establish lines that are square (perpendicular) to the house for the deck's side edges and parallel to the house for the outer beam or rim joist.

**The 3-4-5 method in practice:**

1. Mark a point on your ledger line where one side edge of the deck will be
2. Measure 3 feet from that point along the ledger (parallel to the house) and mark it
3. From the first point, measure 4 feet outward (perpendicular to the house)
4. The diagonal between the 3-foot mark and the 4-foot point should be exactly 5 feet
5. Adjust the outward line until the diagonal measurement is correct

For better accuracy on full-size decks, scale up: 6-8-10, 9-12-15, or 12-16-20. Larger triangles reduce measurement error.

**Using batter boards:** Drive pairs of stakes 2-3 feet beyond each corner of the planned deck. Attach horizontal crosspieces (the batter boards) and run string lines between them. The strings represent the outer edges of the deck. Because the strings attach to batter boards beyond the deck footprint, you can adjust string positions without moving the stakes. This lets you fine-tune the layout until everything is square and at the right dimensions.

**Checking the layout for square:** Measure both diagonals of the rectangle formed by your string lines. If the diagonals are equal, the layout is square. If they differ, the rectangle is racked, and you need to adjust until both diagonals match.

## Determining Footing Locations

Footing placement depends on your beam layout, which depends on joist span and beam span capabilities.

**Working backward from span tables:**

Span tables (published in the [IRC](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) and by lumber associations) tell you the maximum distance a joist can span between supports for a given lumber size and spacing. For example:

- 2x8 joists at 16" on center typically span up to about 10 feet 6 inches
- 2x10 joists at 16" on center span up to about 13 feet 5 inches
- 2x12 joists at 16" on center span up to about 16 feet 5 inches

These numbers determine where your beam goes. If your deck is 14 feet deep (from house to outer edge), 2x10 joists at 16" spacing would be marginal. You would either upgrade to 2x12 joists or add a mid-span beam.

**Beam span determines post spacing.** Similar tables tell you how far a beam can span between posts. A doubled 2x10 beam typically spans 8-10 feet between posts. So for a 16-foot-wide deck using doubled 2x10 beams, you need posts at roughly 8-foot intervals.

**Marking footing locations on the ground:**

With your string lines in place showing the beam line (determined by joist span), drop a plumb bob from the string at each post location. Mark the ground below. These marks are your footing center points.

Double-check every footing location for:
- Distance from the house (should match across all footings on the same beam line)
- Spacing between footings along the beam line
- Relationship to property lines (setback requirements)
- Clearance from underground utilities (call 811 before digging)

## Layout Design Principles That Matter

Beyond the technical layout, how you design the deck's shape and features determines how well it actually functions.

**Size the deck for real furniture, not abstract square footage.** A dining table with four chairs needs an 8x8 foot area minimum. A grill station needs 4 feet of clearance on all sides for safety. Two lounge chairs need about 6x8 feet. A traffic path between zones needs at least 3 feet clear. Sketch your furniture layout on the plan before finalizing dimensions. Many homeowners build a deck too small because the empty frame looked big enough during construction.

**Consider traffic flow from the house.** Where does the door open? Which direction do people naturally walk when they step out? Avoid placing the grill directly in the path from the door to the stairs. Put the dining area closest to the kitchen door if you will be carrying food and dishes back and forth.

**Stair location matters more than you think.** Stairs should lead toward the area of the yard people will actually walk to. If your backyard opens to the right, stairs on the right side of the deck make intuitive sense. Stairs centered on a long deck edge work well for large decks but can feel awkward on smaller ones.

**Think about sun and shade patterns.** Spend a weekend afternoon in your yard and note where the sun hits at different times. Placing the seating area in a spot that gets afternoon shade in summer makes the deck more usable. If there is no natural shade, plan for a pergola, shade sail, or umbrella in your layout from the start rather than trying to add it later.

## Multi-Level and Complex Layouts

If your yard slopes significantly or you want distinct zones (dining, lounging, grilling), a multi-level deck may make sense. Each level adds framing complexity and cost but can work with the terrain rather than fighting it.

**Considerations for multi-level designs:**

- Each level needs its own beam and footing system
- Steps between levels must meet the same code requirements as stairs to the ground
- The height difference between levels should be a consistent step height (typically 7 to 7-3/4 inches per step)
- Framing transitions between levels need to be engineered for lateral stability
- Multi-level decks often cost 30-50% more than a single-level deck of equivalent total square footage

**Angled or curved sections** add visual interest but significantly increase material waste and build complexity. Every angled cut means a scrap piece. Curved framing requires kerfs or specialized lumber. If you are a first-time deck builder, stick to rectangular sections.

## Documenting Your Layout

Before you finish the layout phase, create a clear drawing with:

- All dimensions (overall and between footings)
- Footing locations with distances from the house and from each other
- Ledger board position and height relative to a reference point
- Joist direction, size, and spacing
- Beam size and location
- Stair location and direction
- Railing locations
- Any built-in features (bench locations, planter boxes)

This drawing serves three purposes: it is your permit application plan, your shopping list generator, and your build reference. Many homeowners skip the formal drawing and regret it when they realize mid-build that the footing they just poured is 6 inches off from where it needs to be.

[Simpson Strong-Tie](https://www.strongtie.com/) offers free deck planning tools and connector specification guides that pair well with your layout drawings. The [EPA's WaterSense resources](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) are relevant if your deck design incorporates any water features or irrigation.

## Using Stakes and String to Visualize Before Building

Before digging a single footing hole, outline the deck on the ground using stakes and string at the actual finished dimensions. Stand at the door and look at it. Walk around it. Set up chairs inside the string boundary. Live with it for a weekend.

This costs nothing and takes an hour but has saved countless homeowners from building the wrong size or shape. It is one thing to look at measurements on paper. It is another to stand in the actual space and realize the deck is too narrow for the table you wanted, or that the stairs would land in a mud patch.

## Bottom Line

Deck layout is planning translated into physical reality. Every measurement you establish at this stage becomes permanent once concrete is poured and lumber is cut. Take the time to set batter boards, verify square, calculate spans, and mark footings precisely. If you bring the same attention to layout that you bring to picking out decking material, the build phase goes dramatically faster and the finished deck works exactly as you intended.
