Knowing when to call a drywall repair professional — and what separates a skilled one from someone who will leave your walls looking worse — can save you hundreds of dollars and weeks of frustration. Professional drywall repair typically costs between $200 and $800 for common residential jobs, but the quality gap between contractors is wider than almost any other home trade.

This guide covers what professional drywall repair actually involves, how to evaluate the people doing the work, and what you should expect from the finished product.

## What Makes Professional Repair Different from DIY

The materials are identical. A pro uses the same joint compound, paper tape, and mesh patches available at any hardware store. The difference is speed, technique, and — most critically — the ability to make a repair invisible.

Professional drywall finishers develop their technique over thousands of hours. They know how much pressure to apply on each knife stroke, how thin to spread each coat, and exactly how long to wait before sanding. These are not things you learn from a YouTube video. They are muscle memory.

Where this matters most:

**Seam finishing.** A pro can make a butt joint (where two non-tapered drywall edges meet) disappear. This is arguably the hardest drywall skill. Amateurs almost always leave a visible hump.

**Texture replication.** Matching existing knockdown, orange peel, or hand-applied textures is as much art as craft. Professionals have spray rigs calibrated for specific patterns and the experience to match what is already on your walls.

**Large-scale repairs.** Replacing a 4-by-8-foot section of drywall involves hanging the sheet, setting screws at the right depth, taping four seams, and applying three coats of compound with proper feathering. Each step has to be done correctly or the next one suffers.

## Types of Professionals Who Handle Drywall

Not everyone who fixes drywall has the same skill set. Understanding the differences helps you hire the right person.

**Drywall finishing specialists:** These are the craftspeople who tape, mud, and texture for a living. They are the best choice for repairs that need to be invisible, especially on textured walls and ceilings. They typically charge $65 to $95 per hour.

**General handymen:** A competent handyman can handle basic patches — nail holes, small anchor holes, simple cracks on smooth walls. They usually charge less ($45 to $75 per hour) but may not have the finishing skills for complex texture matching. For straightforward repairs, a handyman is often the most cost-effective option.

**General contractors:** If your drywall damage is part of a larger project — water damage that also requires plumbing work, framing repairs, and insulation replacement — a general contractor can manage the entire scope. They are the most expensive option (their markup covers project management) but simplify multi-trade projects.

**Restoration companies:** After water damage, fire damage, or mold remediation, specialized restoration companies handle drywall as part of their scope. They are familiar with insurance claims and documentation requirements.

## The Professional Repair Process

Understanding the steps helps you evaluate whether a contractor is doing thorough work or cutting corners.

### Step 1: Damage Assessment

A professional starts by determining the full extent of the damage. For water-stained drywall, this means checking behind the visible damage with a moisture meter. For cracks, they assess whether the crack is cosmetic (settling) or structural. This assessment should be free when it is part of the bidding process.

### Step 2: Source Correction

Any professional worth hiring will ask about the cause of the damage before repairing it. Patching over an active leak or ongoing structural movement is a waste of everyone's time and money. If the cause has not been addressed, a good contractor will say so and may decline the job until it is.

### Step 3: Demolition and Prep

Damaged drywall gets cut back to solid material. For water damage, this often means cutting higher and wider than the visible stain because moisture wicks through drywall beyond what you can see. The work area gets protected with drop cloths and plastic sheeting.

### Step 4: Installation

New drywall sections are cut to fit, shimmed if necessary to sit flush with the existing surface, and secured with drywall screws. Screw spacing follows standard framing — every 12 to 16 inches along studs, with screws dimpled just below the surface without breaking the paper face.

### Step 5: Taping and Mudding

Seams get taped (paper tape for corners and butt joints, mesh tape for flat seams in some cases) and covered with multiple coats of joint compound. Most professionals apply three coats: a bedding coat, a fill coat, and a skim coat. Each coat is wider than the last — the final coat may feather out 10 to 12 inches from the seam to create an imperceptible transition.

### Step 6: Sanding and Texture

After the final coat dries, the surface is sanded smooth. For textured walls, the matching texture is applied and allowed to dry. Some textures require a knockdown step where wet compound peaks are flattened with a knife after a brief set time.

### Step 7: Priming

The repaired area receives primer to seal the joint compound and provide a uniform base for painting. Without primer, the compound absorbs paint differently than the surrounding surface, creating visible flashing.

## How to Evaluate Finished Work

Before signing off on a job, inspect the repairs under these conditions:

Hold a bright light at a low angle against the wall (raking light). Every professional knows this is the test. Imperfections invisible under normal room lighting show up immediately. Any contractor confident in their work will not mind you doing this.

Run your hand across the transition between the repair and the original surface. It should be completely smooth — no ridges, no dips, no rough spots.

For textured walls, step back to normal viewing distance (4 to 6 feet). The texture pattern in the repaired area should be visually indistinguishable from the surrounding wall. Some slight variation is normal up close, but from conversational distance, you should not be able to identify where the repair was made.

## Working Safely Around Older Drywall

Homes built before 1978 may have lead paint on drywall surfaces. The [EPA's Renovation, Repair, and Painting Program](https://www.epa.gov/lead/renovation-repair-and-painting-program) requires that contractors working in pre-1978 homes be EPA-certified and follow lead-safe work practices. This is not optional — it is a federal regulation.

If your home is older, ask any contractor you are considering whether they are RRP-certified. If they are not, or if they do not know what you are asking about, find someone else.

[OSHA standards](https://www.osha.gov/) also apply to drywall work, particularly regarding dust exposure and fall protection for ceiling work. A professional who follows safety protocols is one who does quality work in other areas too.

## Coordinating with Painters

Most drywall repair professionals do not paint. They will prime the repair and leave it ready for a painter or for you to paint. This is actually a good thing — drywall finishing and painting are different skills, and trying to do both often means one suffers.

Plan your painting immediately after drywall work. Exposed primer collects dust and fingerprints quickly. ## What About Plaster vs. Drywall?

If your home was built before the 1950s, you may have plaster walls rather than drywall. Plaster repair is a different skill set. Some drywall professionals can handle plaster, but many cannot. The materials and techniques are different — plaster is applied wet over lath strips and builds up in layers, while drywall is dry-hung in sheets.

If you have plaster walls, specifically ask any contractor about their plaster experience. Get photos of past plaster work. The wrong approach to plaster repair (like using drywall compound to fill cracks in plaster) creates problems that are harder to fix than the original damage.
