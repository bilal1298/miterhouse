Before you pull a permit or swing a pry bar, get your tool kit sorted — because buying the wrong stuff first, or the right stuff in the wrong order, costs you real money.

These are the essential tools for home renovation, organized by category, with honest takes on what to buy, what to skip, and where brand choice actually matters. Whether you're heading into a [DIY bathroom remodel](/blog/diy-bathroom-remodel-cost/) or starting a [deck build from scratch](/blog/how-to-build-a-diy-deck-on-a-budget/), this is the kit that gets you through it.

---

## Measuring and Layout — Buy These First

Good measurements cost nothing. Bad ones cost you an afternoon of reruns and a trip back to the lumber yard.

### Tape Measure — $12–$30

Get a 25-foot, 1-inch-wide blade. The wide blade holds itself extended so you can measure solo across a room without the tape buckling at 8 feet. Stanley FatMax and Milwaukee are both around $20 and hold up to daily abuse. Skip the narrow 3/4-inch blades — they fold on you mid-extension.

### Speed Square — $10–$25

A triangular layout tool that marks a clean 90-degree cut line on lumber, doubles as a guide for your circular saw, and lets you check corners in seconds. The Swanson 7-inch Speed Square at $12 is the standard. Buy it before your first project and you'll use it on every single one after.

### 4-Foot Level — $25–$60

Anything shorter than 4 feet is close to useless for cabinets, shelving, and tile. A 2-footer misses the subtle tilts that make a run of tile look wrong from across the room. Empire and Stanley both make reliable 4-foot levels at $25–$40. If you're doing significant cabinet or tile work, the step up to a 6-foot level ($50–$80) pays for itself in fewer mistakes.

### Stud Finder — $20–$55

Electronic stud finders aren't magic, but they're better than knocking and guessing. The Zircon StudSensor HD55 (~$30) finds studs reliably in standard drywall and also signals live AC wires — which you want to know about before you drill into any wall. Calibrate it every time by starting over a stud-free section of wall.

## Cutting Tools

### Circular Saw — $80–$200

The primary saw for cutting lumber, plywood, and sheet goods. A 7-1/4-inch saw handles dimensional lumber and full sheets of plywood without complaint. The SKILSAW SPT67WL at about $130 is genuinely good — worm-drive action, lightweight magnesium shoe, cuts straight. The SKIL 5280-01 at $85 works fine for a starter kit if budget is tight.

One upgrade worth making immediately: swap the stock blade for a Diablo 40-tooth combination blade ($15). The blade that ships in the box is usually throwaway quality.

### Oscillating Multi-Tool — $60–$180

If one tool surprises new renovators, it's this one. An oscillating multi-tool makes flush cuts against walls, cuts through nails in tight spaces, removes grout, and undercuts door casings for flooring — jobs that would otherwise mean renting something specialized. The Ryobi PCL430B handles light work well at around $60 bare. For heavier use, the DeWalt DCS356B (~$130) is noticeably faster and more durable.

### Utility Knife — $10–$35

Used more than you'd think. Scoring drywall, trimming insulation, opening packaging, cutting flooring underlayment — a good utility knife earns its spot in your apron every session. The Milwaukee 48-22-1940 (~$20) has a comfortable grip and a tool-free blade-change that you'll appreciate when you're covered in drywall dust. Keep a pack of extra blades on hand. A dull blade causes more injuries than a sharp one.

## Drilling and Fastening

### Cordless Drill/Driver — $100–$180 (combo kit)

Buy this before anything else on this list. A 20V brushless drill/driver combo kit — drill, two batteries, charger — handles more than half of everything you'll do on a renovation. The DeWalt DCD771C2 and Milwaukee M18 combo kits are the standards at $120–$180. If you're starting from zero, pick one battery platform and commit. Your future tool purchases will share those batteries, and the savings compound fast.

### Impact Driver — $60–$130 (bare tool)

An impact driver isn't a drill. It delivers high rotational force in rapid bursts and drives long deck screws, lag bolts, and self-tapping screws faster and with less wrist strain than any drill can. Once you use one on a deck build, going back to a drill for fastening feels like punishment. Get it in the same battery platform as your drill — Milwaukee M18 and DeWalt 20V bare tools both run $60–$130.

## Demolition

### Pry Bar — $15–$40

A flat bar is the right tool for pulling trim, removing baseboards cleanly, and popping up old flooring without destroying the subfloor underneath. The Stanley 18-inch flat bar at $18 handles most homeowner needs. For heavier demo — pulling embedded nails from framing, lifting subfloor panels — move up to a 30-inch curved wrecking bar. The extra reach turns a 20-minute battle into a 5-minute job.

### Hammer — $20–$50

A 16-oz claw hammer for finish work and trim, a 20-oz framing hammer if you're swinging at structural framing all day. The Estwing 16-oz (~$35) is the classic choice for good reason — one-piece forged steel, no wood handle to crack or loosen. For most homeowners doing mixed work, the 16-oz covers everything.

### Reciprocating Saw — $80–$180

Where a circular saw can't go — inside walls, overhead cuts, in tight corners — the reciprocating saw handles it. Cuts through drywall, pipe, nails, and framing with equal ease. The Milwaukee M18 FUEL (~$170 bare) is the pro standard. For occasional use, the Ryobi PCL515B at ~$80 holds up fine. Buy a variety blade pack: wood with nails, metal-cutting, and a demo blade for mixed materials.

## Finishing Tools

### Random Orbital Sander — $40–$100

Drywall repairs, furniture refinishing, deck prep before staining — the random orbital handles all of it without leaving the swirl marks a belt sander leaves. The Ridgid R2611 at ~$50 is a solid budget pick. Festool makes the best sanders in the business, but $300 is hard to justify unless you're doing a lot of finish carpentry. Stock 80-, 120-, and 220-grit discs and you'll cover the range. Before you [paint a room like a pro](/blog/how-to-paint-a-room-like-a-pro/), get the surfaces right first — this is the tool that does that.

### Caulk Gun — $10–$30

A $12 Albion or Hyde caulk gun gives you control that squeezing the tube by hand never will. For tile surrounds, window trim, and door frames — anywhere you're doing a lot of runs — the $30 dripless models save you from fighting the tube between cuts. Clean caulk lines are what separate finish work that looks professional from work that just looks finished.

### Putty Knives — $8–$25 for a set

Get at least two: a 3-inch for patching small holes and a 6-inch for skim-coating larger repairs. Hyde is the reliable standard. Stiffer blades for scraping, flexible blades for feathering joint compound. If you're heading into a [budget bathroom remodel](/blog/budget-bathroom-remodel-ideas/), these are the prep tools that determine how your paint looks in a year.

### 5-in-1 Tool — $12–$25

Opens paint cans, cleans roller covers, scrapes paint, gouges out old caulk, and hammers in nail pops. It's an ugly-looking tool that earns its drawer space within the first hour of use.

### Shop Vac — $50–$120

Not glamorous, but the tool you reach for every single session. A 6-gallon wet/dry shop vac like the Ridgid HD0600A (~$60) handles drywall dust, water from a burst line, spilled joint compound, and sawdust. Hook it up to your sander's dust port and the air quality on your jobsite is transformed. This is not optional equipment.

## Safety Gear — Don't Cut Corners Here

Hearing loss is permanent. Silicosis is permanent. A stray nail to the eye ends a project and your depth perception permanently. This gear costs $50 total and earns every cent.

### Ear Protection — $10–$30

Foam earplugs at NRR 33 are the most effective option and cost almost nothing. For extended work where you want to hear audio, the 3M WorkTunes over-ear muffs (~$30) let you listen while protecting your hearing. Run a circular saw or reciprocating saw without protection for a few sessions and you'll notice the ringing at night. Do it for years and it doesn't stop.

### Safety Glasses — $8–$25

Clear [ANSI Z87.1](https://www.ansi.org/)-rated glasses are the standard. Keep two pairs: clear for indoor work, tinted for outdoor. The DeWalt DPG82-11C at $12 fits over regular glasses and stays put under a face shield. Grinding, sawing, and nailing all throw debris — you don't need to get unlucky more than once.

### N95 Respirator — $20–$40 for a pack

If you're cutting drywall, sanding compound, disturbing old insulation, or working in any space that generates dust, an N95 is mandatory. Working in a pre-1978 house? Assume lead paint on anything painted before that year and treat the dust accordingly — the [EPA's lead safety guidelines](https://www.epa.gov/lead) cover testing kits and safe work practices. The 3M 8511 N95 is the comfortable standard. A dust mask is not a respirator and won't protect you.

## Specialty Tools — Rent These

For most homeowners, buying specialty tools for a single project is how you end up with a garage full of expensive equipment you use once a decade.

### Tile Wet Saw — Rent at $50–$80/day, Buy at $200–$600

A wet saw with a water-cooled diamond blade is mandatory for ceramic and porcelain tile. Score-and-snap cutters leave too many chips on porcelain for anything but the smallest backsplash. Rent unless you're regularly tiling. If you tile multiple rooms over multiple years, the QEP 650XT at ~$220 is the entry-level buy. For [bathroom tile installation costs](/blog/bathroom-tile-installation-cost/), the rental fee is a rounding error.

### Floor Scraper — Rent at $40/day

Removing old tile from a floor or wall by hand is a half-day of misery that an oscillating floor scraper handles in 30 minutes. Rent it for one-off projects; the time and fatigue savings pay for the rental in the first 20 minutes.

### Pressure Washer — Rent at $40–$70/day, Buy at $150–$400

Deck prep, siding cleaning, concrete before sealing — a pressure washer earns its keep on outdoor projects. For a single deck refinish, rent a gas model. If you'll use it at least twice a year, the Sun Joe SPX3000 electric (~$160) pays for itself within one season.

## What to Spend and What to Save On

Here's the honest breakdown:

| Kit Level | What's Included | Approximate Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Starter** | Tape measure, speed square, 4-ft level, stud finder, utility knife, hammer, pry bar, cordless drill combo, caulk gun, putty knife set, safety trio, shop vac | $400–$700 |
| **Full Kit** | Everything above, plus impact driver, circular saw, oscillating multi-tool, reciprocating saw, random orbital sander, work gloves, quality respirator | $1,200–$2,000 |
| **Specialty** | Tile wet saw, pressure washer, floor scraper — rent unless you need them repeatedly | $50–$80/day |

Buy in order of what you'll actually use. The drill, tape measure, speed square, and level get you through most early-stage renovation work. Add the circular saw when you need to cut sheet goods. Pick up the multi-tool when you hit your first tight-spot cut or grout job.

Watch for combo kit sales at Home Depot and Lowe's around Memorial Day and Labor Day — DeWalt and Milwaukee regularly discount battery platform bundles 30–40%. A two- or three-tool combo kit is almost always cheaper than buying the same tools separately.

The places not to save: the drill, the circular saw, and the level. Spend the extra $30 for quality on the tools you'll use every session. The mud pan and the 5-in-1? Save freely. You'll know exactly which is which after your first project — whether that's a [basement finish](/blog/cost-to-finish-a-basement/), a full deck build, or [a chain link fence installation](/blog/chain-link-fence-installation-costs-and-tips/).
