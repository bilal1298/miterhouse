Hiring a professional fence installer can mean the difference between a fence that lasts 25 years and one that starts leaning in 3. But the contractor selection process is where most homeowners go wrong — they get one or two quotes, pick the cheapest, and end up with post holes that are too shallow, concrete that was skipped, or a fence that sits a foot inside their property line.

Here is how to find, vet, and work with a fence installation professional so you get a quality result at a fair price.

## What Professional Fence Installation Costs

Costs vary widely by fence type, but here are realistic installed price ranges for the most common residential options:

| Fence Type | Cost Per Linear Foot (Installed) | 150 LF Total |
|---|---|---|
| Chain link (4 ft) | $15–$28 | $2,250–$4,200 |
| Chain link (6 ft, vinyl-coated) | $22–$38 | $3,300–$5,700 |
| Wood privacy (6 ft) | $25–$50 | $3,750–$7,500 |
| Vinyl/PVC (6 ft) | $28–$55 | $4,200–$8,250 |
| Ornamental iron/steel (4 ft) | $30–$65 | $4,500–$9,750 |
| Composite (6 ft) | $35–$70 | $5,250–$10,500 |

Gates add $200 to $800 each depending on width and type. Driveway gates with automation run $2,000 to $6,000 or more.

These ranges reflect standard conditions — relatively flat terrain, normal soil, clear fence line, and straightforward rectangular layouts. Complications push costs upward, sometimes significantly.

## Finding the Right Contractor

Skip the big home improvement store subcontractor programs. Those companies take 15 to 25 percent of the contract value as a referral fee, which means you either pay more or the installer cuts corners to protect their margin.

**Better sources for fence contractors:**

- Ask neighbors with fences you like who installed them. This is by far the best lead source — you can see the work aging in real time.
- Local fencing suppliers (not big box stores) maintain referral lists of contractors they work with regularly.
- Your municipal building department often has a list of licensed fence contractors who pull permits regularly.

**Get at least three quotes.** Not two. Three quotes give you enough data points to spot an outlier — whether it is suspiciously cheap or inflated. The middle quote is not automatically the right one, but it is usually the reality check.

## What to Look for in a Contractor

### Licensing and Insurance

Many states require fence contractors to hold a general contractor's license or a specialty fencing license. Verify through your state's licensing board website.

At minimum, confirm:
- **General liability insurance** (protects your property if they damage it).
- **Workers' compensation** (protects you if a worker is injured on your property).
- Ask for certificates of insurance, not just verbal confirmation. A legitimate contractor will produce these within 24 hours.

### Experience with Your Fence Type

A contractor who installs 200 wood privacy fences a year may have limited experience with ornamental iron or vinyl. Ask how many installations of your specific fence type they have completed in the last 12 months. Ask for 3 references for the same type — and actually call them.

### Written Proposal Detail

A professional quote should specify:
- **Fence type, height, style, and material brand** (not just "6-foot privacy fence").
- **Post material, diameter, and setting method** (concrete depth, hole diameter).
- **Post spacing** (8 feet is standard for wood, 10 feet for chain link — wider spacing is cutting corners).
- **Gate specifications** including hardware brand and type.
- **Permit responsibility** (the contractor should pull the permit).
- **Timeline** for completion.
- **Warranty** terms — workmanship and materials separately.
- **Cleanup and disposal** of old fencing if applicable.
- **What is NOT included** — this is where surprises hide. Survey, tree removal, grading, and permit fees are commonly excluded.

If a contractor gives you a verbal quote or a one-line written estimate, keep looking.

## Red Flags to Watch For

**Demanding full payment upfront.** A standard payment structure is 30 to 50 percent at contract signing (to cover materials) and the balance upon completion. Anyone asking for 100 percent upfront is either undercapitalized or planning to disappear.

**No permit discussion.** If the contractor does not ask about or mention permits, they are either planning to skip them or do not know local code. Both are problems.

**Abnormally low price.** If one quote comes in 40 percent below the other two, something is being left out. Common shortcuts: shallower post holes, no concrete, thinner-gauge materials, wider post spacing, or no warranty.

**No written warranty.** Reputable fence installers provide a 1- to 5-year workmanship warranty in writing. The materials typically carry a separate manufacturer warranty (15 to 25 years for most fencing products). No warranty means no recourse when the fence starts leaning 6 months later.

**Pressure to decide immediately.** "This price is only good today" is a sales tactic, not a business practice. A contractor with a full schedule and fair pricing does not need to pressure you.

## What to Expect During Installation

Understanding the process helps you evaluate the crew's work quality in real time.

**Day 0 (before work starts):** The contractor should call the utility locate service (811 in the US). This is free and legally required before any digging. Underground gas, electric, and cable lines are marked with paint or flags within 2 to 3 business days.

**Day 1: Layout and post setting.** The crew marks post locations per the approved plan, digs holes (power auger for most jobs), sets posts in concrete, plumbs them, and braces them. Corner, end, and gate posts go in first because they are structural anchors.

Posts should be set in concrete at least 24 to 36 inches deep, or below the frost line in cold climates. The concrete should be crowned (sloped away from the post) so water sheds off. Ask the crew what their post-depth standard is before they start — this is the single most important quality factor for fence longevity.

**Day 2+ (depending on scope):** After concrete cures (ideally 24 to 48 hours), rails and panels or pickets are installed, gates are hung and adjusted, and cleanup happens.

**Final walkthrough:** Walk the fence with the crew leader before final payment. Check:
- Posts are plumb (use your phone level app).
- Panel/picket alignment is consistent.
- Gates swing freely, self-close (if applicable), and latch securely.
- No concrete is left on surfaces, no debris remains.
- The fence follows the agreed-upon line (ideally marked by a survey).

## The Property Line Question

This is where fence projects create neighbor disputes. Unless you have a recent survey, your property line is an assumption. Fences installed even 6 inches past the property line can legally belong to your neighbor or require removal.

**Get a survey.** Yes, it costs $300 to $600. But it is far cheaper than moving a fence after the fact ($8 to $15 per linear foot for removal and reinstallation) or defending a property line dispute.

Most contractors install the fence 2 to 6 inches inside the surveyed property line to provide a clear buffer. This means the fence is entirely on your property with no ambiguity.

## Contracts and Protection

Before any work starts, have a written contract that includes:

- The detailed scope from the proposal.
- Start and completion dates.
- Payment schedule (never more than 50 percent upfront).
- Change order process (how additions or modifications are handled and priced).
- Warranty terms.
- Lien waiver language (confirms the contractor will not place a mechanic's lien on your property for their subcontractor or supplier disputes).

Keep copies of all permits, inspection records, and warranty documents. You will need them at resale or if a warranty claim arises years later.
