The contractor you hire for a kitchen and bathroom remodel will have more impact on the outcome than any material selection, design choice, or budget decision you make. A skilled, honest contractor with a $20,000 budget will deliver a better result than a mediocre one with $30,000. A bad contractor will cost you more than the price difference — in rework, delays, stress, and potentially legal fees.

Here are the specific red flags that predict problems, the green flags that indicate reliability, and the practical steps that protect you from the start.

## Red Flags That Should Stop You Cold

These are not minor concerns. Any one of these should eliminate a contractor from your consideration:

### No license or expired license

Every state requires general contractors to hold a license for residential remodeling above a certain dollar threshold. "I am between renewals" or "I do not need one for this size job" are excuses, not explanations. Verify the license number directly with your state's licensing board — do not rely on the contractor's website or business card.

An unlicensed contractor means:
- No regulatory oversight or complaint process
- Your homeowner's insurance may not cover damage caused by unlicensed work
- Permits cannot be pulled properly
- Resale inspectors will flag the work

### No written contract or vague contract

A legitimate contractor provides a detailed written agreement before starting. If they want to "just get started" with a handshake and work out the details later, walk away. Once demolition begins, you have zero leverage.

The contract must include: scope of work, materials specified by brand and model, total price, payment schedule, timeline, change order process, warranty terms, and dispute resolution.

### Demanding large upfront payments

Industry standard for a deposit is 10 to 20 percent of the total job cost, or enough to cover the first material order — whichever is less. A contractor asking for 50 percent or more upfront is either undercapitalized (using your money to finish someone else's job) or planning to disappear.

Payment should be tied to completed milestones: demolition complete, rough-in inspected, cabinets installed, project complete. Never pay ahead of completed work.

### No insurance or unverifiable insurance

Ask for a certificate of insurance showing general liability and workers' compensation. Then call the insurance company directly to verify the policy is active. A lapsed policy is the same as no policy — you are personally liable if a worker is injured in your home.

### Pressure to sign immediately

"This price is only good today" or "I have another client ready to sign" are sales tactics, not business practices. A professional contractor gives you time to review the quote, compare options, and ask questions. The [National Fire Protection Association's electrical code](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70) and [residential building code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) require specific work standards — your contractor should welcome your due diligence, not rush past it.

### Unusually low bids

If one bid is 30 percent or more below the others, something is wrong. The contractor is either:
- Omitting scope (you will get hit with change orders once demo starts)
- Planning to use substandard materials
- Underpaying subcontractors (who then cut corners or abandon the job)
- Desperate for work (which may indicate financial trouble or poor reputation)

## Green Flags That Indicate a Good Contractor

Look for these positive signals:

**They pull permits proactively.** A good contractor includes permit costs in the quote and handles the paperwork without being asked. They view inspections as quality assurance, not bureaucratic hassles.

**They have consistent subcontractors.** Ask who does their plumbing, electrical, and tile work. If they use the same subs on every job, those relationships produce consistent quality. If they hire whoever is cheapest and available, quality varies.

**They provide detailed, itemized quotes.** Every material specified by brand and model. Every labor phase broken out. Allowances minimized and clearly explained. This level of detail means they have actually thought through the job, not just ballparked it.

**They can show you recent similar projects.** Not stock photos or projects from five years ago. Recent kitchen and bathroom work in your area, with the option to speak with those homeowners.

**They push back on bad ideas.** A contractor who says "yes" to everything is not doing their job. You want someone who tells you "that backsplash material will not hold up behind a stove" or "that layout does not meet code for counter space." Expertise includes saying no.

**They communicate proactively.** Before you sign, note how responsive they are. If it takes four days to return a phone call during the sales phase — when they want your business — imagine how responsive they will be once they have your deposit.

## The Interview Process

When you meet with each of your three candidates (always get at least three quotes), ask these specific questions:

**"Walk me through how a typical kitchen and bath project goes, week by week."** Their answer reveals how much project management experience they have. Vague answers suggest they wing it.

**"What is your change order process?"** Changes happen on every project. A good contractor has a standard process: written change order, price and timeline impact documented, signed by both parties before work proceeds.

**"Who will be on site daily, and who is my point of contact?"** On larger companies, the person selling you the job may not be the person managing the construction. Know who you will actually communicate with day-to-day.

**"What happens if you go over the timeline?"** Listen for whether they take responsibility or blame external factors. A contractor who builds buffer time into the schedule is more realistic than one who promises aggressive timelines.

**"Can I see your insurance certificate and license?"** A legitimate contractor hands these over immediately. Hesitation is a red flag.

**"How do you handle the lead paint / asbestos question in older homes?"** If your home was built before 1978, renovation disturbs materials that may contain lead paint. A contractor who does not ask about the home's age or dismisses this concern is either ignorant of [EPA lead safety requirements](https://www.epa.gov/lead) or choosing to ignore them.

## Structuring the Contract

For a combined kitchen and bathroom remodel, your contract should include these specific protections:

**Detailed scope of work.** Room by room, phase by phase. Include what is NOT included — this prevents scope disputes later.

**Material specifications.** Not "granite countertops" but "2cm Bianco Romano granite, eased edge, from [specific supplier]." Vague specifications allow substitutions.

**Payment schedule with milestones:**
1. 10-15% deposit at contract signing
2. 20-25% when demolition is complete and rough-in begins
3. 20-25% when rough-in passes inspection
4. 20-25% when cabinets and major installations are complete
5. 10-15% at final completion and walkthrough
6. 5% retainage held for 30 days after completion

**Timeline with penalties.** Include a completion deadline and a per-day penalty for contractor-caused delays ($50-$150/day is common). Exceptions for weather, material delays, and homeowner-caused changes should be spelled out.

**Warranty.** Minimum 1 year on all labor. Material warranties pass through from manufacturers. The contractor should warrant their workmanship separate from product warranties.

**Lien waiver requirement.** Before each payment, the contractor provides lien waivers from themselves and all subcontractors for previously paid work. This protects you from a subcontractor placing a lien on your home if the general contractor does not pay them.

## During Construction: What to Watch

Stay engaged without micromanaging. Here are the things worth checking:

**Weekly progress photos.** Take photos of the work every few days, especially behind walls before they are closed up. These serve as documentation if disputes arise and as a record of where pipes and wires run for future reference.

**Inspection results.** Your contractor should schedule inspections at the required stages (rough-in, framing, final). Ask for copies of the inspection reports. If an inspection fails, understand why and verify the correction before work proceeds.

**Material deliveries.** When cabinets, countertops, or fixtures arrive, verify they match the specifications in your contract. Substitutions without your written approval are a contract violation.

**Subcontractor quality.** If the tile work looks uneven, the cabinet doors do not align, or the paint finish has drips — address it immediately, not at the final walkthrough. Problems are cheaper to fix during construction than after the job is "finished."

**Site cleanliness.** A contractor who leaves a job site messy at the end of each day is cutting corners in other places too. Dust barriers around the work area, protection on floors and fixtures outside the renovation zone, and daily debris removal are standard professional practice.

## Common Contract Disputes and How to Avoid Them

**Scope creep.** The kitchen demo reveals rotted subfloor under the sink. Is that included in the original scope? Define in the contract how "unforeseen conditions" are handled — typically as a documented change order with the homeowner's approval before proceeding.

**Material substitutions.** The specified tile is on 8-week backorder. The contractor suggests a "similar" alternative. The contract should require written approval for any substitution and a price adjustment if the alternative is less expensive.

**Timeline overruns.** Half of all kitchen and bath remodels run 1-3 weeks over the original estimate. Build this expectation into your planning, and make sure the contract addresses what happens when the timeline slips.

## After the Project

Your relationship with the contractor does not end at the final payment:

- **Test everything thoroughly** during the walkthrough. Open every drawer, run every faucet, test every outlet, cycle every appliance.
- **Create a punch list** in writing. Both parties sign it with a completion deadline (typically 2 weeks).
- **Collect all documentation:** permits, inspection reports, lien waivers, warranty information, paint colors and material specifications, appliance manuals.
- **Leave an honest review.** Good contractors depend on referrals. If they earned it, a detailed positive review helps them and helps the next homeowner.

The right contractor turns a stressful renovation into a manageable project with a great outcome. The wrong one turns it into a cautionary tale. Take the vetting process seriously — the time you spend choosing well is the best investment in the entire project.
