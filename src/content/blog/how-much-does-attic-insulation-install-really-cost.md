Installing attic insulation costs $1,500 to $3,500 for a professional job on a typical 1,000-square-foot attic, or $500 to $1,500 if you do the work yourself. The price depends primarily on which insulation type you choose and the R-value you need to achieve — both of which are determined by your climate zone and your attic's current condition, not by preference.

That range represents one of the highest-ROI home improvement investments available. The Department of Energy estimates that proper attic insulation reduces heating and cooling costs by 10 to 50 percent depending on the starting condition. On a $200 monthly utility bill, even a 15 percent reduction pays back a $2,000 installation within six years — and the insulation keeps performing for 20 to 80 years depending on type.

## Figuring Out What You Need

Before you price the job, you need two numbers: your target R-value and your current R-value. The difference between them determines how much insulation to install.

### Target R-Value by Climate Zone

The [Energy Star program](https://www.energystar.gov/) and the Department of Energy publish recommended R-values by zone:

- **Zone 1 (Southern Florida, Hawaii):** R-30 to R-49
- **Zones 2-3 (Gulf Coast, Desert Southwest, Deep South):** R-30 to R-60
- **Zone 4 (Mid-Atlantic, Tennessee, lower Midwest):** R-38 to R-60
- **Zones 5-7 (Northern US, upper Midwest, New England):** R-49 to R-60
- **Zone 8 (Northern Alaska):** R-49 to R-60

Most attic insulation professionals recommend targeting R-49 for zones 4 and above and R-38 for zones 2 and 3. Going beyond R-60 provides diminishing returns — the cost of additional material outweighs the energy savings.

### Measuring What You Have

Pull back a section of existing insulation at any accessible point and measure the depth from the top of the ceiling drywall to the top of the insulation. Then multiply by the R-value per inch for your insulation type:

- Fiberglass batts: R-3.0 to R-3.7 per inch
- Blown cellulose: R-3.2 to R-3.8 per inch
- Blown fiberglass: R-2.2 to R-2.7 per inch
- Spray foam (closed-cell): R-5.8 to R-6.5 per inch

If you have 6 inches of fiberglass batts, you have roughly R-19. To reach R-49, you need an additional R-30 — which translates to about 9 inches of blown cellulose on top of the existing batts.

## Cost by Insulation Type

### Blown-In Cellulose: The Value Pick

**Installed cost:** $0.80 to $1.50 per square foot  
**DIY cost:** $0.40 to $0.70 per square foot (machine rental typically free with 20+ bag purchase)  
**For 1,000 sq ft to R-49:** $800-$1,500 installed / $400-$700 DIY

Cellulose is ground-up recycled newspaper treated with borate for fire and pest resistance. It flows around wiring, plumbing, and irregular framing like water, filling every gap. That conformability is its biggest advantage over batt insulation — there are no seams, no voids, no gaps around obstacles.

The DIY approach is genuinely accessible. You rent a blowing machine (free with a minimum bag purchase at most home centers), feed bags into the hopper, and direct the hose across the attic floor. A helper keeps the machine fed while you direct the hose. Two people can insulate 1,000 square feet in 3 to 5 hours.

The catch: cellulose settles 15 to 20 percent over the first few years. Install to 15 to 20 percent above your target depth to compensate. If you need 14 inches settled depth, install to 17 inches.

### Blown-In Fiberglass: The Moisture-Resistant Option

**Installed cost:** $1.00 to $1.80 per square foot  
**DIY cost:** $0.50 to $0.90 per square foot  
**For 1,000 sq ft to R-49:** $1,000-$1,800 installed / $500-$900 DIY

Blown fiberglass shares the same installation process as cellulose — a machine blows it through a hose into the attic. The differences: fiberglass does not absorb moisture (cellulose can hold moisture if a roof leaks), it settles less over time (5 to 10 percent versus 15 to 20 percent for cellulose), and it provides slightly lower R-value per inch (roughly R-2.5 versus R-3.5).

The lower per-inch R-value means you need more material depth — about 20 inches of blown fiberglass to reach R-49, compared to 14 inches of cellulose. In attics with limited joist depth, that extra 6 inches matters.

### Fiberglass Batts: The Budget Option (With Caveats)

**Installed cost:** $1.00 to $2.20 per square foot  
**DIY cost:** $0.50 to $1.20 per square foot  
**For 1,000 sq ft to R-49:** $1,000-$2,200 installed / $500-$1,200 DIY

Batts are the rolls of pink or yellow insulation everyone recognizes. They come in standard widths (15 and 23 inches) to fit between framing spaced at 16 or 24 inches on center.

The problem: batts do not conform. Every wire, pipe, junction box, and irregular framing member creates a gap. Studies consistently show that batt installations perform 15 to 25 percent below their rated R-value because of these air gaps. In an attic full of wiring and plumbing penetrations, blown insulation provides meaningfully better real-world performance.

Batts make sense for new construction with clean, empty joist bays and standard spacing. For retrofit insulation — adding to what is already there, or insulating an existing attic — blown products are almost always the better choice.

### Spray Foam: The Premium Play

**Installed cost:** $1.50 to $3.50 per square foot (open cell) / $3.00 to $7.00 per square foot (closed cell)  
**DIY cost:** Not recommended (requires specialized equipment and training)  
**For 1,000 sq ft to R-49:** $3,000-$7,000 installed

Spray foam is the highest-performing option but rarely cost-effective for an attic floor — which is where most homeowners need insulation. Spray foam excels on the underside of the roof deck (converting an unconditioned attic to conditioned space) or in areas where air sealing and insulation must happen simultaneously.

For a standard unconditioned attic where you are insulating the floor, blown cellulose or fiberglass at one-third the cost provides comparable thermal performance.

## The Hidden Cost: Air Sealing

Here is the detail that determines whether your insulation investment pays off or underperforms: air sealing.

Before any insulation goes in, the air gaps between the conditioned house and the unconditioned attic must be sealed. These gaps exist at every penetration through the attic floor:

- Electrical wire holes and junction boxes
- Plumbing vent pipes
- Recessed light housings (these are major leakers)
- HVAC duct chases
- The top plates of interior walls
- The chimney chase (requires fire-rated sealant)

Air sealing costs $350 to $1,000 as a standalone service, or is included in many professional insulation installations. The [EPA's Energy Star program](https://www.energystar.gov/) estimates that air sealing alone can save 15 percent on heating and cooling costs — sometimes more than the insulation itself.

For DIYers, air sealing means spending 2 to 4 hours with cans of expanding foam, a caulk gun, and aluminum flashing before you start blowing insulation. Seal every hole, gap, and penetration you can find in the attic floor. It is tedious, unglamorous work — and it is the single most impactful thing you can do for your home's energy efficiency.

## DIY Installation: What It Actually Takes

### Equipment

- Insulation blowing machine (free rental with material purchase at most home improvement stores)
- Hose extensions (included with rental)
- N95 respirator (essential — not optional)
- Safety goggles
- Long sleeves and pants (insulation fibers irritate skin)
- Headlamp or portable work light
- Depth rulers (stake markers to gauge installed depth)
- Utility knife, caulk gun, and expanding foam for air sealing

### The Process

1. **Air seal first.** Every penetration, every gap, every accessible top plate.
2. **Install baffles at eaves.** Cardboard or foam baffles keep insulation from blocking soffit vents, maintaining the airflow path from soffit to ridge vent. This ventilation prevents moisture buildup and ice dams.
3. **Set depth markers.** Plant rulers or stakes at 4-foot intervals across the attic floor, marked at your target depth.
4. **Blow insulation.** Work from the far end of the attic toward the access hatch. Keep the hose end at a consistent height and sweep in overlapping passes. Do not pack the material down — its R-value depends on trapped air pockets.
5. **Check depth at markers.** Adjust as needed to achieve consistent coverage.

Total time for a 1,000-square-foot attic: 4 to 8 hours for air sealing, 3 to 5 hours for blowing. Two days of work for a DIYer.

## When to Hire a Professional

**Your attic has vermiculite insulation.** Vermiculite (small gray pebble-like granules) may contain asbestos from the Libby, Montana mine. Do not disturb it — have it tested before any work. The [EPA provides guidance on radon and asbestos-containing materials](https://www.epa.gov/radon) in older homes.

**The attic has active moisture or mold problems.** These need to be resolved before insulation. A professional can diagnose the moisture source — roof leak, inadequate ventilation, bathroom exhaust fan venting into the attic — and address it. Insulating over a moisture problem traps the moisture and [accelerates mold growth](https://www.epa.gov/mold).

**You need spray foam.** This requires specialized equipment and training. Improper application creates adhesion problems, off-gassing, and fire risk.

**Accessibility is extremely limited.** If your attic has tight clearances, no walkable path, or requires navigating around HVAC equipment, the physical difficulty and safety risk increase substantially.

## Return on Investment

Attic insulation consistently ranks as one of the highest-ROI home improvements:

- **Energy savings:** 10 to 50 percent reduction in heating and cooling costs
- **Payback period:** 2 to 6 years depending on current insulation level and energy costs
- **Home value:** Appraisers and buyers increasingly value energy efficiency features
- **Comfort:** Fewer drafts, more consistent temperatures room to room, reduced ice dams in cold climates

The investment makes sense whether you plan to stay in the house for decades or sell in a few years. Few home improvements pay for themselves this quickly and keep paying for this long.
