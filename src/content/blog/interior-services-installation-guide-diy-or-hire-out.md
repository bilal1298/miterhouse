Every interior renovation involves a mix of tasks — some you should absolutely do yourself, some are borderline, and some will cost you more in mistakes than hiring a professional. The trick is knowing which is which before you start ripping things apart. Here's a project-by-project breakdown of interior installation work, with honest assessments of difficulty, cost savings, and where DIY goes wrong.

## The DIY Decision Framework

Before each project, run through three questions:

1. **Does it require a permit or inspection?** If yes, check whether your jurisdiction allows homeowner permits or requires a licensed contractor. Most electrical and plumbing work falls here.
2. **What does a mistake cost?** A crooked shelf costs you an hour and two new holes. A botched tile shower costs $3,000–$8,000 to tear out and redo.
3. **Do I have (or can I rent) the right tools?** Skill can compensate for experience on many tasks. Lack of proper tools cannot.

## Project-by-Project Breakdown

### Painting — DIY Almost Always

**Difficulty:** 3/10
**Savings over hiring:** 60–75%
**Tools needed:** Roller, brush, tape, drop cloths ($40–$80 if starting from scratch)

Interior painting is the highest-ROI DIY skill. A professional painter charges $300–$800 per room. Your material cost for the same room is $60–$120 in paint and supplies.

**DIY confidently:** Walls, ceilings, trim, doors, closets
**Consider hiring for:** Very high ceilings (20+ feet), elaborate trim work, spray finishes on cabinets

The most common DIY painting mistake isn't technique — it's skipping prep. Patch every nail hole, sand every patch, prime every repair, and tape every edge. The painting itself is the easy part. Check our [room painting guide](/blog/how-to-paint-a-room-like-a-pro/) for the full process.

### Flooring — Depends on the Material

**Difficulty:** 4–8/10 depending on material
**Savings over hiring:** 40–60%

| Flooring Type | DIY Friendly? | Key Challenge |
|---|---|---|
| Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) | Yes — click-lock, no glue | Subfloor prep and undercutting door jambs |
| Laminate | Yes — similar to LVP | Expansion gaps and transition strips |
| Hardwood (engineered, floating) | Yes with patience | Acclimation time, staggering joints properly |
| Hardwood (nail-down) | Moderate — needs a floor nailer | Rental nailer ($60/day), learning curve on first row |
| Tile | Moderate to difficult | Layout planning, thin-set technique, grouting |
| Carpet | Hire out | Stretching requires a power stretcher and knee kicker — rental isn't worth the frustration |

For LVP and laminate, the labor savings are substantial — installers charge $3–$6 per square foot. A 300-square-foot room saves you $900–$1,800 in labor for a weekend of work.

Tile is where DIY gets risky. A bathroom floor done wrong means water damage behind walls within months. If you tile, invest in a quality waterproofing membrane and follow it exactly.

### Drywall — Hang It Yourself, Maybe Hire Finishing

**Difficulty:** Hanging 4/10, finishing 7/10
**Savings over hiring:** 30–50%

Hanging drywall is straightforward — measure, cut, screw to studs. A drywall lift ($40/day rental) makes ceiling sheets manageable solo.

Finishing (taping, mudding, sanding) is where skill separates professionals from homeowners. A bad mud job shows through every paint job for the life of the house. Level 4 or 5 finish requires three coats of mud, progressive sanding, and good lighting to spot imperfections.

**Practical approach:** Hang the drywall yourself and hire a taper/finisher. Hanging is $0.50–$1.00/sq ft of labor saved. Finishing is $0.75–$1.50/sq ft, but the quality difference is worth paying for on visible walls.

### Trim and Molding — Strong DIY Candidate

**Difficulty:** 5/10
**Savings over hiring:** 50–65%
**Tools needed:** Miter saw ($150–$300 or $50/day rental), nail gun ($100–$200 or $40/day rental), coping saw ($15)

Baseboards, crown molding, window casings, and door trim are excellent DIY projects. The work is visible and satisfying, and mistakes are fixable with caulk and wood filler.

**Tips that make the difference:**
- Cope inside corners instead of mitering — coped joints stay tight as the house settles
- Use a laser level for chair rails and picture molding
- Pre-prime trim before installing, then touch up nail holes after
- Invest 30 minutes learning to use a coping saw on scrap before starting

Crown molding in a 12x14 room: $80–$150 in materials DIY vs. $400–$800 installed by a carpenter. The miter saw pays for itself on the first room.

### Cabinet Installation — Moderate Difficulty

**Difficulty:** 6/10
**Savings over hiring:** 20–40%

Installing pre-built cabinets (from IKEA, Home Depot, or a cabinet shop) is well within DIY range with careful preparation. The critical steps:

1. Find and mark every stud — cabinets must be screwed into studs, not drywall anchors
2. Start with the upper cabinets (easier access before lowers are in the way)
3. Use a ledger board screwed to the wall at the correct height as a temporary support
4. Check level constantly — out-of-level cabinets cause cascading problems with doors, drawers, and countertops
5. Use cabinet screws (not drywall screws) through the back rail into studs

Where to hire out: custom cabinets that require on-site fitting, and countertop templating/installation (especially stone).

### Electrical — Know the Line

**Difficulty:** Varies wildly
**Savings over hiring:** 40–70% (when legal to DIY)

Per [OSHA guidelines](https://www.osha.gov/) and local building codes, electrical work has clear boundaries:

**DIY-safe (no permit usually needed):**
- Replacing outlets and switches (power OFF at breaker, verify with a non-contact tester)
- Swapping light fixtures
- Installing dimmer switches
- Adding a smart thermostat (low voltage)

**Permit required, homeowner may be allowed:**
- Adding outlets on an existing circuit
- Installing a new light fixture with a switch
- Upgrading to GFCI outlets in kitchens/bathrooms

**Hire a licensed electrician:**
- New circuits from the panel
- Panel upgrades (100A to 200A)
- Any work in the panel itself
- Knob-and-tube or aluminum wiring remediation
- Whole-house rewiring

For related electrical work, see our [240V breaker installation guide](/blog/installing-240v-breaker-what-to-know-first/) and our [smart home system guide](/blog/smart-home-system-installation-guide-diy-or-hire-out/).

### Plumbing — Similar Boundaries

**Difficulty:** Varies
**Savings over hiring:** 30–60%

**DIY-safe:**
- Replacing faucets and toilets
- Swapping shut-off valves
- Replacing a garbage disposal
- Installing a new showerhead
- Clearing drain clogs

**Hire a licensed plumber:**
- Moving drain or supply lines
- Installing new fixtures where none existed
- Gas line work (always)
- Water heater installation
- Anything behind finished walls

A faucet replacement saves you $150–$300 in labor for 30 minutes of work. A toilet swap saves $200–$400 for an hour of work. These are the highest-value DIY plumbing tasks. Check our [compression fitting guide](/blog/how-to-install-compression-fitting-the-right-way/) for pipe connection basics.

## Sequencing Interior Work

If you're renovating an entire room or floor, order matters:

1. **Demolition** — remove what's going
2. **Rough trades** — electrical, plumbing, HVAC (hire these out)
3. **Insulation** — if walls were opened
4. **Drywall** — hang and finish
5. **Prime walls and ceiling** — before trim goes up
6. **Install trim and molding** — baseboards go on after drywall, before flooring in most cases (or after, if you prefer)
7. **Paint** — walls, ceiling, and trim
8. **Flooring** — last to go down to protect from construction damage
9. **Fixtures** — outlets, switches, lights, plumbing fixtures
10. **Touch-up** — final caulk, paint touch-up, hardware

## Bottom Line

Most interior installation work is DIY-friendly if you're honest about where your skills end. Paint, flooring, trim, and basic fixture swaps offer the biggest savings for the least risk. Drywall finishing, tile, and cabinet installation are the middle ground — doable but quality-sensitive. Electrical and plumbing beyond basic swaps should go to licensed professionals. Start with the easy wins, build confidence, and work your way up to the harder projects over time.
