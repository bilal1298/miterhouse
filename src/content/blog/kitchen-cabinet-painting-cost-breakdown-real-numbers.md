Painting kitchen cabinets professionally costs $3,000 to $8,000 for a standard-sized kitchen, while doing it yourself runs $200 to $600 in materials alone. The catch is that DIY cabinet painting looks noticeably worse than professional work about 70% of the time — not because the paint is different, but because most homeowners underestimate how much prep work actually matters.

If your cabinets are structurally sound but cosmetically dated, painting them is one of the best returns on investment in a kitchen update. A full [kitchen remodel](/blog/federal-way-kitchen-remodel-what-homeowners-should-know/) can easily hit $25,000 to $50,000, while a quality cabinet paint job refreshes the entire look for a fraction of that.

## Professional Cabinet Painting Costs

### What Pros Charge

Professional cabinet painters typically price by the number of doors and drawers, with a base charge for the boxes (the cabinet frames themselves).

- **Per door/drawer face:** $40 to $100 each
- **Base cabinet boxes:** $1,000 to $2,000 for the full kitchen
- **Average kitchen (20–30 doors):** $3,000 to $6,000
- **Large kitchen (40+ doors):** $5,000 to $10,000
- **Premium finish (lacquer or conversion varnish):** Add 30% to 50%

These prices include removal of doors and hardware, cleaning, sanding, priming, two coats of paint, reinstallation, and new hardware holes if you are changing pulls.

### What Is Included (and What Is Not)

A good professional quote covers:
- Door and drawer removal
- Labeling and organizing all hardware
- Degreasing and sanding all surfaces
- Priming bare or stained wood
- Two coats of finish paint
- Reinstallation and alignment
- Touch-up visit after cure

Typically not included:
- New hardware (you buy it; they install it)
- Interior cabinet painting (adds 30% to 40%)
- Replacing damaged doors or drawer fronts
- Painting cabinet interiors

### Sprayed vs. Brushed and Rolled

This is the single biggest quality differentiator. Professional shops spray cabinet doors off-site using HVLP (high-volume, low-pressure) spray systems in a controlled environment. This produces a factory-smooth finish with no brush marks, drips, or orange peel texture.

On-site painting — where the painter works in your kitchen — uses a combination of spraying (for doors) and brushing/rolling (for boxes). The results are good but not as smooth as shop-sprayed finishes.

Shop-sprayed finishes cost $500 to $1,500 more than on-site work but look dramatically better and last longer. If you are paying for professional painting, the shop spray option is worth the premium.

## DIY Cabinet Painting Costs

Materials for painting your own cabinets:

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Deglosser/TSP cleaner | $10–$15 |
| Sandpaper (120 and 220 grit) | $15–$25 |
| Primer (2 gallons) | $40–$70 |
| Paint (2 gallons high-quality) | $80–$140 |
| Brushes, rollers, foam rollers | $25–$40 |
| Painter's tape | $15–$25 |
| Drop cloths | $10–$20 |
| **Total materials** | **$195–$335** |

If you rent or buy a small HVLP sprayer ($75 to $150 rental for a weekend, $300 to $500 to buy), your finish quality improves significantly.

### Realistic DIY Timeline

Here is what most weekend warriors do not account for — this project takes much longer than a weekend.

- **Day 1:** Remove all doors, drawers, and hardware. Label everything. Clean all surfaces with TSP or deglosser.
- **Day 2:** Sand all surfaces (doors, drawers, and boxes). Wipe clean. Tape off countertops, walls, and appliances.
- **Day 3:** Prime everything. Wait for cure (check the primer label — some need 24 hours).
- **Day 4:** Light sand the primer. Wipe clean. Apply first coat of paint.
- **Day 5:** Apply second coat of paint on boxes. First coat on door fronts.
- **Day 6:** Second coat on door fronts. First coat on door backs.
- **Day 7:** Second coat on door backs.
- **Days 8–14:** Allow to cure before reinstalling. Most paints need 7 to 14 days to fully harden.

That is two solid weeks from start to finish, including cure time. During this time, your kitchen has no cabinet doors and is covered in tape and drop cloths.

## Choosing the Right Paint

This is where many DIY projects fail. The wrong paint on kitchen cabinets chips, peels, and yellows within months.

**Best options for kitchen cabinets:**

- **Alkyd/oil-modified acrylic (Benjamin Moore Advance, Sherwin-Williams Emerald Urethane):** The current gold standard for DIY cabinet painting. Self-leveling, hard finish, excellent adhesion. Slow dry time (allow 16 to 24 hours between coats).
- **Acrylic-alkyd hybrids:** Combine the durability of alkyd with easier cleanup. These are what most professionals use for brush and roll applications.
- **Lacquer or conversion varnish:** Professional-only products sprayed in a booth. Hardest finish available, but toxic fumes require proper ventilation and PPE.

**Avoid:** Regular wall paint (latex or acrylic). Even premium wall paints are too soft for kitchen cabinets. They will stick to each other when doors are closed (a problem called "blocking") and show wear within a year.

## Prep Work: The 80% That Determines the Result

### Cleaning

Kitchen cabinets accumulate years of grease, especially above and beside the stove. Any grease left on the surface will prevent paint adhesion. Use TSP (trisodium phosphate) or a commercial degreaser, and scrub thoroughly. Pay special attention to the upper cabinets near the range hood.

### Sanding

You are not sanding to remove the old finish. You are sanding to create a rough surface for the primer to grip. Use 120-grit for the initial scuff, then 220-grit between coats.

If your cabinets have a factory lacquer or thermofoil finish, standard sanding may not be enough. A liquid deglosser applied before sanding helps break the surface tension.

### Lead Paint Concerns

If your home was built before 1978, your cabinets may have lead paint. The [EPA requires specific procedures for disturbing lead paint](https://www.epa.gov/lead), including containment, wet methods, and proper disposal. A lead test kit costs $10 to $30 and takes 30 minutes. Do this before you start sanding.

### Priming

Use a bonding primer — not the same primer you would use on drywall. Shellac-based primers (Zinsser BIN) offer the best adhesion to previously finished surfaces and block stains and odors. They dry fast, sand easily, and are the industry standard for cabinet prep.

Apply one coat of primer to every surface that will be painted. Let it cure completely (check the label), then light-sand with 220 grit before your first coat of paint.

## Color Selection

White and off-white remain the most popular choices, accounting for roughly 60% of cabinet painting projects. They brighten the kitchen, pair with any countertop and backsplash, and have the broadest appeal at resale.

That said, painted cabinets in deeper colors — navy, forest green, charcoal — are increasingly popular for islands and lower cabinets. The two-tone approach (light uppers, dark lowers or island) adds visual interest without the commitment of an all-dark kitchen.

A word of caution: dark colors show every imperfection in the finish. Brush marks, dust nibs, and orange peel that are invisible in white are glaringly obvious in charcoal or navy. If you are going dark, a sprayed finish is almost mandatory.

## When to Paint vs. When to Replace

Painting makes sense when:
- Cabinet boxes are structurally solid
- Doors are flat-panel, shaker, or raised-panel solid wood
- You like the layout and just want a fresh look
- Your budget is under $10,000

Replace instead when:
- Doors are warped, delaminating, or water-damaged
- Boxes are falling apart at the joints
- You want to change the door style entirely
- The layout needs reconfiguring

A middle option — replacing just the doors and drawer fronts while painting the boxes — runs $2,000 to $5,000 for new doors plus the painting cost. This gives you a new door style with a fresh color at roughly half the cost of full replacement.

## Hiring the Right Painter

Not all painters do cabinet work well. Look specifically for painters who specialize in cabinetry — they will have spray equipment, a shop for off-site work, and a portfolio of finished kitchens.

Questions to ask:
- Do you spray in a shop or paint on-site?
- What primer and paint products do you use?
- How many coats of finish do you apply?
- What is your warranty on peeling or chipping?
- Can I see three recent kitchen projects in person?

A [general contractor](/blog/hiring-a-general-kitchen-remodel-contractor-red-flags-and-tips/) may subcontract the painting, which adds a markup. Going directly to a cabinet painter saves 15% to 20%.

Get three quotes, ask to see the actual products they use (not just brand names — the specific product line matters), and choose based on quality of finish and warranty, not just price. A $4,000 paint job that chips in 6 months is no bargain over a $6,000 job that looks great for a decade.
