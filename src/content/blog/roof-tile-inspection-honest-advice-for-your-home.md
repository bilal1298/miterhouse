A professional roof tile inspection costs between $150 and $500 depending on your roof size, material, and location. It takes 1 to 3 hours for a thorough inspection and can save you thousands by catching problems before they turn into leaks, structural damage, or emergency repairs. If your tile roof is more than 10 years old and has never been inspected, schedule one now — it is the most cost-effective maintenance you can do.

This guide covers what inspectors actually check, what the common problems are, how much repairs cost when issues are found, and what you can safely check yourself between professional inspections.

## Why Tile Roofs Need Specific Inspection

Tile roofs — whether clay, concrete, or slate — are among the longest-lasting roofing materials available. A well-maintained clay tile roof can last 75 to 100 years. But "well-maintained" is the operative phrase. Tile roofs fail differently than asphalt shingle roofs, and the failure modes are not always visible from the ground.

The tiles themselves rarely wear out. What fails is everything around them: the underlayment beneath the tiles degrades, flashing corrodes, tiles shift from wind or foot traffic, and the battens or deck structure beneath the tiles can rot from trapped moisture. A professional inspection catches these hidden problems before they manifest as interior leaks.

## What a Professional Roof Tile Inspection Covers

A thorough inspection evaluates the entire roofing system, not just the visible tile surface.

### Tile Condition Assessment
The inspector checks every accessible tile for cracks, chips, displacement, and deterioration. Concrete tiles can develop surface erosion over time as the cement binder weathers. Clay tiles may crack from impact or freeze-thaw cycles. The inspector notes the percentage of tiles needing replacement and whether matching tiles are still available.

### Underlayment Evaluation
Beneath the tiles, a waterproof underlayment (typically felt paper or synthetic membrane) provides the actual waterproofing layer. Over 20 to 30 years, this underlayment degrades even under intact tiles. Inspectors check exposed sections at edges and penetrations, and may lift a few tiles in suspected problem areas to assess the underlayment condition directly.

### Flashing Inspection
Every roof penetration — chimneys, vents, skylights, walls — has flashing that seals the junction. Flashing is typically the first component to fail, even on a tile roof. The inspector checks for corrosion, separation, cracked sealant, and proper overlap. Flashing repair or replacement on a tile roof costs $200 to $800 per area.

### Ridge and Hip Tiles
The tiles along the ridge (peak) and hip (angled edge) lines are typically set in mortar. Over time, this mortar cracks and crumbles, allowing water to enter beneath the tiles. Re-pointing (applying new mortar) is a common maintenance need for tile roofs, costing $15 to $30 per linear foot.

### Drainage Assessment
The inspector evaluates whether water flows properly off the roof and into gutters. Debris accumulation in valleys, behind chimneys, or in low spots can dam water and force it under tiles. Proper drainage is critical for tile roofs because the tiles themselves are not waterproof — they shed bulk water by overlapping, but standing water defeats this system.

### Structural Check
The inspector looks for sagging roof planes, which can indicate failing support structure beneath the tiles. Tile roofs are heavy — clay tiles weigh 600 to 1,200 pounds per square (100 square feet), compared to 200 to 350 pounds for asphalt shingles. Any structural compromise is a serious concern with this weight load.

## Common Problems Found During Inspection

Based on what I have seen and what roofing professionals report, here are the most frequent findings and their typical repair costs.

**Cracked or broken tiles ($10–$50 per tile, plus labor).** Individual broken tiles are the easiest fix. The challenge is often finding matching replacement tiles, especially for discontinued styles. Keep spare tiles from your original installation if you have them.

**Deteriorated underlayment ($1,500–$5,000 to replace on a typical roof).** This is the big one. If the underlayment has failed under intact tiles, the entire roof section needs to be stripped — tiles carefully removed, underlayment replaced, tiles reinstalled. It is labor-intensive because each tile must be handled individually.

**Failed flashing ($200–$800 per location).** Flashing around chimneys, valleys, and wall-to-roof junctions is the most common leak source on tile roofs.

**Crumbling ridge mortar ($500–$2,000 for re-pointing the ridge line).** Mortar deterioration is normal over time and is a maintenance item rather than a failure. Budget for re-pointing every 15 to 25 years.

**Displaced tiles ($5–$15 per tile to reposition).** Wind, foot traffic (from satellite dish installers, HVAC techs, etc.), or animal activity can shift tiles out of position. Displaced tiles are an easy fix individually but indicate vulnerability if widespread.

## DIY Inspection: What You Can Check Safely

You do not need to wait for a professional to catch obvious problems. From the ground or a safe vantage point:

**Use binoculars.** Walk around your home and examine the roof surface with binoculars. You can spot cracked tiles, displaced tiles, and deteriorated mortar without climbing on the roof. Do this after every major storm.

**Check the attic.** Go into your attic during daylight. If you can see daylight through the roof, you have a problem. Look for water stains on the underside of the decking, wet insulation, or signs of mold. Check after heavy rain for active drips.

**Inspect gutters.** Tile fragments in your gutters indicate that tiles are deteriorating. Excessive debris from mortar crumbling is also a warning sign.

**Watch for displaced tiles.** From the ground, look for tiles that appear to be sitting differently than their neighbors — higher, lower, or at a different angle.

**Do not walk on a tile roof** unless you know exactly where to step. Stepping in the wrong spot cracks tiles. Professionals know to step on the lower third of each tile where it is supported by the tile beneath, and they use foam-padded shoes or walk boards.

## Inspection Frequency Recommendations

| Roof Age | Inspection Frequency |
|---|---|
| 0–10 years | Every 5 years (unless storm damage) |
| 10–25 years | Every 2–3 years |
| 25–40 years | Annually |
| 40+ years | Annually, plus underlayment assessment |

After any major storm event (hail, high winds, heavy snow), schedule an inspection regardless of the roof's age.

## Choosing an Inspector

For tile roof inspection specifically, look for inspectors or roofing contractors with experience in tile — not all roofers are familiar with tile systems. A contractor who primarily works with asphalt shingles may miss tile-specific issues or, worse, damage tiles during the inspection.

The [International Code Council](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) publishes roofing standards that govern tile installation and maintenance. An inspector familiar with these codes can tell you not just what is wrong, but whether the original installation met code — which matters for insurance claims and resale.

The [National Wood Flooring Association (NWFA)](https://www.nwfa.org/) is a resource for interior flooring, and while it does not cover roofing, the parallel inspection mindset applies — periodic professional evaluation catches problems early.

## Budgeting for Tile Roof Maintenance

A tile roof is a long-term investment that pays off through longevity — but only with regular maintenance. Budget annually for:

- Professional inspection: $150 to $500 every 1 to 3 years
- Minor repairs (cracked tiles, mortar touch-up): $200 to $500 per year on average
- Major maintenance (underlayment replacement, re-pointing): $2,000 to $8,000 every 15 to 25 years
- Gutter cleaning and valley clearing: $200 to $400 per year

Spread over a tile roof's 50-to-100-year lifespan, the average annual maintenance cost is modest compared to replacing an asphalt roof every 20 to 30 years. The key is consistent inspection that catches problems at the repair stage rather than the replacement stage.
