Getting quotes for a fence installation that you can actually compare requires preparation on your end. Most homeowners get three wildly different numbers from three different companies and have no idea which one represents the best value because the quotes are not apples-to-apples. The scope is different, the materials are described differently, and key details are missing from at least one of them.

Here is how to get useful fence installation quotes, read them correctly, and avoid the traps that cost homeowners money.

## Before You Call Anyone

Do your homework first. The 30 minutes you spend preparing saves hours of confusion later and produces significantly better quotes.

### Measure Your Property

Walk the fence line with a tape measure or a measuring wheel (available at any hardware store for $20 to $40). Record the total linear footage, the number of corners, any grade changes, and where you need gates.

You do not need to be precise to the inch. Fence companies will measure again during the site visit. But knowing your approximate footage prevents wildly inaccurate phone estimates and helps you spot errors in written quotes.

**A rough guide to lot sizes and typical fence footage:**

| Lot Size | Approximate Perimeter | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1/4 acre (typical suburb) | 400 - 500 linear feet | Minus the house footprint: 250 - 350 LF |
| 1/3 acre | 480 - 550 linear feet | Minus house: 300 - 400 LF |
| 1/2 acre | 590 - 660 linear feet | Minus house: 400 - 500 LF |
| 1 acre | 835 linear feet | Minus house: 650 - 750 LF |

Remember, you rarely fence the entire perimeter. Most residential fences cover the backyard and sometimes the side yards, not the front (which may be restricted by code or HOA rules anyway).

### Decide What You Want

Before soliciting quotes, decide on these basics:

- **Fence type:** Privacy, semi-privacy, picket, or chain link
- **Height:** 4 feet (common for front/decorative) or 6 feet (common for back/privacy)
- **Material:** Wood (cedar, pressure-treated pine), vinyl, chain link, aluminum, composite
- **Gates:** Number, width, and type (walk-through, double-drive for vehicle access)
- **Purpose:** Pet containment, privacy, security, decorative, or a combination

The more specific you are, the more comparable your quotes will be. Saying "I want a 6-foot cedar privacy fence with dog-ear pickets, board-on-board construction, 4x4 posts set 30 inches deep, and two 4-foot walk-through gates" gives every company the same target. Saying "I want a fence in my backyard" invites three different interpretations.

## How Many Quotes to Get

**Three is the minimum. Five is ideal for jobs over $5,000.**

One quote tells you nothing about the market. Two quotes let you see a range but might both be outliers. Three quotes give you a reliable sense of the going rate. Five quotes on larger jobs help you spot both bargains and price-gouging.

### Where to Find Fence Companies

**Local referrals** are the strongest lead source. Ask neighbors who recently installed fences. Drive around your neighborhood and photograph fences you like --- then knock on the door and ask who built it. People love talking about their fence projects.

**Online directories** (Google, Yelp, Angi, HomeAdvisor) are useful for identifying companies you might not hear about through word of mouth. Look for companies with at least 20 reviews and a 4.0+ rating.

**Lumber yards.** Local lumber yards often know the best fence contractors in the area because those contractors buy materials from them regularly. This is an underused resource.

**Big-box store installation services** (Lowe's, Home Depot) subcontract to local installers. The pricing is typically 10 to 20 percent higher than going direct because the retailer takes a commission. But they do offer financing options and a standardized warranty.

## Reading a Fence Quote

A good fence quote contains specific, verifiable details. Here is what to look for and what to question.

### What Should Be in the Quote

**Materials specification:**
- Fence style and height
- Wood species and grade (or vinyl brand/model)
- Post material, dimensions, and length
- Rail dimensions
- Picket dimensions and style (dog-ear, flat-top, shadowbox)
- Fastener type (galvanized nails, stainless steel screws)

**Construction details:**
- Post spacing (should be 6 to 8 feet on center; wider than 8 feet is a red flag)
- Post depth (minimum 1/3 of total post length)
- Post setting method (concrete, dry-set concrete, gravel-and-compact)
- Number of rails (2-rail for 4 ft fence, 3-rail for 6 ft)

**Scope of work:**
- Old fence removal (yes/no, and disposal)
- Grading or site clearing
- Utility locating (811 call)
- Permit procurement
- Number and type of gates
- Post caps or other finishing details

**Business terms:**
- Total price (itemized is better)
- Payment schedule
- Start date and estimated completion
- Warranty terms (labor and materials separately)
- Permit responsibility

### Red Flags in a Quote

**Single-line pricing.** "6 ft wood fence, 300 LF --- $8,500" tells you nothing about what you are getting.

**No material specification.** "Wood fence" could mean anything from premium cedar to the cheapest grade of pressure-treated pine. The cost difference is 40 to 60 percent.

**"Per post" pricing without total.** Some contractors quote by the post ($150 to $250 per post) without clearly stating how many posts the job requires. Do the math yourself.

**No mention of concrete.** Every post should be set in concrete (or at minimum, compacted gravel in very dry climates). If the quote does not mention it, ask.

**Abnormally low price.** If one quote is 40 percent below the others, something is being cut. Common shortcuts: thinner posts, wider spacing, shallow post holes, no concrete, lower-grade materials.

## Typical Quote Ranges by Region

These numbers represent all-in professional installation costs for 200 linear feet of 6-foot wood privacy fence, including materials, labor, two gates, and standard site prep.

| Region | Low End | Average | High End |
|---|---|---|---|
| Northeast | $4,000 | $5,500 | $8,000 |
| Southeast | $3,000 | $4,200 | $6,000 |
| Midwest | $3,200 | $4,500 | $6,500 |
| Southwest | $3,500 | $4,800 | $7,000 |
| West Coast | $4,500 | $6,000 | $9,000 |

These ranges reflect 2026 pricing. Material costs (especially lumber) fluctuate seasonally and year to year. Ask for a price-lock period in the contract (60 to 90 days is standard).

## Negotiating Your Quote

Fence quotes are negotiable. Most companies have 10 to 20 percent margin built into their pricing. Here are effective negotiation strategies.

**Time your request.** Late fall and winter are slow seasons for fence companies. You are more likely to get competitive pricing from October through February than during the spring and summer rush.

**Bundle services.** If you need old fence removal, ask for a package price. It usually comes out cheaper than line-item pricing.

**Offer flexible scheduling.** Telling a contractor "I'm flexible on the start date --- just fit me in when you have a gap" makes you an easy customer. Contractors reward easy customers with better pricing.

**Pay in cash or check.** Credit card processing costs companies 2 to 3 percent. Some offer a corresponding discount for check or cash payment.

**Get a neighbor on board.** If your neighbor also needs fencing, a shared project along the property line reduces cost for both of you. The fence company gets a bigger job with minimal extra mobilization.

**Do not negotiate quality.** Pushing for a lower price by accepting thinner posts, wider spacing, or cheaper materials is false economy. Those shortcuts show up within 3 to 5 years as leaning posts, sagging panels, and premature rot.

## After Choosing a Contractor

Once you have selected a company, take these final steps before work begins:

1. **Sign a detailed contract** that matches the quote. Do not accept verbal changes.
2. **Verify the permit** is pulled and approved before the crew shows up.
3. **Confirm 811 has been called** and the yard is marked.
4. **Clear the fence line** of furniture, planters, toys, and anything the crew would need to move.
5. **Inform neighbors** about the project timeline.
6. **Document the yard condition** with photos before work begins. This protects both you and the contractor if there are disputes about landscaping damage.

According to the [American Wood Council](https://www.awc.org/) and [ICC building standards](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/), proper post depth and concrete volume are the two factors that most affect long-term fence stability. Whichever contractor you choose, make sure those details are specified and followed. Everything else can be fixed; post setting cannot.
