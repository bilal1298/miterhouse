Installing laminate flooring yourself costs $1 to $4 per square foot in materials alone — add $1.50 to $3 per square foot if you hire a professional installer. For a typical 250-square-foot room, that's $250 to $1,000 for DIY or $625 to $1,750 with professional labor. Laminate is one of the few flooring types where DIY is genuinely practical for first-timers, making it a strong choice when budget matters.

Here's the full picture of what installation involves, what it costs at every step, and where the money-saving opportunities actually are.

## Step-by-Step Installation Process and Costs

### Step 1: Measure and Order Materials

Measure each room's length and width. Multiply for square footage. Add 10% for cuts and waste (15% for rooms with many angles or closets).

**Material cost per square foot:**
- Budget laminate (6-7mm): $0.70–$1.50
- Mid-range laminate (8-10mm): $1.50–$3.50
- Premium laminate (12mm): $3.50–$6.00
- Underlayment (if not attached): $0.25–$0.75

**Pro tip:** Order all rooms at once from the same lot number. Laminate batches can have slight color variations. Mixing lots shows in natural light, especially at transition points between rooms.

### Step 2: Prep the Room

Clear all furniture. Remove existing baseboards (label them on the back for easy reinstallation). Pull up existing flooring if necessary.

**Old flooring removal costs:**
- Carpet and pad: $0.50–$1.00/sq ft (easy DIY — 1 to 2 hours per room)
- Sheet vinyl: $0.75–$1.50/sq ft
- Tile: $2.00–$4.00/sq ft (heavy, dusty, loud — most people hire this out)

If your home was built before 1980, old vinyl flooring or adhesive may contain [asbestos](https://www.epa.gov/asbestos). Get it tested before disturbing it. Asbestos abatement costs $1,500 to $3,000 or more, but the health risk of improper removal is serious.

### Step 3: Prepare the Subfloor

This step determines whether your floor looks professional or amateur. Do not rush it.

**Checking flatness:** Lay a 6-foot straightedge across the subfloor in multiple directions. Mark any spots where the gap underneath exceeds 3/16 inch. These need fixing.

**Fixing low spots:** Floor-leveling compound costs $25 to $40 per bag and covers roughly 20 square feet at 1/8-inch depth. Mix, pour, spread with a trowel, and let self-level. Cure time is typically 4 to 6 hours.

**Fixing high spots:** Sand plywood with a belt sander. Grind concrete with a concrete grinder (rent for $50–$75/day). Both create significant dust — use proper dust protection.

**Fixing squeaks:** Drive 2-1/2-inch screws through the subfloor into joists at every squeak. Walk the entire room systematically. This costs $10 to $20 in screws and takes under an hour. It's the cheapest upgrade you'll ever do on a floor project.

For concrete subfloors, check the [IRC building code requirements](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) for moisture testing protocols. Most laminate manufacturers require a calcium chloride moisture test showing results below 3 lbs/1,000 sq ft/24 hours.

### Step 4: Install Underlayment

If your laminate doesn't have pre-attached underlayment, roll out your chosen pad across the entire floor. Butt edges together without overlapping (overlaps create high spots). Tape seams with the manufacturer's recommended tape.

On concrete subfloors, use underlayment with a built-in vapor barrier, or lay 6-mil polyethylene sheeting first with 6-inch overlaps at seams.

**Time:** 30 to 60 minutes for a standard room.

### Step 5: Plan Your Layout

Before clicking anything together, plan your starting point and direction:

**Direction:** Run planks parallel to the longest wall or toward the main light source. This creates the most visually appealing result.

**First row width:** Measure the room width and divide by your plank width. If the last row would be less than 2 inches wide, rip the first row narrower to balance both sides. A 1-inch sliver of laminate at the far wall looks terrible.

**Stagger pattern:** Mark the starting length for each row's first plank. Stagger end joints by at least 8 inches (12 inches is better) from row to row. Avoid a repeating step pattern.

**Dry-lay the first two rows** to confirm your plan works before committing.

### Step 6: Install Planks Row by Row

**First row:** Place spacers (1/4 inch) against the starting wall. Lay the first row with the tongue facing the room and the groove against the wall. Click end joints together as you go.

**Subsequent rows:** Angle the long edge of the new plank into the installed row at roughly 20 degrees and press down to click. Once the long edge is engaged, click the end joint to the adjacent plank. Work from left to right (or right to left — just be consistent).

**Last row:** Measure the remaining width, subtract 1/4 inch for the expansion gap, rip planks to width on a table saw or with a circular saw and guide. Use a pull bar to click the last row into place.

**Cutting tips:**
- Crosscuts for length: Miter saw or circular saw
- Rip cuts for width: Table saw is easiest; circular saw with a straightedge guide works
- Irregular cuts around pipes, vents, and doorways: Jigsaw
- Undercutting door casings: Oscillating tool with a flush-cut blade

**Time estimate:** 4 to 8 hours for a 250-square-foot room, depending on experience.

### Step 7: Install Transitions and Trim

Add transition strips at every doorway where laminate meets another surface. Screw the track into the subfloor, snap the cover on.

Reinstall baseboards (or install new ones) to cover the expansion gaps at walls. Use a finish nailer or construction adhesive. If gaps remain between baseboard and floor, add quarter-round shoe molding.

Paint or stain new trim before installation to save time and reduce mess. Touch up nail holes after with a matching filler.

## Professional Installation: What You're Paying For

Hiring a pro for laminate installation costs $1.50 to $3.00/sq ft in labor. That buys you:

**Speed:** A two-person crew installs 400 to 600 square feet per day. That same area takes a solo DIYer 2 to 3 days.

**Precision:** Experienced installers make cleaner cuts, tighter joints, and handle complex areas (around kitchen islands, at fireplace hearths, where rooms meet at odd angles) that challenge first-timers.

**Warranty:** Many manufacturers require professional installation to honor product warranties. If your laminate costs $3+/sq ft and has a 25-year warranty, professional installation protects that investment.

**Problem handling:** Experienced installers have seen every subfloor issue. They know immediately whether a soft spot needs plywood replacement or just more screws, and they can fix it on the spot.

## Room-Specific Considerations

### Living Rooms and Hallways
High traffic demands AC4 or higher rated laminate. Thicker products (10mm+) feel more solid in these areas and resist denting from dropped objects better. Expect to spend $2.50 to $4.50/sq ft on materials for spaces that need to hold up.

### Bedrooms
Lower traffic means you can prioritize appearance over durability. AC3 rated laminate in the $1.50 to $2.50 range performs well for decades in bedrooms. This is where cost savings make the most sense.

### Basements
Moisture is the primary concern. Even "waterproof" laminate can fail if moisture migrates up through a concrete slab. Install a moisture barrier underlayment, test the slab for moisture, and ensure your sump pump works. For basements with any history of water, [LVP is a safer choice](/blog/low-flooring-installation-pricing-guide-for-homeowners/).

### Kitchens
Laminate in kitchens is a debated choice. Water-resistant laminate handles normal spills if cleaned promptly. But a dishwasher leak or refrigerator water line failure can destroy laminate flooring quickly. If you choose laminate for a kitchen, use a product specifically rated for kitchen use with waterproof core technology.

## Saving Money on Laminate Installation

**Strategy 1:** Buy materials from the cheapest source, hire labor-only installation. Many independent installers charge $1 to $2/sq ft for labor-only jobs — less than contractors who mark up materials.

**Strategy 2:** Do removal and prep yourself, hire the laminate installation only. Removing old carpet takes no skill. Leveling and squeak-fixing are straightforward. Paying a pro only for the laminate portion saves $0.50 to $2.00/sq ft.

**Strategy 3:** Buy during clearance events. The [National Wood Flooring Association](https://www.nwfa.org/) reports that January and holiday weekends offer the deepest discounts. Discontinued colors in quality lines sell at 40–60% off.

**Strategy 4:** Do the entire installation yourself. The tools cost $100 to $200 total (or $50/day to rent). For a 300-square-foot room, DIY saves $450 to $900 in labor. That's a strong return on a weekend's work.

The bottom line: laminate installation is one of the best-value flooring projects available. Whether you DIY or hire out, the per-square-foot cost delivers a hardwood appearance that holds up well in dry, climate-controlled rooms.
