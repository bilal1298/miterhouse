Most plumbing disasters didn't start as emergencies—they started as someone deciding a job was simpler than it was.

That's the thing about plumbing: the easy jobs are genuinely easy, and the hard jobs are genuinely dangerous. The mistake is misclassifying which is which. Get that right and you'll save a few hundred dollars a year on simple repairs without ever putting yourself in the category of homeowners who flooded a bathroom trying to move a drain line.

Here's a clear-eyed breakdown of where the line is and why it matters.

---

## The Three Questions That Govern Every Decision

Before you pick up a wrench or reach for your phone, ask yourself three things:

**1. What happens if this fails?** A dripping faucet wastes water and annoys you. A bad sewer connection can contaminate your yard, rot your subfloor, or come up in a future buyer's inspection report as unpermitted work. The downside asymmetry between "easy" and "hard" plumbing jobs is severe.

**2. Can you undo it if something goes wrong?** Replacing a faucet is reversible. Cutting into a drain-waste-vent stack is not. If you can't walk back a mistake before it becomes a bigger problem, that's a strong signal to call a pro.

**3. Does this job require a permit?** Water heater replacements, drain relocations, and repiping almost always require permits in the US. Unpermitted work doesn't just risk fines—it can invalidate your homeowner's insurance on a related claim and complicate a sale years later.

If any of those three questions makes you hesitate, keep reading before you start.

## Jobs You Can Safely Do Yourself

### Faucet Replacement

Swapping a kitchen or bathroom faucet is beginner work. Shut off the supply valves under the sink, disconnect the supply lines, remove the old faucet, drop in the new one. The only tool you might not own is a basin wrench—a $20 tool that reaches the mounting nuts behind the sink deck. Pick up a Ridgid 2006 or the Husky 14-inch version; either will handle 95% of installs.

**DIY cost:** $50–$300 for the faucet, $0–$20 for supply lines if yours are old.
**Plumber cost:** $150–$350 labor, plus parts.
**Time:** 1–2 hours.

The one gotcha: corroded shutoff valves that haven't moved in 15 years. Turn them gently—if they crumble or leak when you test them, replace the valve at the same time (covered below). Don't force a brittle valve or you'll create a bigger problem than a leaky faucet.

### Clearing Clogged Drains

A slow or blocked drain is almost always a DIY fix. Your first tool should be a hand auger (drain snake)—a $25–$35 Ridgid K-3 or equivalent will clear the overwhelming majority of hair, grease, and soap clogs in sinks, tubs, and showers. For toilets, use a flange plunger with the fold-out cup, not a standard cup plunger which can't seal properly.

Skip the chemical drain cleaners as a first move. Products like Drano work on soft clogs but are hard on older pipes and destroy the rubber gaskets in P-traps over time. A snake is faster and cleaner with zero chemistry to deal with.

**DIY cost:** $0–$35.
**Plumber cost:** $100–$250 for standard clearing.

One caveat: if you're snaking the same drain every couple of months, you have a venting problem or a deeper partial blockage, not a simple clog. At that point, a camera inspection from a plumber is worth the $150–$200 it costs—you'll know exactly what you're dealing with.

### Replacing Shutoff Valves

The quarter-turn valves under your sinks and behind toilets wear out. They stick, they corrode, they drip. Replacing them is a solid DIY job now that compression-fit valves have made soldering optional.

Turn off the main water supply to the house, drain the line, remove the old valve, and install a new SharkBite or similar push-to-connect fitting if you want zero-solder work. A Watts 3/8-inch compression angle stop runs about $12 at any hardware store and takes 20 minutes to install.

**DIY cost:** $8–$25 per valve.
**Plumber cost:** $75–$200 per valve installed.

### Toilet Replacement

This one looks harder than it is. The whole job comes down to one measurement: the rough-in distance, which is the distance from the wall to the center of the drain flange—typically 12 inches, sometimes 10 or 14. Measure before you buy, then match it exactly on the new toilet's spec sheet. Mismatching rough-in dimensions is the most common and expensive beginner mistake.

Use a new wax ring every time. A Fluidmaster 7530 ring works for standard flanges; if your flange sits below the finished floor, go with a double-thickness ring or a foam gasket ring with an extension. Tighten the flange bolts slowly and evenly—alternating sides—until the toilet doesn't rock. Over-tightening cracks the base.

**DIY cost:** $150–$600 for the toilet, $10–$20 in hardware.
**Plumber cost:** $200–$400 labor on top of fixture cost.

### Showerhead Swap

The simplest job on this list. Unscrew the old head counterclockwise, clean the threads on the shower arm, wrap two layers of Teflon tape clockwise, thread on the new head, and hand-tighten plus a half-turn with channel-locks. If you see a drip at the connection, a quarter-turn more usually stops it.

**DIY cost:** $25–$300 depending on the head.
**Plumber cost:** Not worth calling one for this job.

## Jobs That Need a Licensed Plumber

### Sewer Line Blockages and Damage

When multiple drains throughout the house back up at the same time, or you're seeing sewage come up in your lowest fixtures, you're dealing with the main sewer line. This isn't a hand-auger situation. Power augers and hydro-jetting machines—the tools that actually clear a main line—cost $500–$1,500 to rent and require real experience to use without damaging the pipe. And if the line has root intrusion or a collapsed section, you won't know until a plumber runs a camera through it.

**Plumber cost:** $150–$500 for clearing; $3,000–$10,000+ if excavation or trenchless repair is needed.

If you get a 2 a.m. sewage backup and can't wait, read through the options in this breakdown of [24 hour plumbing repair](/blog/24-hour-plumbing-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/) before you make a call you'll regret.

### Water Heater Replacement

A tank water heater involves gas or 240V electrical connections, a temperature-pressure relief valve, correct venting, and permit-required work in most US jurisdictions. Gas connections especially: an improperly installed fitting is a carbon monoxide and explosion risk. This is not where you learn on the job.

If your water heater is failing and you're wondering whether you can nurse it through the night, the honest answer depends on the failure mode—a full breakdown of when to act is in this piece on [24 hour water heater repair](/blog/24-hour-water-heater-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/).

**Plumber cost:** $300–$600 labor for a standard tank swap, plus the unit ($400–$1,200).

### Gas Line Work

Don't touch gas lines. Any modification, extension, or repair requires a licensed plumber or gas fitter. There's no exception and no middle ground here.

### Moving or Adding Drain Lines

This is what catches DIYers during bathroom renovations. Moving a toilet 12 inches, adding a second sink, or relocating a shower drain all involve cutting into the drain-waste-vent system—a network that requires a precise 1/4-inch-per-foot slope, proper venting, and permits in almost every municipality.

If you're budgeting a [DIY bathroom remodel](/blog/diy-bathroom-remodel-cost/) and considering repositioning fixtures, rough-in costs are where the budget typically blows past estimates. The fixture itself is the visible line item; the drain relocation underneath it is where the real money goes. A more complete look at what you're getting into is in this [bathroom remodel cost breakdown](/blog/bathroom-remodel-cost-breakdown/).

**Plumber cost:** $500–$3,000+ depending on what's involved.

### Repiping

Galvanized steel pipes from the 1960s–70s corrode from the inside out. Polybutylene—the gray plastic piping installed through the mid-1990s—is known to fail without warning. If you have either, repiping is coming. It means opening walls, running new copper or PEX supply lines throughout the house, pulling permits, and scheduling inspections. This is a three-to-five-day project minimum in most homes.

**Plumber cost:** $4,000–$15,000 depending on home size and access.

## What to Look For Before You Call Anyone

Certain problems tell you a plumber visit is coming whether you like it or not:

**Wet spots on ceilings or walls.** The stain is never where the leak is. A professional diagnosis is worth the service call before you open drywall in the wrong place.

**Low pressure throughout the house.** If it's not a city-side issue, this often signals a failing pressure regulator, a partially closed main, or the beginning of mineral buildup in aging galvanized supply lines.

**Gurgling when you flush.** A sink that gurgles when you flush a toilet is a venting problem in the drain-waste-vent system. Not an emergency, but it won't fix itself and can develop into a sewage backup.

**Water that smells like sulfur or runs brown.** The sulfur smell usually means the anode rod in your water heater is spent. Brown water from a cold tap often means galvanized pipe corrosion—time to get a plumber's opinion on whether you're early or late in the repiping conversation.

## How to Pick a Good Plumber

When you do need a pro, the quality gap between plumbers is wide. A few things worth doing:

**Verify the license.** Every state licenses plumbers. Ask for their license number and check it on your state's contractor licensing board website. They should also carry general liability insurance and workers' comp—ask for proof before anyone starts work.

**Get three quotes for anything over $500.** For a water heater, sewer job, or larger renovation, three quotes will show you the range. A bid that's dramatically lower usually means no permit, cut-rate materials, or both.

**Ask specifically about permits.** A plumber who says "we don't need a permit for that" on a job that clearly requires one is telling you something important about how they work. Walk away.

**Don't accept phone quotes for complex work.** A serious plumber needs to see the job before pricing it. Any firm number for a sewer replacement or repipe given over the phone is a number that will change on-site.

## The Honest Bottom Line

Plumbing is a good area for homeowner DIY. The basic jobs are genuinely learnable, the tools are cheap, and the savings on simple repairs are real. A faucet swap, a toilet replacement, clearing a drain—you can handle all of those with the right tool and an afternoon.

The jobs above that line sit well above it. There's no partial credit: a pipe either holds pressure or it doesn't, a drain either has the right slope or it doesn't, a gas connection either seals or it doesn't. If you're unsure where a job falls, the cost of a professional assessment is almost always lower than the cost of finding out the hard way.
