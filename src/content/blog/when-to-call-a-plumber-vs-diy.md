If a faucet is dripping or a drain is slow, you can almost certainly fix it yourself for under $20. If your sewer line is backing up or you need to move a drain, put the wrench down and call a licensed plumber—the cost of getting it wrong far exceeds the cost of the service call.

That's the short version. The longer version involves knowing exactly where that line sits, because it moves depending on the task, your skill level, and what's actually at stake if something goes sideways.

## The Core Question: What Are You Risking?

Every plumbing decision comes down to three factors: what happens if it fails, how hard it is to undo, and whether a permit is required. A leaking faucet wastes water and annoys you. A botched sewer connection can contaminate your yard, rot your subfloor, or get your house flagged during a future sale inspection. The asymmetry matters.

The sweet spot for DIY plumbing is anything that's fully above-ground, uses existing connections, doesn't require opening walls, and doesn't involve gas or the main supply line. Most of those jobs take an afternoon and a trip to the hardware store.

## Jobs You Can Safely DIY

### Replacing a Faucet

Swapping a kitchen or bathroom faucet is beginner-level work. Shut off the supply valves under the sink, disconnect the supply lines, remove the old faucet, drop in the new one, reconnect the lines. You'll need a basin wrench for the mounting nuts if the space is tight.

**DIY cost:** $50–$300 for the faucet itself, plus $0–$20 in supply lines if yours are old.  
**Plumber cost:** $150–$350 labor, plus parts.  
**Time:** 1–2 hours.

The only real gotcha here is corroded shutoff valves that won't turn. If yours haven't moved in 20 years and they crumble when you try, that's a sign you may need to replace them at the same time—which is still a DIY job, just slightly more involved.

### Unclogging Drains

A slow or blocked drain is almost always a DIY fix. Start with a drain snake (also called a hand auger)—a $25–$35 tool that will clear the vast majority of hair, grease, and soap clogs in sinks, tubs, and showers. For toilets, a flange plunger with a fold-out cup creates a proper seal.

Avoid chemical drain cleaners as a first resort. They work on soft clogs but can damage older pipes and eat through the rubber gaskets in P-traps. A snake is faster and doesn't leave a chemical problem to deal with later.

**DIY cost:** $0–$35 (snake rental or purchase).  
**Plumber cost:** $100–$250 for a standard drain clearing.

If you're snaking the same drain every three months, that's a sign of a deeper blockage or a venting problem—worth a professional camera inspection at that point.

### Replacing Shutoff Valves

The little valves under your sinks and behind your toilets wear out. They corrode, they stick, they drip. Replacing them is a legitimate DIY job if you're comfortable turning off the main water supply to the house, cutting out the old valve, and sweating or compression-fitting a new one in.

Compression-fit valves (no soldering required) have made this much more accessible. You turn off the main, drain the line, remove the old valve, slip on the new compression fitting, and tighten. Done.

**DIY cost:** $8–$25 per valve.  
**Plumber cost:** $75–$200 per valve installed.

### Toilet Replacement

Replacing a toilet is one of the more satisfying DIY plumbing jobs—it looks intimidating but it's mostly just unbolting, lifting, and reseating. The critical measurement is the rough-in: the distance from the wall to the center of the drain flange, typically 12 inches in most homes (10" and 14" exist too). Buy a toilet with the matching rough-in and you're in good shape.

The wax ring is the most important part of the job. Use a standard ring for most installs, or a double-thickness ring if the flange sits below the finished floor. Don't reuse an old wax ring under any circumstances.

**DIY cost:** $150–$600 for the toilet, $5–$15 for the wax ring and hardware.  
**Plumber cost:** $200–$400 labor on top of the fixture cost.

### Showerhead and Shower Arm Swap

This is the easiest plumbing job on the list. Unscrew the old showerhead (counterclockwise), clean the threads on the shower arm, wrap with fresh Teflon tape, thread on the new head, hand-tighten plus a quarter turn with pliers. Done.

**DIY cost:** $25–$300 depending on the showerhead.  
**Plumber cost:** Unnecessary for a basic swap—though a plumber will often do it as an add-on during another visit.

## Jobs That Need a Licensed Plumber

### Sewer Line Work

If drains throughout the house are backing up simultaneously, or you're seeing sewage in your lowest drains, you likely have a main sewer line issue—blockage, root intrusion, or a collapsed section. This is not a DIY job for several reasons:

1. You need a camera inspection to know what you're actually dealing with.
2. Clearing a sewer line properly requires a power auger or hydro-jetting equipment that runs $500–$1,500 for a rental and requires training to use safely.
3. If the line is damaged or offset, you may need excavation or trenchless repair.

**Plumber cost:** $150–$500 for clearing; $3,000–$10,000+ for line replacement depending on depth, length, and access.

### Water Heater Replacement

Replacing a tank water heater involves gas or 240V electrical connections, a temperature-pressure relief valve, proper venting (for gas units), and code compliance. In most jurisdictions, this work requires a permit and inspection per the [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/).

Gas lines especially: a bad connection is a carbon monoxide and explosion risk — the [Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Guides/General-Information/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center) has documented hundreds of CO incidents from improperly installed gas appliances. This is not the place to learn on the job.

**Plumber cost:** $300–$600 labor for a standard tank swap, plus the unit ($400–$1,200).

### Gas Line Work

Full stop. Do not touch gas lines. Any modification, extension, or repair of a gas line requires a licensed plumber or gas fitter with the proper certifications. The consequences of a leak—explosion, fire, CO poisoning—are catastrophic.

### Repiping

If your home has galvanized steel pipes from the 1960s or 1970s, or you've discovered you have polybutylene (the gray plastic piping that was used through the 1990s and is known to fail), repiping is in your future. This involves opening walls, running new supply lines throughout the house, and patching afterward. It's a multi-day project that requires permits and inspections.

**Plumber cost:** $4,000–$15,000 depending on home size and access.

### Moving or Adding Drain Lines

This is the one that catches DIYers off guard during bathroom renovations. Moving a toilet 12 inches, adding a second sink, or relocating a shower drain all involve cutting into the drain-waste-vent (DWV) system—a network that needs precise slope (1/4" per foot), correct venting, and often a permit.

If you're pricing out a [DIY bathroom remodel](/blog/diy-bathroom-remodel-cost/) and thinking about repositioning fixtures, factor in professional rough-in costs. The fixture costs are visible; the drain relocation is where budgets get blown.

## How to Find a Good Plumber

When you do need a pro, the quality difference between plumbers is significant. Here's how to avoid the bad ones:

**Check license and insurance first.** Every state requires plumbers to be licensed. Ask for their license number and verify it on your state's contractor licensing board website — most states list active licenses through a searchable database maintained by their [Department of Consumer Affairs](https://www.usa.gov/state-consumer) or equivalent agency. They should also carry general liability insurance and workers' comp.

**Get three quotes for anything over $500.** For a water heater or larger job, three quotes will usually show you the range clearly. If one is dramatically lower, ask why—it often means they're cutting corners on permits or materials.

**Ask about permits explicitly.** Reputable plumbers pull permits for work that requires it (water heaters, repiping, drain relocations). If a plumber says "we don't need a permit for that" and you know the job typically requires one, walk away. Unpermitted work can create problems when you sell.

**Avoid anyone who quotes over the phone for complex work.** A real plumber needs to see the job before pricing it. Phone quotes for a sewer replacement or repipe are guesses that usually balloon on-site.

**Check reviews, but read them.** A plumber with 4.7 stars and 200 reviews is more reliable than one with 5.0 stars and 8 reviews. Look specifically for mentions of showing up on time, honest diagnosis, and clean work.

## What Plumbers Actually Cost

Most licensed plumbers charge a **service call fee of $50–$100** just to show up, which is credited against the job if you hire them. On top of that:

- **Standard hourly rate:** $75–$150/hr in most markets.
- **Emergency/after-hours:** $150–$250/hr and up.
- **Flat-rate jobs:** Many plumbers now price common jobs flat (faucet install, toilet replacement) rather than hourly. This is fine as long as you get the total in writing before they start.

## Red Flags to Watch For

**Wet spots on ceilings or walls.** An active leak inside a wall needs professional diagnosis. The source is almost never where the stain is.

**Low water pressure throughout the house.** If it's not a municipal issue, this often signals a supply line problem, failing pressure regulator, or the early stages of mineral buildup in galvanized pipes.

**Water that smells like sulfur or looks brown.** This is often a water heater issue (the anode rod) or a sign that galvanized pipes are corroding. Either warrants a plumber's evaluation.

**Gurgling sounds when you flush.** Gurgling from a sink when you flush a toilet means your drain-waste-vent system has a venting problem. It's not an emergency, but it can become one if ignored.

**Frozen pipes.** Thawing a frozen pipe yourself can work, but if the pipe has already split or you can't access it, a plumber can assess the damage before you turn the water back on and flood the room.

## The Honest Bottom Line

Plumbing is one of the better areas for homeowner DIY—the basics are genuinely learnable, the tools are inexpensive, and the savings on simple jobs are real. But the jobs that sit above that threshold sit well above it. There's no partial credit in plumbing: a pipe either holds pressure or it doesn't, a drain either has the right slope or it doesn't, a gas connection either seals or it doesn't.

Know the list. Do the ones you can. Pay a pro for the ones you can't—and pick a good one.
