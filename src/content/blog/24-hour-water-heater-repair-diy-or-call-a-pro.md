Your water heater is dead and you're standing in a cold house at 11 PM. Before you call a 24-hour plumber and hand over $300 in emergency labor fees, spend five minutes figuring out what actually failed.

Most water heater "emergencies" aren't. A tripped breaker, a blown pilot light, or a single bad element can all mimic total failure — and all three have fixes that cost less than $30 and take under an hour. The failures that genuinely require an immediate pro call are far less common, and they announce themselves clearly.

Here's how to tell the difference.

## Start With a Fast Diagnosis

Don't call anyone until you've run through these checks. They take five minutes and can save you the after-hours premium, which typically runs 1.5x to 2x standard rates on nights and weekends.

**Gas unit, no hot water:** Walk to the unit and look at the pilot light window. If the flame is out, your fix is free. The relight procedure is printed on the unit itself — turn the gas valve to "pilot," hold the igniter for 30–45 seconds, then release. If the pilot lights and stays lit, you're done. If it goes out again within an hour, you have a bad thermocouple (more on that below).

**Electric unit, no hot water:** Go to your breaker panel. A tripped breaker is the single most common cause of sudden hot water loss on electric heaters. Reset it once. If it holds, you're done for now — but schedule an electrician to figure out why it tripped in the first place. A breaker that trips twice means something downstream is pulling too much current.

**Lukewarm water instead of hot:** You're getting some hot water, which means the unit is alive. On electric heaters, this is almost always one failed heating element out of two — the lower element does most of the work, and when it fails, you get warm but not hot. On gas units, a partially clogged burner orifice or a thermostat set too low produces the same symptom.

**Popping, rumbling, or banging:** That's sediment on the bottom of your tank flashing to steam when the burner fires. It sounds alarming but it isn't an emergency — it means your tank is overdue for a [flush of the tank](/blog/how-often-should-i-flush-my-water-heater-honest-guide/), which any homeowner can do with a garden hose.

**Water pooling at the base:** This one's different. A leak from the tank itself — not from fittings, not from the pressure relief valve discharge, but from the tank body — means internal corrosion. You don't repair this. You replace the unit, and sooner is better because that corrosion is progressing daily.

## Repairs You Can Do Yourself Tonight

Once you've identified the failure mode, several of the most common ones are genuinely within reach for a homeowner who's comfortable with basic tools.

### Pilot Light Out (Gas Units)

This is the most common gas water heater "failure" and it's completely free to fix. Turn the gas control valve to "pilot," press and hold the igniter button or use a long lighter at the pilot port, and maintain pressure for 30–45 seconds after ignition. The thermocouple — a thin probe that sits in the pilot flame — needs time to heat up before it'll hold the gas valve open. Release too early and it goes out again.

### Thermocouple Replacement (Gas Units)

If the pilot lights but won't stay lit, your thermocouple has failed. It's a thin copper rod that sits in the pilot flame and generates a small voltage to keep the gas valve open. When it wears out, the safety valve cuts gas to the pilot — which is correct behavior; it's doing its job.

A replacement thermocouple costs $10–$30 at any hardware store and fits most brands — Rheem, AO Smith, Bradford White all use standard fittings. Shut off the gas supply, disconnect the three connections at the gas valve (thermocouple lead, pilot supply, and main burner line), pull the burner assembly, swap the thermocouple, and reassemble. It takes about 20 minutes with an adjustable wrench. This is absolutely a DIY repair — there's no reason to pay $150–$250 in labor for it.

### Heating Element Replacement (Electric Units)

An electric tank heater has two elements: one near the top, one near the bottom. The lower element does the heavy lifting and fails first. When it goes, you get lukewarm water instead of hot. When the upper element fails, you often get no hot water at all.

Turn off the breaker for the water heater. Attach a garden hose to the drain valve and run it outside or to a floor drain, then open a hot water faucet somewhere in the house to break the vacuum and let the tank drain below the element port. Unscrew the access panel cover, remove the insulation, and use a $5 element wrench to pull the old element. Thread in the new one — a Rheem or AO Smith replacement element runs $20–$40 and is available at any home center — refill the tank before turning the power back on. Total job time: 45 minutes.

This is a legitimate DIY repair that plumbers charge $150–$300 to do. If you're going to own an electric water heater, learn to do this.

### Thermostat Adjustment

If you're getting water that's just barely warm, check the thermostat setting before assuming anything's broken. Both gas and electric units have adjustable thermostats, and they sometimes get bumped or drift over time. The Department of Energy recommends 120°F as the sweet spot — hot enough for showers, low enough to prevent scalding and keep standby heat loss manageable.

On electric units, the thermostat is behind the same access panel as the element. On gas units, it's the dial on the gas valve itself.

## What You Shouldn't Touch

There's a clear line between the repairs above and the ones that require a licensed plumber or electrician. Don't cross it.

**Gas line work.** Replacing a thermocouple doesn't involve the gas supply line. Anything that does — replacing a gas valve, moving the supply line, converting fuel types — belongs to a licensed plumber. If you [smell gas near your water heater](/blog/water-heater-smells-like-gas-but-no-leak-honest-guide/), shut off the supply at the meter shutoff and get out. Call your gas utility's emergency line, not a plumber.

**T&P relief valve issues.** The temperature-and-pressure relief valve is a safety device. If your [pop-off valve is leaking](/blog/water-heater-blow-off-valve-leaking-a-practical-guide/) continuously, it's either failed (which means you replace it — straightforward) or it's venting because pressure in your system is genuinely too high (which means something else is wrong). The second scenario requires a pro to diagnose. Don't cap the discharge pipe. Don't ignore it.

**Breaker problems.** Reset the breaker once. If it trips again, hire an electrician. A breaker that keeps tripping is telling you there's a fault somewhere — in the element, the wiring, or the panel itself. This is not a water heater problem anymore; it's an electrical problem.

**Full unit replacement.** A 40-gallon tank weighs 100–150 pounds empty and considerably more when you're wrestling it out of a tight utility closet. Installation involves either gas connections and venting or 240V wiring, plus supply and drain lines, a drain pan, and earthquake straps if you're in a seismic zone. Most jurisdictions require a permit and inspection for water heater replacement. The permit isn't optional — it protects your homeowners insurance coverage and your home's resale. If you do need to call, know what you're paying for:

| Service | Typical Range |
|---|---|
| After-hours service call (diagnosis only) | $150–$300 |
| Thermocouple replacement | $150–$250 (parts + labor) |
| Heating element replacement | $150–$300 |
| Thermostat replacement | $150–$350 |
| T&P relief valve replacement | $100–$200 |

The after-hours premium is real and non-negotiable. If you can manage without hot water until Monday morning, you'll save $100–$200 on the same repair. That's worth knowing before you pick up the phone at midnight.

## How to Vet an Emergency Plumber Fast

When you do call, protect yourself:

Get a price range before anyone shows up. A reputable company will give you a rough estimate over the phone based on your description. If they refuse to quote anything until they're on-site, that's a setup for an inflated number after you've already committed.

Verify licensing before they arrive. Every state has an online lookup for licensed contractors — use it. An unlicensed plumber working on gas lines or electrical is a liability problem for you, not them.

Ask about the diagnostic fee and whether it applies to the repair. Most emergency services charge $75–$150 to show up and assess. Some credit it toward the job; some don't. Get that in writing.

Confirm the full repair quote before authorizing work. Once they've diagnosed the issue, you're entitled to a firm price before they start. "I'll know more once I open it up" is an acceptable answer for the first five minutes. After that, they should have a number. If the quote feels high, say so — or tell them you'll get a second opinion in the morning. A reputable plumber won't walk off the job over that.

## When to Replace Instead of Repair

Here's the honest version: if your unit is over 10 years old and the repair quote is over $400, you're probably paying to delay an inevitable replacement by one or two years.

A standard tank water heater — Rheem, AO Smith, Bradford White, it doesn't matter — lasts 10–15 years. After that, the anode rod is likely depleted, sediment has built up on the bottom, and the likelihood of tank corrosion increases sharply. Spending $350 on a 13-year-old unit usually makes less sense than putting that money toward a new one, especially when you factor in the improved efficiency of current models.

Replace without hesitation if you see:
- Rust-colored water from the hot side only
- A visible leak from the tank body (not fittings, not the T&P valve — the tank itself)
- Corrosion or mineral crust on the outside of the tank
- Two or more repairs in the past two years
- The unit is over 12 years old — check the serial number for manufacture date; most manufacturers encode year and month in the first four characters

## The Short Version

Most water heater failures at 2 AM are a pilot light, a tripped breaker, or a dead heating element. All three are cheap, DIY-fixable, and don't require an emergency call. The ones that do — active leaks, gas smells, electrical burning — are obvious and non-negotiable. Know which category you're in before you spend $300 on a service call for something that costs $20 in parts.
