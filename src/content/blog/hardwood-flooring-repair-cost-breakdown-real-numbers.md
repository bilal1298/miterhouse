Hardwood floor repairs range from under $100 for a DIY scratch fix to $5,000+ for professional refinishing of an entire room. The cost depends almost entirely on what kind of damage you're dealing with and how much floor area is affected. Most homeowners spend between $500 and $2,500 on a repair project.

Here's what each type of repair actually costs and when each one makes sense.

## Minor Surface Repairs: Scratches, Dents, and Scuffs

Surface-level damage is the most common issue and the cheapest to fix. This includes scratches that haven't gone through the finish, small dents from dropped objects, and scuff marks from furniture.

**DIY cost: $15–$75 in materials.** Wood filler sticks, touch-up markers, and matching stain pens handle most surface scratches. For shallow dents in solid hardwood, a damp cloth and a household iron can swell the compressed wood fibers back into shape — a trick that genuinely works on unfinished or lightly finished surfaces.

**Professional cost: $200–$500 for a spot repair.** A flooring pro will screen (lightly sand) the damaged area, apply filler if needed, and recoat with matching finish. This makes sense when the damage covers several square feet or when you need an invisible repair in a prominent area.

The one thing to know: matching existing floor finish is harder than it looks. Polyurethane yellows with age, and stain colors shift over time. A spot repair that looks perfect today may stand out once the surrounding floor continues to age. For best results, recoat the entire room rather than spot-treating if the finish is more than 5 years old.

## Board Replacement: Fixing Structural Damage

When individual boards are cracked, deeply gouged, warped, or water-damaged beyond surface repair, they need to come out. This is a more involved process than patching.

**Materials cost: $3–$15 per square foot for matching replacement boards.** The challenge is finding wood that matches your existing floor in species, width, grade, and color. If your floors are more than 10 years old, an exact match may require custom milling, which pushes costs to $10–$20 per square foot.

**Professional labor for board replacement: $8–$15 per square foot (minimum charges of $300–$500 apply).** The process involves cutting out the damaged board with a circular saw set to the exact floor thickness, chiseling the remaining pieces, prepping the subfloor underneath, and weaving in the replacement board. The bottom groove of the new board gets cut off so it can be face-nailed and glued into place.

A few boards in a hallway might cost $400–$800 total. Replacing a 50-square-foot section of water-damaged flooring could run $1,000–$2,000.

**The DIY option is real but tricky.** You need to set your circular saw depth precisely — cut too deep and you damage the subfloor or hit nails. The weaving technique for the replacement board takes practice. If you've done woodworking projects, this is manageable. If your most advanced tool experience is assembling IKEA furniture, hire this one out.

## Refinishing: The Big Reset

Full refinishing is the nuclear option for hardwood floor repair. It fixes widespread scratches, worn finish, moderate staining, and gives you the chance to change stain color entirely.

**Professional refinishing cost: $3–$8 per square foot.** For a 300-square-foot room, expect to pay $900 to $2,400. The process includes:

1. Sanding with a drum sander (3 passes at different grits)
2. Edge sanding along walls and in corners
3. Vacuuming and tack-cloth cleaning between coats
4. Applying stain (optional — natural is beautiful too)
5. Applying 2–3 coats of polyurethane finish

**Timeline: 3–5 days total.** One day for sanding, one day for stain, and 1–2 days for finish coats with drying time between each. You can't walk on the floor during this process, so plan to be out of the room.

**DIY refinishing cost: $1–$3 per square foot.** Equipment rental (drum sander, edge sander, buffer) runs $100–$200 per day. Sandpaper, stain, and polyurethane add another $200–$400 for a typical room. Total: $400–$1,000 for that same 300-square-foot room.

A word of caution on DIY refinishing: the drum sander is an aggressive tool that can gouge your floor permanently if you pause in one spot or tilt it wrong. Watch every tutorial you can find, practice on scrap plywood, and go slowly. The money you save is real, but so is the risk. The [National Wood Flooring Association](https://www.nwfa.org/) has technical resources worth reviewing before you rent the equipment.

## Water Damage Repairs

Water damage is the most expensive repair category because it often involves more than just the flooring surface.

**Minor water damage (cupping from humidity):** $1–$3 per square foot for dehumidification and monitoring. Boards that have cupped from seasonal humidity changes often flatten on their own once the moisture issue is resolved. Don't sand cupped boards flat — if they later flatten naturally, you'll end up with crowning instead.

**Moderate water damage (staining, localized warping):** $500–$2,000. This involves removing affected boards, drying the subfloor thoroughly (fans and dehumidifier for 3–7 days), treating for mold if present, and installing replacement boards.

**Severe water damage (flooding, long-term leaks):** $2,000–$8,000+. Extended water exposure damages the subfloor underneath. You may need to pull up a large section of hardwood, replace subfloor plywood, address any mold or mildew, and install new flooring. At this point, you're approaching the cost of a full replacement.

The critical step with any water damage: find and fix the source before repairing the floor. A slow plumbing leak will destroy your new repair just as efficiently as it destroyed the original floor. ## Squeaky Floor Repairs

Squeaks happen when boards move against each other, against nails, or against the subfloor. This is usually a structural issue rather than a surface one.

**From above (if you can access the squeaky area):** $50–$150 in materials. Specialty breakaway screws (like Squeeeeek No More) pull the subfloor tight to the joists through the hardwood. Counter-boring and plugging with matching wood is another option but requires more skill.

**From below (if you have basement or crawlspace access):** $20–$50 in materials. Shimming between the subfloor and joists, or screwing up through the subfloor into the hardwood from underneath, is the cleanest fix. You can't see any repair from the living space.

**Professional squeaky floor repair: $200–$400 per area.** Worth it if the squeaks are widespread or you don't have access from below.

## Gap Repair

Gaps between hardwood boards are seasonal and normal in most climates. Boards expand in summer (higher humidity) and contract in winter (dry heated air). Gaps up to the thickness of a dime during winter months are normal and not a defect.

**When gaps need fixing:**
- Year-round gaps wider than 1/8" suggest the floor was installed at the wrong moisture content
- Gaps that collect enough debris to be a cleaning nuisance
- Gaps in high-visibility areas that bother you aesthetically

**Repair options:**
- **Flexible wood filler ($5–$15 per tube):** Good for narrow gaps. Must be flexible — rigid filler will crack out as boards continue their seasonal movement.
- **Rope or spline inserts ($100–$300 for a pro):** Natural fiber rope stained to match gets pushed into wider gaps. Old-school technique that works well.
- **Humidity control ($200–$500 for a whole-house humidifier):** Addresses the root cause in dry climates. Maintaining 35–55% relative humidity keeps gaps minimal.

The [National Wood Flooring Association](https://www.nwfa.org/) is the go-to resource for wood flooring installation and repair best practices.

## When to Repair vs. Replace

**Repair makes sense when:**
- Damage is localized (less than 30% of total floor area)
- The floor hasn't been refinished more than twice (solid hardwood)
- Matching replacement boards are available or can be milled
- The subfloor underneath is sound

**Replacement makes sense when:**
- Widespread structural damage or deep water staining
- The floor has been sanded so many times the tongues are thin
- You want to change species, plank width, or overall look
- Repair costs approach 60–70% of replacement cost

## Getting Accurate Repair Quotes

When contacting flooring contractors for repair work, provide:
- Photos of the damage (close-up and wider room shots)
- Approximate square footage of the affected area
- The wood species and approximate age of the floor if you know it
- Whether you have access from below (basement/crawlspace)
- Any known cause of the damage (water, pets, furniture, age)

Get three quotes minimum. Repair work varies more in pricing than new installation because each situation is unique. A contractor who sees the damage in person will give you a much more accurate number than one quoting over the phone.

Most hardwood floor repairs are worthwhile investments. Even at the higher end, repairing existing hardwood costs less than full replacement, and well-maintained hardwood floors consistently add value to a home at resale.
