Drain problems range from a 5-minute fix with a plunger to a $10,000 sewer line replacement, and the difference between those extremes depends on where the clog or damage is, what your pipes are made of, and how long the problem has been developing. The good news: most residential drain repairs fall squarely in the DIY-friendly zone if you know how to diagnose the issue first.

Here is a practical guide to figuring out what is wrong with your drains, what you can fix yourself, and when to bring in a professional.

## Diagnosing the Problem

Before you grab a tool, spend five minutes diagnosing. The symptoms tell you exactly where the problem sits.

**Single slow drain.** If only one sink, tub, or shower is draining slowly, the clog is in that fixture's drain or trap — within 5 feet of the drain opening. This is almost always a DIY fix.

**Multiple slow drains.** When two or more drains on the same floor are sluggish, the clog is in a branch line — the horizontal pipe connecting those fixtures to the main stack. Still potentially DIY, but harder to reach.

**All drains in the house are slow or backing up.** This indicates a main sewer line problem between your house and the street. Water backing up through the lowest drain (typically a basement floor drain or first-floor shower) confirms it. This is professional territory.

**Gurgling sounds from drains.** Air bubbles pushing through standing water in a trap usually means a venting issue — the vent pipe that allows air into the drain system is blocked or undersized. Sometimes it is a partial clog creating negative pressure.

**Sewer smell from drains.** Either a dry P-trap (pour water down the drain) or a crack in the drain pipe allowing sewer gas to escape.

## DIY Drain Repairs: What You Can Handle

### Clearing a Clogged Sink or Tub Drain

**Method 1: The plunger.** Use a cup plunger (flat bottom) for sinks and a flange plunger (extended lip) for toilets. Block the overflow hole with a wet rag to create full suction. Plunge vigorously 15–20 times. This clears most simple clogs instantly.

**Method 2: Clean the P-trap.** Place a bucket under the trap, unscrew the slip nuts by hand or with channel-lock pliers, and pull the trap down. Clean out the accumulated gunk — you will be amazed what collects in there. Reassemble with new nylon washers if the old ones are compressed ($3 for a pack).

**Method 3: Hand-crank drain snake.** Feed the cable into the drain opening past the trap, cranking as you push. When you hit resistance, work the cable back and forth while cranking. A 25-foot hand snake ($25–$40) handles nearly all fixture clogs. Pull the cable out slowly — the clog comes with it.

**Method 4: Enzymatic drain cleaner.** For maintenance on slow drains, enzymatic cleaners (not chemical drain openers) break down organic buildup overnight without damaging pipes. Use monthly as prevention. Chemical drain cleaners containing lye or sulfuric acid work fast but corrode pipes over time, especially old metal drains. I have seen enough corroded-through pipes to avoid recommending them.

### Replacing a P-Trap

P-traps eventually develop leaks at the slip joints or corrode through (metal traps in older homes). Replacement is straightforward:

1. Place a bucket under the trap and disconnect both slip nuts
2. Take the old trap to the hardware store to match the size — 1-1/4" for bathroom sinks, 1-1/2" for kitchen sinks
3. PVC replacement traps cost $5–$12 and last essentially forever
4. Assemble the new trap finger-tight, then snug with pliers — do not overtighten PVC
5. Run water and check for drips at every joint

Total time: 20–30 minutes. Total cost: under $15.

### Fixing a Leaking Drain Pipe Joint

PVC drain pipes connect with glued joints or slip joints. If a glued joint is leaking, the proper fix is cutting out the section and re-gluing with PVC primer and cement. You need:

- PVC pipe cutter or hacksaw ($10–$20)
- PVC primer and cement ($8 for both)
- Coupling fittings to rejoin the cut section ($2–$5 each)

Allow 2 hours of cure time before running water through the line. The [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) requires all drain joints to be watertight — which means proper primer and cement application, not just friction fit.

### Clearing a Branch Line Clog

If the clog is beyond the trap and the hand snake cannot reach it, you need access to a cleanout fitting. Most homes have cleanout plugs on branch lines in the basement or crawlspace — they are capped PVC or threaded metal fittings on horizontal drain pipes.

Open the cleanout (have a bucket ready — water will flow out), and run your snake from there. A 50-foot snake ($40–$75) reaches most branch line clogs. Work slowly and let the cable do the work — forcing it can kink the cable or damage pipe joints.

## When to Call a Professional

### Main Sewer Line Problems

If your diagnosis points to the main line, call a licensed plumber with camera inspection capability. Here is what the process looks like and costs:

**Camera inspection:** $125–$350. The plumber feeds a fiber-optic camera through a cleanout to identify exactly what is wrong — roots, grease buildup, collapsed pipe, or belly (low spot) in the line.

**Snaking the main line:** $200–$500. A motorized cable with cutting heads clears roots and buildup. This is a temporary fix for root intrusion — roots grow back within 6–18 months.

**Hydro-jetting:** $350–$800. High-pressure water (3,000–4,000 PSI) scours the pipe interior clean. More thorough than snaking and keeps the line clear longer. Not appropriate for fragile or damaged pipes.

**Pipe lining (trenchless repair):** $4,000–$8,000 for a typical 50-foot residential line. An epoxy-saturated liner is pulled through the existing pipe and inflated, creating a new pipe inside the old one. This avoids digging up your yard and works on most pipe materials except severely collapsed sections.

**Trench replacement:** $5,000–$15,000+. The old pipe is dug up and replaced with new PVC. Necessary for collapsed pipes, severe bellies, or pipes with multiple breaks. Add $2,000–$5,000 if the city-side connection at the street needs replacement too. For more details, read our [sewer plumbing repair guide](/blog/sewer-plumbing-repair-guide-what-to-know/).

### Vent Pipe Issues

Vent repairs typically require working on the roof or inside walls — both are situations where most homeowners should hire help. A blocked vent pipe on the roof can sometimes be cleared by flushing a garden hose down it, but if the vent pipe is cracked or incorrectly sized, a plumber needs to evaluate the system.

### Old Cast Iron or Galvanized Drain Pipes

Homes built before 1970 often have cast iron drain stacks and galvanized branch lines. These materials corrode from the inside out, and by the time you see leaks or chronic clogs, the internal diameter of the pipe may be reduced by 50% or more from rust scale.

Repairing individual joints on cast iron is possible using no-hub couplings (rubber gaskets with stainless steel band clamps), but if the pipe shows widespread deterioration, full replacement with PVC is the only long-term fix. This is a job requiring structural support considerations since cast iron is heavy, and the connections to the main stack need to be done correctly to maintain proper slope and venting.

## Essential Drain Repair Tools

Investing in basic drain tools pays for itself quickly:

- **Flange plunger** — $8–$12. Works on both sinks and toilets.
- **25-foot hand snake** — $25–$40. Handles 90% of fixture clogs.
- **Channel-lock pliers** — $15–$25. Necessary for slip joints and compression fittings.
- **Pipe wrench (14")** — $20–$30. For threaded metal drain fittings and cleanout plugs.
- **Bucket and flashlight** — You already have these.

For about $80–$120 in tools, you can handle most drain problems that would otherwise cost $150–$400 in service calls. Per [EPA WaterSense recommendations](https://www.epa.gov/watersense), maintaining your drain system also prevents water waste from chronic leaks.

## Preventing Drain Problems

Most drain clogs are entirely preventable:

- **Kitchen drains:** Never pour grease down the drain. Use a drain strainer to catch food particles. Run hot water for 30 seconds after using the disposal.
- **Bathroom sinks and tubs:** Install mesh drain covers to catch hair — the number-one cause of bathroom drain clogs. Clean the stopper mechanism monthly.
- **Toilets:** Only flush toilet paper and human waste. "Flushable" wipes are not flushable regardless of what the packaging says — they cause more main line blockages than anything else.
- **Main sewer line:** If you have trees near your sewer line, consider annual root treatment. Copper sulfate flushed down the toilet ($10 for a year's supply) kills roots inside the pipe without harming the tree.
