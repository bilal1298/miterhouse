Wood flooring repair costs between $3 and $12 per square foot for refinishing, $10 to $30 per square foot for board replacement, and $200 to $500 for targeted spot repairs like filling gouges or fixing squeaks. The total depends entirely on what is actually wrong with your floor and how much area is affected.

Before you price out a full replacement, understand that most wood floor problems can be repaired for a fraction of what new flooring costs. The trick is accurately diagnosing the issue first.

## Types of Wood Floor Damage and What They Cost to Fix

### Surface Scratches and Scuffs

Light scratches that do not penetrate the stain layer are the simplest fix. A hardwood floor refresher product costs $15 to $30 and covers 500 square feet. You apply it with a mop, it fills micro-scratches, and the floor looks noticeably better within an hour.

For medium scratches that reach the wood but do not gouge deeply, wood filler sticks ($5 to $15) or wax repair kits ($10 to $25) handle individual marks. Match the color carefully under natural light. Store lighting skews the tones.

### Deep Gouges and Dents

Individual gouges deeper than 1/16 inch need wood filler, sanding, and spot-finishing. Expect to spend $20 to $50 in materials per repair area if you DIY. A professional charges $150 to $300 per visit for spot repairs like this, which usually includes fixing multiple blemishes in one trip.

For dents in softer woods like pine, you can sometimes raise the grain with a damp cloth and an iron set to steam. Place the wet cloth over the dent, press the iron on it for 30 seconds, and check. This works about 60% of the time on shallow dents.

### Water Damage

Water damage ranges from minor cupping (boards curving slightly upward at the edges) to severe buckling where boards pop up from the subfloor. The cost spread is enormous.

**Minor cupping** may resolve on its own once the moisture source is eliminated. If boards have cupped but are still attached and structurally sound, let the floor dry slowly over 2 to 4 weeks before sanding. Sanding cupped boards before they dry flat results in crowned boards later. Cost if it resolves naturally: $0. Cost if it needs light sanding afterward: $2 to $5 per square foot.

**Buckling** means boards have lifted off the subfloor. You need to find and fix the water source, remove the damaged boards, dry the subfloor thoroughly (which might take a week with fans and a dehumidifier), and install new boards. Budget $10 to $30 per square foot for board replacement plus subfloor remediation.

**Black staining** from prolonged water exposure often means the wood is damaged beyond cosmetic repair. You can try oxalic acid ($10 to $20 per treatment area), which bleaches out some dark water stains. It works on surface stains but not on stains that have penetrated through the board.

### Squeaky Floors

Squeaks come from boards rubbing against each other or against the subfloor. From below (if you have basement or crawlspace access), you can drive screws through the subfloor into the hardwood to pull loose boards tight. A handful of screws and 30 minutes fixes most isolated squeaks.

From above, specialized breakaway screws like Counter-Snap ($25 for a kit) let you drive a screw through the hardwood, pull the board down, then snap the screw head off below the surface. Fill the tiny hole with matching putty.

For widespread squeaking across a room, the subfloor may need re-securing. That runs $300 to $1,000 professionally, depending on access and room size.

## Full Refinishing: When the Whole Floor Needs Help

If your hardwood has widespread scratches, worn finish, or a color you want to change, full refinishing is the move. This involves sanding the entire floor down to bare wood, applying stain if desired, and sealing with polyurethane.

### Refinishing Cost Breakdown

| Component | DIY Cost (per sq ft) | Professional Cost (per sq ft) |
|-----------|---------------------|-------------------------------|
| Drum sander rental | $0.30–$0.50 | Included |
| Sandpaper/edger pads | $0.20–$0.40 | Included |
| Stain | $0.15–$0.30 | Included |
| Polyurethane (3 coats) | $0.40–$0.70 | Included |
| Labor | Your time | $2.50–$5.00 |
| **Total** | **$1.05–$1.90** | **$3.00–$8.00** |

For a typical 300-square-foot room, that works out to $315 to $570 for DIY or $900 to $2,400 for professional refinishing.

The [National Wood Flooring Association](https://www.nwfa.org/) recommends refinishing hardwood every 7 to 10 years with normal traffic, or whenever the finish is visibly worn through to bare wood.

### DIY Refinishing Reality Check

Renting a drum sander and edger costs $60 to $100 per day. Most first-timers need two days minimum. The drum sander is aggressive and will gouge your floor if you stop moving or apply uneven pressure. Practice in a closet first.

The edger handles the 4 to 6 inches along walls that the drum cannot reach. It is a separate rental and uses different sandpaper discs.

After sanding, vacuum thoroughly (every bit of dust), then tack-cloth the entire surface before applying stain. Dust in the finish is the single most common complaint with DIY refinishing jobs.

## Board Replacement: Replacing Damaged Sections

When individual boards are cracked, warped beyond repair, or missing, you need to weave in new boards.

**Matching the wood species** is the first challenge. If your floor is red oak (the most common in US homes), finding replacement boards is straightforward. For less common species or older floors with unusual widths, you may need to check salvage yards or specialty flooring suppliers.

**Cutting out damaged boards** requires a circular saw set to the exact thickness of the hardwood, plus a chisel to clean up the cut edges. Cut the damaged board lengthwise along its center, then pry each half toward the center to remove without damaging adjacent boards.

**Installing the replacement** means cutting the bottom lip of the groove side so the new board can drop in. Glue it in place with construction adhesive, face-nail with finishing nails, set the nails, fill the holes, and sand flush.

A professional charges $10 to $30 per square foot for board replacement, including matching, cutting, weaving in, and finishing. The minimum trip charge for most flooring contractors is $200 to $400 regardless of how few boards you need replaced.

## When to Repair vs. When to Replace

**Repair makes sense when:**
- Damage covers less than 30% of the total floor area
- The existing wood is at least 3/4 inch thick (enough to sand again)
- The subfloor underneath is solid
- You like the wood species and want to keep it

**Replace the whole floor when:**
- The wood has been sanded to the tongue-and-groove joint (no material left)
- More than half the boards show structural damage
- The subfloor has significant water damage or rot
- You want a completely different look or floor height

For older homes, check for [lead paint](https://www.epa.gov/lead) on previously painted wood floors before sanding. Pre-1978 homes are at risk, and sanding lead paint creates hazardous dust.

## Hiring a Floor Repair Professional

Get at least three quotes. A good flooring repair contractor will:

- Inspect the floor in person before quoting (never accept a phone quote for repair work)
- Explain exactly what needs repair and what does not
- Provide a clear scope that specifies which boards get replaced vs. which get refinished
- Include finish matching in their quote

Ask to see before-and-after photos of repair jobs, not just full refinishing. Refinishing a whole floor is relatively straightforward. Blending a patch repair into an existing finish is the harder skill, and that is what you are hiring for.

## Materials Worth the Extra Cost

**Water-based polyurethane** dries faster and has less odor than oil-based, but oil-based gives a warmer amber tone that many homeowners prefer on traditional hardwood. Water-based runs $40 to $70 per gallon; oil-based runs $30 to $50.

**Pre-mixed wood filler** saves time but limits your color options. Sawdust mixed with wood glue matches better because you can use sawdust from the actual floor boards.

**High-quality sandpaper** makes a bigger difference than most people expect. Cheap sandpaper clogs faster, tears easily, and leaves inconsistent scratch patterns. Spend the extra $10 to $20 on premium paper.

## Timeline for Common Repairs

- **Spot repair (gouges, dents):** 2 to 4 hours including dry time
- **Squeak fix:** 30 minutes to 2 hours
- **Board replacement (small area):** 1 day plus 24-hour cure time
- **Full room refinishing (DIY):** 3 to 5 days including dry time between coats
- **Full room refinishing (pro):** 2 to 3 days

You cannot walk on freshly polyurethaned floors for at least 24 hours with water-based products or 48 hours with oil-based. Do not put furniture back for 72 hours minimum. Plan accordingly, especially if it is a main living area or hallway.

## Bottom Line

Most wood floor damage looks worse than it is. A floor that seems ready for the dumpster might only need strategic board replacements and a fresh coat of finish. Start by diagnosing the actual problem areas, get a professional assessment if you are unsure, and tackle repairs before assuming you need a full replacement. The cost difference between repairing and replacing can easily save $3,000 to $8,000 on a typical room.
