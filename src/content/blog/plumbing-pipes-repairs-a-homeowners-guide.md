Plumbing pipes eventually fail in every home. Copper develops pinhole leaks after 20 to 50 years, galvanized steel corrodes from the inside out, and even modern PEX can be damaged by rodents, UV exposure, or improper installation. Understanding what type of pipes you have and how they commonly fail helps you respond quickly when something goes wrong.

The average homeowner spends $150 to $600 on a typical pipe repair. Knowing what you are dealing with before the plumber arrives (or before you grab a wrench yourself) makes the whole process faster and cheaper.

## Identifying Your Pipe Types

Your home likely has multiple pipe types serving different functions. Here is how to tell them apart.

**Copper pipes** have a distinctive reddish-brown color (or green patina on older exposed sections). They are the gold standard for water supply lines and have been used since the 1950s. Joints are soldered with flux and a torch. Common in supply lines throughout the house.

**Galvanized steel pipes** are gray and threaded at the joints. Common in homes built before 1960. These corrode internally, gradually restricting water flow and eventually leaking. If you have galvanized supply lines, full repiping is in your future.

**PEX (cross-linked polyethylene)** is flexible plastic tubing, usually red for hot and blue for cold. It has been widely used since the early 2000s. Connections use crimp rings, clamp rings, or expansion fittings. Relatively easy to work with for DIY repairs.

**PVC and ABS** are white and black plastic pipes used for drain, waste, and vent (DWV) lines. PVC uses purple primer and solvent cement. ABS uses its own cement. These rarely fail unless physically damaged or improperly installed.

**CPVC** is cream-colored plastic used for hot and cold supply lines in some homes built in the 1980s and 1990s. It becomes brittle with age and is prone to cracking, especially near hot water connections.

## The Most Common Pipe Failures

### Pinhole Leaks in Copper

These small leaks develop when the pipe wall thins from the inside due to aggressive water chemistry. You will often notice a green stain on the pipe surface before any dripping starts. A single pinhole leak can be patched, but multiple pinhole leaks in the same section suggest systemic corrosion that requires section replacement.

**Quick fix:** A push-fit coupling (SharkBite or similar, $8 to $15) lets you cut out the damaged section and splice in a repair without soldering. This is a legitimate permanent repair when installed correctly.

### Corroded Galvanized Joints

The threaded connections on galvanized pipes are where failure typically starts. You will see rust-colored water, reduced pressure, and eventually dripping at joints. Replacing individual fittings is possible but often causes the adjacent pipe to crack because the corrosion has weakened everything.

**Honest advice:** If you are repairing galvanized pipes, you are really just buying time until a full repipe. Budget $4,000 to $15,000 for a whole-house repipe with copper or PEX, and start planning.

### Cracked PVC Drain Lines

PVC cracks from physical impact, ground shifting, or root intrusion. The repair involves cutting out the damaged section and gluing in a new piece with couplings. This is genuine DIY territory for accessible pipes.

**What you need:** A hacksaw or PVC cutter, the right diameter pipe and couplings, PVC primer, and PVC cement. Total materials cost: $5 to $20.

### Frozen and Burst Pipes

Water expands roughly 9% when it freezes, and that force is enough to split copper, PEX, and even steel. The burst usually occurs downstream from the ice blockage, where pressure builds. If you discover a frozen pipe before it bursts, thaw it slowly with a hair dryer or heat tape. Never use an open flame.

After a burst, your immediate priorities are:
1. Shut off the main water supply
2. Open faucets to drain remaining pressure
3. Document the damage for insurance
4. Call a plumber for the repair

## Tools for Basic Pipe Repair

A well-equipped pipe repair toolkit does not require a massive investment:

- Pipe cutter (copper) or tubing cutter: $12 to $25
- PVC cutter or hacksaw: $10 to $20
- Push-fit fittings (SharkBite or equivalent) in your pipe sizes: $8 to $15 each
- Channel-lock pliers: $15 to $25
- Teflon tape: $3
- Emery cloth or sandpaper for pipe prep: $5
- Pipe deburring tool: $8

For under $100, you can handle most accessible pipe repairs. The push-fit fitting revolution has genuinely changed what homeowners can accomplish without soldering.

## When to Call a Plumber

Some situations exceed DIY scope regardless of your skill level.

**Behind walls or under slabs.** Accessing these pipes requires demolition and sometimes jackhammering. A plumber with a camera can pinpoint the problem before opening walls, saving thousands in unnecessary demolition.

**Multiple simultaneous leaks.** This signals systemic failure, not a spot repair. You need a plumber to assess the whole system and recommend the right scope of work.

**Sewer line problems.** Main sewer lines require camera inspection, and repairs often involve heavy equipment. This work requires permits and must meet [building code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) requirements.

**Low water pressure throughout the house.** This could indicate a failing pressure regulator, corroded main line, or a municipal issue. A plumber with a pressure gauge can diagnose this in minutes.

**Any gas piping.** Gas line work is governed by [NFPA codes](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70) and must be done by licensed professionals. No exceptions.

## Preventing Pipe Problems

Most pipe failures are preventable with basic maintenance:

- **Insulate exposed pipes** in unheated spaces before winter. Foam pipe insulation costs under $1 per linear foot.
- **Know your main shutoff location.** Practice turning it off so you can react fast in an emergency.
- **Replace washing machine hoses** every 5 years. Burst washing machine hoses are one of the leading causes of residential water damage.
- **Address slow drains promptly.** A slow drain becomes a clogged drain becomes a backed-up drain. Clean them early.
- **Monitor your water bill.** An unexplained increase often indicates a hidden leak.
- **Test your water pressure.** A $10 gauge screws onto any hose bib. Residential pressure should be 40 to 80 psi. Above 80 damages pipes and fixtures over time; install a pressure-reducing valve.

## What a Plumber's Visit Actually Looks Like

If you have never called a plumber, here is what to expect. Most charge a service call fee of $50 to $150 just to show up and diagnose the problem. This is standard and reasonable -- they are bringing a truck full of specialized tools and years of training.

After diagnosis, they will provide an estimate. For straightforward repairs, many plumbers can complete the work on the same visit. For larger jobs, they may need to schedule a return trip with specific materials.

Ask about warranty. Reputable plumbers warranty their work for at least one year. The materials themselves often carry manufacturer warranties of 5 to 25 years depending on the product.

## Bottom Line

Plumbing pipes are durable but not permanent. Know your pipe types, keep basic repair supplies on hand, and understand the boundary between DIY fixes and professional work. The $100 you spend on a plumber's diagnosis can save you from turning a $200 repair into a $10,000 water damage claim.
