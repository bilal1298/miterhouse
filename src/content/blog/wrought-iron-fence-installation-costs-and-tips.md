Wrought iron fence installation costs $30 to $100 per linear foot installed, depending on height, design complexity, and whether you are buying true custom fabrication or pre-made ornamental steel panels. A 100-foot perimeter with a single gate typically runs $3,500 to $10,000 for the complete job. That makes wrought iron the most expensive mainstream fencing option — two to three times the cost of wood or chain link — but it also lasts 50 to 100 years with proper maintenance, making the lifetime cost surprisingly competitive.

The trick with wrought iron is understanding what you are actually buying, because the term covers a wide range of products at very different price points.

## "Wrought Iron" vs. Ornamental Steel vs. Aluminum

True wrought iron — hand-forged iron with almost zero carbon content — has not been manufactured at scale since the early 1900s. When someone says "wrought iron fence" today, they almost always mean one of three things:

**Ornamental steel (most common):** Factory-welded hollow or solid steel tubes and pickets, powder-coated or galvanized for corrosion protection. This is what 90 percent of residential "wrought iron" fences are made from. Panels come in standard 6-foot or 8-foot widths and predetermined heights. Cost: $20 to $45 per linear foot for materials, $30 to $70 installed.

**Custom fabricated steel:** A local metal shop welds your fence from steel bar stock to your specifications — unique picket spacing, decorative scrollwork, custom gate arches. The materials are similar to ornamental steel but the labor is entirely bespoke. Cost: $50 to $150+ per linear foot installed.

**Ornamental aluminum:** Looks identical to steel from 10 feet away but weighs 40 percent less and never rusts. The trade-off is reduced strength — aluminum bends more easily under impact. Good for decorative front-yard fencing, not great for security or areas where large dogs lean on the fence. Cost: $25 to $55 per linear foot installed.

For most homeowners, pre-manufactured ornamental steel panels offer the right balance of appearance, durability, and cost. Custom fabrication makes sense for short runs (a front entrance or a pool enclosure) where the design is visible and the length is manageable.

**Materials total:** $2,700-$5,820

### Labor

Professional installation of pre-manufactured panels runs $15 to $35 per linear foot for labor alone. For 100 linear feet, that is $1,500 to $3,500. The labor cost is higher than wood or chain link because:

- Steel posts require larger, deeper holes (typically 10 to 12 inches wide and 30 to 42 inches deep)
- Panels must be precisely level — even small deviations are visible on rigid metal
- Attachment brackets must be drilled and bolted to posts, not simply nailed
- Gate installation requires exact plumb to function correctly

**Project total (100 ft, 5 ft tall, one gate):** $4,200-$9,320

## Installation Process for Pre-Made Panels

Most homeowners hire this out, and for good reason. But understanding the process helps you evaluate contractor quality and spot cut corners.

### Layout and Post Holes

Posts are set at exactly the panel width apart — typically 72 or 96 inches on center. There is no fudge room like with wood fencing. If a post is off by an inch, the panel will not mount correctly.

Post holes need to be deeper than wood fence posts. The general rule for ornamental steel is to bury one-third of the total post length below grade. For a 5-foot fence with posts that extend 18 inches below the panel bottom, the post is roughly 7.5 feet total, meaning the hole is 30 inches deep.

### Setting Posts in Concrete

Steel posts go into wet concrete with meticulous attention to plumb and alignment. Most good installers set the corner and end posts first, run a string line between them, and then set line posts to the string. Each post gets checked with a level in two directions.

The concrete should crown above grade to shed water away from the post base. Even galvanized or powder-coated steel will eventually corrode at the soil-concrete-air interface if water pools there.

### Panel Attachment

Pre-made panels typically mount to posts using U-shaped brackets that bolt through the post wall. The panel rails sit in the brackets and are secured with tamper-resistant screws. This is the part where precision matters most — if posts are not exactly parallel, the panels will not sit square, and you will see gaps or stress marks at the brackets.

Some higher-end systems use a welded-in-place approach where the installer field-welds each panel to the posts. This creates a seamless connection but makes future panel replacement much harder. Bracket-mounted systems allow you to replace a damaged panel without cutting.

### Gate Hanging

Gates are the weak point of any iron fence. A 4-foot walk gate weighs 40 to 60 pounds, and a double drive gate can weigh 150+. The hinge posts carry this weight every time the gate swings, plus the lateral force of wind loading.

Good installers set gate posts in oversized holes (14 to 16 inches wide) with extra concrete and often add steel reinforcement — a rebar cross driven through the concrete, or a larger-diameter post. Self-closing hinges with adjustable spring tension are standard for pool enclosures and often required by code.

## The DIY Question

Let me be straightforward: wrought iron fence installation is not a good DIY project for most homeowners. Here is why:

- The panels weigh 50 to 80 pounds each and are awkward to handle alone
- Post placement tolerance is tight — 1/4 inch off and the panel brackets will not align
- Cutting or modifying steel panels requires an angle grinder with a metal cutoff wheel, and every cut needs to be re-coated to prevent rust
- Any welding repairs require equipment and skills most homeowners do not have

**The exception:** If you are installing a short run (under 50 feet) of pre-made panels on flat ground with no gates, and you have a helper, it is doable. The post-setting process is the same as for any fence — dig, plumb, concrete. The panel mounting is just bracket bolting. But keep your expectations realistic about timeline and difficulty.

## Maintenance: The Long Game

The reason wrought iron (and ornamental steel) fences last decades is their maintenance is simple but non-negotiable. Skip it, and rust takes over in 5 to 10 years.

### Annual Inspection

Walk the fence each spring. Look for:
- Chips or scratches in the powder coat or paint — any exposed steel will rust
- Loose brackets or connections
- Rust spots, especially at joints, post bases, and anywhere water sits
- Gate alignment and hinge condition

### Rust Treatment

Small rust spots caught early are a 10-minute fix. Wire-brush the area to bare metal, apply a rust converter (Ospho or naval jelly), let it dry, then spot-prime with a rust-inhibiting metal primer and touch up with matching paint or a clear sealant.

Neglected rust that has eaten into the metal requires grinding, patching (possibly welding), and full refinishing of the affected section. This is where a $5 touch-up becomes a $500 repair.

### Repainting

A full repaint is needed every 10 to 15 years for painted fences, less frequently for powder-coated. The process involves wire-brushing the entire fence, applying primer to any bare spots, and rolling or spraying two coats of exterior metal paint. For 100 feet of fence, budget $300 to $600 in materials or $1,000 to $2,500 if you hire a painter.

According to the [American Wood Council](https://www.awc.org/), one often-overlooked detail is ensuring that any iron fence mounting near wood structures (deck posts, wood retaining walls) includes a barrier to prevent galvanic corrosion between dissimilar materials.

## Pool Fencing Considerations

If your wrought iron fence is doubling as a pool barrier, code requirements are strict and non-negotiable:

- **Height:** Minimum 48 inches in most jurisdictions, measured from the outside grade
- **Picket spacing:** Maximum 4 inches between pickets — no gap large enough for a child's head
- **Climb resistance:** No horizontal elements that create a ladder effect in the first 45 inches
- **Gate:** Must be self-closing and self-latching, with the latch at least 54 inches from the ground or on the pool side behind the fence
- **Clearance:** Maximum 4 inches between the bottom rail and the ground

Not every ornamental steel panel meets pool code. Specifically check that the panel style does not have decorative scrollwork or horizontal rails below 45 inches that could serve as footholds. Your local building inspector will check this, and failing the inspection means removing and replacing non-compliant sections.

## Is Wrought Iron Worth the Premium?

For a front yard, a short decorative run, or a pool enclosure — absolutely. The visual impact is significant, the maintenance is manageable, and the 50+ year lifespan means you install it once and likely never replace it.

For a full-perimeter backyard fence, the math gets harder to justify unless appearance is the top priority. At $80 per linear foot installed for 300 feet, you are looking at $24,000 — a substantial investment that a cedar fence would accomplish for $7,000 to $12,000.

The smart play for many properties is a combination: wrought iron for the visible front and side yards, and a more economical material ([cedar](/blog/cedar-fence-installation-guide-diy-or-hire-out/) or [chain link with privacy slats](/blog/chain-link-fence-installation-costs-and-tips/)) for the backyard. That gets you curb appeal where it counts without blowing the entire budget on fencing nobody sees from the street.
