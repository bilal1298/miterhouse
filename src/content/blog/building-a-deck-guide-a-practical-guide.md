The number-one cause of structural deck failure isn't cheap lumber or undersized joists — it's missing ledger flashing. A $30 piece of Z-flashing installed during construction prevents water from rotting the rim joist behind the ledger board. Skip it, and you're looking at a $2,000–$6,000 repair in 5–8 years. Every other deck-building decision matters less than getting this one right.

That said, a well-built 12×16 pressure-treated deck adds $10,000–$20,000 in home value while costing $2,000–$5,000 in materials for a DIY build. The return makes it one of the best outdoor investments — but only if the structure is sound.

## Before You Buy a Single Board

### Size It for How You'll Use It

- **10×10 (100 sq ft):** A small table and 4 chairs, or a couple loungers. Good for small yards or a secondary deck off a bedroom.
- **12×16 (192 sq ft):** Most popular size. Fits dining for 6 plus a grill area. Enough for most families.
- **16×20 (320 sq ft):** Separate dining and lounging zones. Significant value add but also significant cost, and may trigger stricter permit requirements.
- **Multi-level or wrap-around:** Adds visual interest but bumps complexity, materials, and build time by 40–60%.

### Check Property Requirements

- **Setbacks:** Most zoning codes require 5–15 feet from property lines. Call your local planning department.
- **Easements:** Utility easements may block certain areas.
- **Underground utilities:** Call 811 before digging. Free service, marks all buried lines. Every footing hole is a potential hazard without this step.
- **Drainage:** The deck should not redirect water toward the house foundation.

### Draw a Plan and Pull the Permit

You don't need an architect, but you need dimensioned drawings showing deck size and height, footing locations (6–8 feet apart), beam and joist layout, stair location, railing details, and ledger connection. Many municipalities accept hand-drawn plans for simple decks.

Permits run $100–$500 and include inspections at footings (before pouring), framing (before decking), and final. Don't skip this. Those inspections catch structural mistakes before they become dangerous problems. [IRC code requirements](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) govern deck construction — your local building department checks plans against these.

## Materials and Costs

### Framing

Pressure-treated southern pine is the standard. Everything structural — posts, beams, joists, ledger, rim joists — should be pressure-treated and rated for ground contact (posts, anything within 6 inches of ground) or above-ground use (joists, beams).

**Material list for a 12×16 attached deck:**
- 4×4 or 6×6 posts: 4–6 pieces
- 2×10 or 2×12 beam material: 32–48 linear feet
- 2×8 or 2×10 joists: 12–16 pieces at 12 or 16 feet
- 2×8 ledger board: 16 feet
- 2×8 rim joist: 48 linear feet
- Joist hangers, post bases, lag screws, structural screws
- Concrete for footings: 8–12 bags per footing (tube forms)

**Estimated framing cost:** $800–$1,500

### Decking (for 12×16)

- Pressure-treated: $300–$600
- Composite (mid-range like Trex Select): $600–$1,600
- Composite (premium like TimberTech Pro): $1,200–$2,400
- Hardwood (ipe, cumaru): $1,200–$3,000+

### Railings

- Pressure-treated wood: $200–$500
- Composite kit: $400–$1,200
- Aluminum: $600–$2,000
- Cable: $800–$2,500

### Hardware

[Simpson Strong-Tie](https://www.strongtie.com/) connectors, joist hangers, post bases, and structural fasteners add $200–$500. Don't cut corners here — proper hardware is literally what holds the deck together.

## The Build Sequence

### 1. Footings

Dig to below frost line. Insert sonotube forms, level, pour concrete. Set post bases into wet concrete with bolts positioned exactly per your layout. Let concrete cure 48 hours minimum before loading.

Get footing locations precise. Even an inch off compounds into framing problems. Use string lines between batter boards to mark centers.

### 2. Posts and Beams

Set posts into post bases. Plumb with a level. Cut to the height that puts your beam where it needs to be (deck height minus joist depth minus decking thickness).

Install beams on top of posts. For built-up beams (two 2× boards laminated together), use structural screws or carriage bolts at 16 inches on center.

### 3. Ledger Board

For attached decks: remove siding at the ledger location, flash the area, bolt the ledger to the rim joist with 1/2-inch lag screws or through-bolts at 16 inches on center (staggered high-low), and install Z-flashing above. This is the most critical connection on the entire deck. Get it wrong and water rots the rim joist behind it for years before you notice.

### 4. Joists

Install rim joists to close the frame perimeter. Hang joists between the ledger and beam using joist hangers. Check each joist for crown (the natural bow) and install crown-up. Add blocking at midspan for spans over 10 feet.

### 5. Decking

Start at the outer edge (farthest from the house). Use hidden fastener systems for [composite decking](/blog/composite-deck-building-a-diyers-honest-take/) or face-screw for pressure-treated. Leave 1/8-inch gaps between boards for drainage and expansion. Stagger end joints by at least two joist bays.

### 6. Railings and Stairs

Bolt railing posts through the deck frame to the rim joist or outer joist — never just screw them to the face of the decking. Posts must resist 200 pounds of lateral force.

Cut stair stringers from 2×12 pressure-treated lumber. Keep riser heights consistent (within 3/8 inch) with minimum 10-inch tread depth.

### 7. Finish

For pressure-treated lumber, wait 3–6 months before staining. New PT wood is too wet from the treatment solution. Test readiness by sprinkling water on the surface — if it beads up, wait longer. If it soaks in, you're good to stain.

## Timeline

**DIY (weekends only):**
- Planning, permits, material ordering: 2–4 weeks
- Footings: 1 weekend plus cure time
- Framing: 1–2 weekends
- Decking: 1 weekend
- Railings and stairs: 1 weekend
- **Total: 4–8 weekends**

**Professional:** 3–5 days for a crew of 2–3. Complex multi-level designs: 5–10 days.

Build in early spring or mid-fall. Summer heat makes outdoor construction miserable, and winter freezing delays concrete curing. For specialty builds, see our guide on [custom deck construction](/blog/the-real-deal-on-custom-deck-building/).

## Five Mistakes That Cost Real Money

1. **Not calling 811 before digging.** Hitting a buried gas or electric line is dangerous and expensive.
2. **Undersized footings.** When in doubt, go bigger.
3. **Missing ledger flashing.** The number-one cause of deck failure — and the cheapest to prevent.
4. **Buying lumber without sorting.** Pick through the pile at the yard. Reject boards that are twisted, bowed, or have large knots at stress points.
5. **Not checking for square.** Measure the diagonals of your frame — they should be equal. An out-of-square frame shows the moment decking goes on.

Building a deck is a substantial project, but it's within reach for anyone with basic carpentry skills and the patience to follow code requirements. Don't shortcut the structural connections, give yourself realistic timelines, and you'll end up with outdoor space that pays for itself in property value and daily enjoyment.
