Compression fittings are one of the most reliable and DIY-friendly ways to connect copper, brass, or chromed supply tubes in residential plumbing. They create a watertight seal without soldering, threading, or special tools. If you are connecting a supply line to a shut-off valve, hooking up a faucet, or making a repair on a copper water line, compression fittings are likely your best option. Here is how they work and how to install them correctly.

## How Compression Fittings Work

A compression fitting has three parts: the body (the main fitting piece with internal threads), the compression nut, and the compression ring (also called a ferrule or olive). When you tighten the nut onto the body, it drives the ferrule down against the tube, compressing it into the fitting body. This metal-to-metal contact creates a seal.

The beauty of compression fittings is that they require no heat, no flux, and no special certification. They work on copper, brass, and chromed copper tubing. Some work on plastic tubing with an insert stiffener. They are removable and reusable (with a new ferrule), making them ideal for repair work and connections that may need future service.

## Where Compression Fittings Are Used

You will find compression fittings throughout residential plumbing:

- **Under-sink supply connections:** The supply tubes that run from the shut-off valves to your faucet typically use compression fittings at the valve end
- **Toilet supply lines:** The connection at the shut-off valve is usually a compression fitting
- **Ice maker lines:** The 1/4-inch copper or braided line to your refrigerator connects with compression fittings
- **Shut-off valve installation:** Many straight and angle stop valves use compression connections to the supply pipe
- **Repair couplings:** When you need to splice into a copper line without soldering

Compression fittings are approved for use in accessible locations. Most plumbing codes do not allow them inside walls or ceilings where they would be concealed and inaccessible for inspection. For concealed connections, soldered (sweated) joints or press fittings are the standard.

The [Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)](https://www.cpsc.gov/) maintains safety standards for plumbing products, and compression fittings from major manufacturers meet these requirements. Always buy fittings from established brands rather than unmarked imports.

## Tools and Materials You Need

The tool list for compression fitting installation is short:

- **Two adjustable wrenches** (or two open-end wrenches sized to your fitting). One wrench holds the fitting body stationary while the other tightens the compression nut.
- **Pipe cutter** (for copper tubing) or a fine-tooth hacksaw. A pipe cutter produces a cleaner, squarer cut.
- **Deburring tool or half-round file.** Removing burrs from the cut end is critical for a good seal.
- **Teflon tape (thread seal tape).** Optional but helpful on the compression nut threads to reduce friction during tightening.

That is it. No torch, no solder, no flux, no pipe dope (unless specifically recommended by the fitting manufacturer for your application).

## Step-by-Step Installation

### Preparing the Tube

**Cut the tube square.** A tube cutter rotates around the pipe and produces a perfectly square cut. If using a hacksaw, use a miter box to keep the cut straight. A crooked cut means the ferrule does not compress evenly, which means a leak.

**Deburr the inside and outside.** After cutting, the tube has a sharp burr on the inside edge and sometimes a raised lip on the outside. Remove both. The inside burr restricts water flow and can cause turbulence-related noise. The outside burr prevents the ferrule from seating properly.

**Clean the tube end.** Wipe the last 2 inches of tube with a clean cloth. Dirt, corrosion, or old flux residue interferes with the ferrule seal.

**Verify the tube is round.** If the tube has been kinked, dented, or ovalized, the ferrule cannot compress uniformly. Use a sizing tool or carefully reshape the tube end. If the damage is severe, cut back to a clean section.

### Dry-Fitting the Connection

Before tightening anything, dry-fit the assembly to verify alignment and reach.

1. Slide the compression nut onto the tube (threads facing the fitting body)
2. Slide the ferrule onto the tube
3. Insert the tube into the fitting body until it bottoms out against the internal stop
4. Slide the ferrule down against the fitting body
5. Thread the compression nut onto the fitting body by hand

The tube must reach the internal stop inside the fitting. If the tube is too short, the connection will not seal properly under pressure. Some plumbers mark the tube at the insertion depth so they can verify full insertion during final assembly.

### Tightening the Compression Nut

This is where most DIY mistakes happen. The instinct is to crank the nut as tight as possible. That is wrong. Over-tightening deforms the ferrule beyond its design limits and can actually create leaks or crack the fitting.

**The correct technique:**

1. Hand-tighten the nut until snug
2. Using two wrenches (one on the fitting body, one on the nut), tighten an additional 1/2 to 3/4 turn past hand-tight
3. For brass ferrules on copper tube: 1 to 1-1/4 turns past hand-tight
4. Stop and test before tightening further

The specific number of turns varies by manufacturer, tube material, and fitting size. When in doubt, tighten 1/2 turn past hand-tight, turn on the water, and check for leaks. If you see a drip, tighten 1/8 turn at a time until it stops.

**Never use pipe wrench jaws on compression fittings.** The serrated teeth damage the nut and fitting body. Use smooth-jaw wrenches only.

### Testing for Leaks

Turn the water supply on slowly. Watch the fitting for 2-3 minutes. Run a dry finger around the connection. Even a very slow seep will show as dampness. Check again 15 minutes later and again an hour later. Some micro-leaks only appear once the fitting reaches thermal equilibrium.

If a connection leaks after proper tightening (3/4 turn past hand-tight), do not just keep tightening. Disassemble, inspect the ferrule and tube end, clean everything, and reassemble with a new ferrule.

## Compression Fitting Sizes

Compression fitting sizes refer to the outside diameter (OD) of the tube they connect to, not the inside diameter. Common residential sizes:

| Tube Size (OD) | Common Application |
|---|---|
| 1/4 inch | Ice maker lines, humidifier lines |
| 3/8 inch | Supply tubes (faucet to valve), dishwasher lines |
| 1/2 inch | Main supply lines, shut-off valves |
| 5/8 inch | Larger supply lines (less common residential) |
| 3/4 inch | Main water line connections |

Adapter fittings are available for connecting different sizes (e.g., 1/2-inch compression to 3/8-inch compression) and for transitioning between compression and threaded connections (e.g., 1/2-inch compression to 1/2-inch FIP).

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

**Using compression fittings on plastic tubing without an insert.** Soft tubing (PEX, polyethylene) collapses under the ferrule's compression unless you slide a brass insert stiffener inside the tube first. Without it, the tube deforms and the seal fails.

**Reusing old ferrules.** Once a brass or copper ferrule has been compressed, it has taken a permanent set matching the tube and fitting it was originally tightened on. Reinstalling it on a different fitting or after repositioning rarely produces a reliable seal. New ferrules cost pennies; use them.

**Double-wrench neglect.** Holding the fitting body while tightening the nut prevents the entire assembly from twisting. If the fitting body rotates during tightening, it can stress the tube connection on the other side or misalign the tube. Always use two wrenches.

**Mixing materials.** Brass compression fittings on copper tube work well because the metals are compatible. Brass fittings on galvanized steel pipe create galvanic corrosion that leads to failure. If you are connecting dissimilar metals, use a dielectric union or transition fitting, not a compression fitting.

## Compression Fittings vs. Other Connection Methods

How do compression fittings compare to the alternatives?

**Soldered (sweated) joints:** Stronger and code-approved for concealed locations. Requires a torch, flux, solder, and practice. Better for permanent main-line connections. Not practical for tight spaces near finished surfaces.

**Push-fit (SharkBite):** Fastest installation, no tools required. More expensive per fitting ($5-$15 vs. $1-$5 for compression). Good for emergency repairs and tight spaces. Some plumbers and inspectors are cautious about their long-term reliability in permanent installations.

**PEX crimp/clamp:** The standard for modern residential supply lines. Requires specific PEX tools. Not compatible with rigid copper tubing.

**Threaded connections:** Used for joining pipes with factory-cut threads (galvanized, black iron). Not interchangeable with compression.

Compression fittings occupy the sweet spot: reliable, affordable, no special tools, and easily serviceable. For accessible connections on copper supply lines, they remain the go-to choice.

## When to Call a Plumber

While compression fittings are straightforward, call a plumber if:

- You are working on main water lines larger than 3/4 inch
- The existing piping is corroded, brittle, or galvanized
- You need to make connections inside walls or ceilings (concealed locations)
- You are connecting to gas lines (compression fittings are NOT approved for gas in most jurisdictions)
- Local code requires a licensed plumber for the work scope

[UL (Underwriters Laboratories)](https://www.ul.com/) certifications on plumbing components confirm they meet safety standards. Verify any fittings you purchase carry appropriate listings.

## The Bottom Line

Compression fittings are one of the most accessible plumbing skills a homeowner can learn. The technique is simple (cut square, deburr, assemble, tighten 1/2-3/4 turn past hand-tight, test), the tools are minimal, and the fittings cost a few dollars each. Master this one skill and you can handle supply line replacements, shut-off valve installations, and emergency repairs without waiting for a plumber. Just respect the limits: accessible locations only, no gas lines, and call a pro when you are in over your head.
