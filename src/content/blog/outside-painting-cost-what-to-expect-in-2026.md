Outside painting in 2026 costs $1.50 to $4.50 per square foot of paintable surface for professional work, putting a typical 2,500-square-foot home (exterior surface area) at $3,750 to $11,250. Paint prices are up about 10 percent from 2024, and labor rates have increased 5 to 8 percent in most markets. Here is a clear picture of what you should expect to spend and why.

## 2026 Pricing: What Has Changed

Three factors have shifted outside painting costs upward since 2023:

**Labor rates.** Skilled painting labor is charging $30 to $65 per hour in most US markets, up from $25 to $55 a few years ago. The trades are experiencing a labor shortage, and experienced painters can command higher rates because demand exceeds supply.

**Paint costs.** A gallon of quality exterior paint now runs $40 to $75, compared to $35 to $65 in 2023. Reformulations for better durability and lower VOC content have increased manufacturing costs. The good news is that current formulations genuinely last longer — a 2026 exterior paint job using premium products should outperform the same brand's 2020 product.

**Material costs.** Caulk, primer, masking materials, and sundries have all seen modest increases. These are small individually but add up across a multi-day project.

Despite these increases, outside painting remains one of the most cost-effective home improvements. A $6,000 paint job dramatically improves curb appeal and protects your siding investment.

## Choosing Exterior Paint in 2026

The exterior paint market has three clear tiers, and the differences are not marketing hype — independent testing consistently shows that premium paints outlast economy products by 3 to 5 years.

**Economy tier ($25–$35/gallon):** Adequate for low-priority surfaces like fences, sheds, or rental properties. Coverage requires more coats, color fade is faster, and mildew resistance is limited. Examples: Glidden Exterior, most store brands.

**Mid-range ($40–$55/gallon):** The sweet spot for most exterior projects. Two coats provide solid coverage, good color retention for 7 to 10 years, and decent mildew resistance. Examples: Behr Ultra Exterior, Sherwin-Williams A-100, Benjamin Moore Regal Select Exterior.

**Premium ($55–$80/gallon):** Best-in-class adhesion, color retention, and mildew/algae resistance. Self-priming in many cases (over sound existing paint). Expected life of 10 to 15 years with proper application. Examples: Benjamin Moore Aura Exterior, Sherwin-Williams Duration, PPG Timeless Exterior.

For most homeowners painting their own home, mid-range paint is the right choice. If you are paying a professional crew whose labor costs $4,000+, the incremental cost of upgrading to premium paint ($200 to $400 more for a whole house) is a small percentage of the total — and it means more years before you need to repaint.

## The Prep Cost Reality

Nobody enjoys talking about prep, but it is where your money either saves you or costs you in the long run.

**Pressure washing ($200–$500):** Non-negotiable. Painting over dirt, mildew, or chalking paint is the single most common cause of premature paint failure. A thorough wash followed by 24 to 48 hours of drying is the foundation of every good exterior paint job.

**Scraping ($300–$1,500 in labor):** The most variable cost in exterior painting. A home with paint in good condition — firmly adhered, no peeling — needs only light sanding of glossy areas. A home with extensive peeling needs every loose flake removed. On badly failing paint, scraping alone can take a two-person crew 2 to 3 days.

**Lead paint considerations:** For pre-1978 homes, scraping triggers the [EPA's RRP requirements](https://www.epa.gov/lead/renovation-repair-and-painting-program). Lead-safe practices include ground containment, HEPA vacuuming, specific work methods, and proper disposal. Budget $500 to $2,500 extra for lead-safe work, depending on the extent of disturbance.

**Caulking ($200–$500):** Fresh caulk at every joint between dissimilar materials (siding to trim, around windows and doors, at vent penetrations) seals out moisture and prevents paint failure from behind the surface. This is both a painting prep step and a maintenance task that protects your home's envelope.

**Wood repair ($200–$2,000+):** Rot discovered during prep gets repaired or replaced before painting. Common problem areas: window sills, door thresholds, the bottom edges of siding near the foundation, and fascia boards near gutters. Small repairs are routine; extensive rot repair can significantly increase the project cost.

## Spray vs. Brush-and-Roll

Professional exterior painters use one of two application methods, and the choice affects both cost and quality.

**Spray application:** Faster, covers large flat surfaces efficiently, and provides an even film thickness. Downsides: requires extensive masking of windows, doors, landscaping, and adjacent surfaces; overspray is a real concern on windy days; and spray-only application sometimes misses getting paint into cracks and crevices. Most professional crews spray the body and brush the trim.

**Brush-and-roll:** Slower but works paint into surface texture more thoroughly. Better adhesion in many cases because the brushing action forces paint into pores and grain. Preferred for detail work and trim. Some painters brush-and-roll the entire exterior, which takes longer but can produce a more durable result.

**Back-brushing:** The best of both worlds. The painter sprays a section, then immediately follows with a brush to work the paint into the surface. This is the premium method — it is 20 to 30 percent more expensive than spray-only but delivers superior adhesion and durability.

## Seasonal Pricing and Timing

In 2026, the painting season in most of the US runs from late April through October. Within that window, pricing fluctuates:

**Peak season (June–August):** Highest demand, longest wait times, full pricing. Book 4 to 8 weeks ahead in busy markets.

**Shoulder season (April–May, September–October):** Good painting weather with lower demand. Some contractors offer 5 to 10 percent discounts. The best balance of price and conditions.

**Off-season (November–March):** Some contractors offer 10 to 20 percent discounts for winter scheduling. This only works in mild climates — exterior paint needs temperatures above 50°F during application and for 24 hours after. In northern states, the off-season means interior work only.

The [Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/) has guidance on safe work practices for exterior painting, particularly regarding ladder safety and fall prevention.

## How to Save Without Sacrificing Quality

The most effective ways to reduce outside painting costs:

**Do your own prep.** If you can handle pressure washing, scraping, and caulking, offer to do that work before the painting crew arrives. Some contractors reduce their bid by 15 to 25 percent when prep is already done. Just make sure your prep meets their standards — shoddy prep that the crew has to redo costs everyone.

**Stick to similar colors.** Going from white to white or beige to beige skips the extra primer coat and possible third finish coat that a dramatic color change requires. Staying in the same color family can save 15 to 20 percent on materials and labor.

**Maintain annually.** Touch up chips and recaulk gaps each year. Homes that get annual maintenance need less prep when repaint time comes — saving $500 to $1,500 on the next paint job.

**Book smart.** Schedule during shoulder season for better pricing and faster scheduling. If your project can wait until September, you may save 5 to 15 percent compared to a July booking.
