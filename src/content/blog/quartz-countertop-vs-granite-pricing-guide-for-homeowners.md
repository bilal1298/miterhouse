Quartz and granite are the two most popular premium countertop materials, and their pricing overlaps significantly. Granite runs $50 to $120 per square foot installed; quartz runs $55 to $150 per square foot. For a typical 40-square-foot kitchen, that puts both options in the $2,000 to $6,000 range depending on the specific product you choose.

The price overlap means your decision between quartz and granite rarely comes down to cost alone. It comes down to maintenance tolerance, aesthetic preference, and how you actually use your kitchen. Here is a direct comparison of everything that matters.

## Price Comparison: Head to Head

| Category | Granite | Quartz |
|---|---|---|
| Entry-level (per SF installed) | $50–$75 | $55–$80 |
| Mid-range (per SF installed) | $75–$100 | $80–$110 |
| Premium (per SF installed) | $100–$120 | $110–$150 |
| 40 SF kitchen (entry) | $2,000–$3,000 | $2,200–$3,200 |
| 40 SF kitchen (mid) | $3,000–$4,000 | $3,200–$4,400 |
| 40 SF kitchen (premium) | $4,000–$4,800 | $4,400–$6,000 |

At the entry level, granite is about 10 percent cheaper than quartz. At the premium level, the gap widens — high-end quartz (Cambria, high-end Caesarstone, Silestone Ethereal) can push significantly past granite's ceiling. But in the mid-range where most homeowners shop, the difference is $200 to $400 on a typical kitchen — close enough that it should not be the deciding factor.

## What You Are Paying For

### Granite Pricing Explained

Granite is a natural stone, quarried in slabs. The price is driven by:

**Color rarity.** Granite is graded into levels (sometimes 1 through 5, sometimes labeled by origin). Level 1 includes abundant colors like Uba Tuba (dark green-black), Caledonia (gray speckled), and New Venetian Gold (warm tan). Level 3+ includes rare colors, dramatic veining, and exotic origins. The stone itself has identical physical properties across levels — you are paying for aesthetics, not performance.

**Slab thickness.** Standard is 3cm (about 1.25 inches). Thinner slabs (2cm) cost 15 to 20 percent less but require plywood substrate backing and are less durable.

**Origin.** Brazilian and Indian granites are generally less expensive than Italian or African varieties. Shipping distance affects the price.

### Quartz Pricing Explained

Quartz (the countertop product) is an engineered material — roughly 90 percent ground natural quartz bound with polymer resins and pigments. The price is driven by:

**Brand.** Cambria (made in the US) and Caesarstone (Israel) command premium prices. MSI and Viatera offer competitive products at lower price points. The brand premium is partly quality, partly marketing — compare specific products rather than just brand names.

**Pattern complexity.** Solid colors and simple speckled patterns are the cheapest. Patterns that mimic natural marble (with realistic veining) require more complex manufacturing and cost 30 to 50 percent more.

**Thickness.** Standard is also 3cm. Some brands offer 2cm for lower price points.

## Performance Comparison

This is where the decision usually gets made.

### Durability

**Granite:** 6 to 7 on the Mohs hardness scale. Resists scratching from knives and everyday use. Can chip if hit hard on an edge. Can crack at weak points (around sink cutouts, unsupported overhangs) under extreme stress.

**Quartz:** Slightly softer than granite but more flexible due to the resin binder. More resistant to chipping and cracking. Virtually impossible to scratch in normal kitchen use.

**Winner:** Tie. Both are excellent for daily kitchen use. Quartz has a slight edge in crack and chip resistance; granite has a slight edge in scratch resistance.

### Heat Resistance

**Granite:** Extremely heat resistant. You can set a hot pan directly on granite without damage — it is an igneous rock formed at temperatures far exceeding anything in a kitchen.

**Quartz:** The polymer resin in quartz softens at around 300 degrees Fahrenheit. A hot pan placed directly on quartz can leave a permanent discolored or warped mark. Trivets or hot pads are mandatory.

**Winner:** Granite, decisively. If you routinely pull pans from the oven and set them on the counter, granite is the safer choice.

### Stain Resistance

**Granite:** Porous. Unsealed granite absorbs liquids — coffee, wine, oil — and stains. Sealed granite resists staining for 6 to 12 months before the sealer needs refreshing. Light-colored granites stain more noticeably than dark.

**Quartz:** Non-porous. Liquids sit on the surface and wipe off. No sealing ever required. Quartz resists staining from virtually everything in a normal kitchen.

**Winner:** Quartz, decisively. If you want zero maintenance related to staining, quartz is the clear pick.

### Maintenance

**Granite:** Seal annually (some colors need it more frequently). Clean with mild soap and water — no harsh chemicals. Resealing takes 15 minutes and costs $15 to $30 for a bottle of granite sealer that covers the entire kitchen.

**Quartz:** No sealing. Clean with soap and water. Avoid harsh chemicals (bleach, abrasive cleaners) that can dull the finish over time. That is it.

**Winner:** Quartz. Less maintenance over the life of the counter.

### Appearance

**Granite:** Every slab is unique. Natural veining, mineral deposits, and color variations mean no two kitchens look the same. You select your specific slab at the yard. This is a pro if you want a one-of-a-kind surface and a con if you want consistency between sections.

**Quartz:** Engineered for consistency. Every slab of a given pattern matches. Modern manufacturing produces remarkably realistic marble and stone looks. The latest patterns from Cambria, Caesarstone, and Silestone are difficult to distinguish from natural stone without touching them.

**Winner:** Subjective. Granite offers authentic natural variation; quartz offers controlled aesthetics and the ability to mimic materials (like Calacatta marble) at a fraction of the cost and maintenance.

## Total Cost of Ownership Over 15 Years

Cost is not just the installation price. Factor in maintenance and potential replacement.

| Cost Factor | Granite | Quartz |
|---|---|---|
| Installation (40 SF mid-range) | $3,500 | $3,800 |
| Sealer ($25/year x 15 years) | $375 | $0 |
| Professional resealing (every 5 years) | $300 | $0 |
| Chip repair (estimated once) | $200 | $100 |
| **15-year total** | **$4,375** | **$3,900** |

Over 15 years, quartz's higher upfront cost is offset by zero maintenance — making the total cost of ownership roughly equal. In the mid-range, there is no meaningful financial argument for one over the other.

## Resale Value

Both materials are equally desirable to buyers. Real estate agents consistently report that quartz and granite kitchens sell at comparable speeds and price premiums. The [Remodeling Magazine Cost vs. Value report](https://www.remodeling.hw.net/cost-vs-value/2025/) shows mid-range kitchen remodels returning 60 to 75 percent of their cost, with countertop material being a significant factor.

If you are renovating specifically for sale, choose the material and color that matches current buyer preferences in your market. In 2026, white and gray quartz with marble-look patterns is the strongest trend in most US markets. But trends rotate — in some regions, natural granite with warm tones is making a comeback.

## Making the Decision

**Choose granite if:**
- You regularly place hot pans on the counter without trivets.
- You want a genuinely natural, one-of-a-kind surface.
- You do not mind spending 15 minutes a year on sealing.
- Budget matters and you are happy with a Level 1 color ($50 to $75/SF is hard to beat for natural stone).

**Choose quartz if:**
- You want zero maintenance — no sealing, period.
- You prefer consistent color and pattern across all sections.
- You want a marble look without marble's problems (staining, etching, maintenance).
- You are comfortable using trivets for hot cookware.

**Either works if:**
- Your budget is $3,000 to $5,000 for a typical kitchen.
- You plan to stay in the home 5+ years.
- You want a durable, attractive surface that holds up to daily use.
