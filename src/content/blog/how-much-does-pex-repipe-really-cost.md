If your home has galvanized steel pipes, they're corroding from the inside right now. The rust you see at joints is the tip of the iceberg — the real damage is the scale narrowing your pipes to a fraction of their original diameter, choking your water pressure a little more each year. And if you have polybutylene pipes (gray, blue, or black flexible tubing marked "PB"), insurance companies are increasingly dropping coverage for homes that still have them. A planned repipe costs $4,000 to $15,000. An emergency repair after a catastrophic pipe failure — with water damage, hotel stays, and remediation — costs multiples of that.

PEX (cross-linked polyethylene) is the go-to replacement material for good reason: it costs 30 to 50 percent less than copper, installs faster, resists corrosion, and handles freeze-thaw cycles without bursting as easily.

## Why Homes Need Repiping

Repiping isn't a cosmetic upgrade. It's driven by a real problem or a countdown to one:

**Galvanized steel pipes** (common in homes built before 1970) corrode from the inside out. Gradually declining water pressure, rusty water when you first turn on the faucet, or visible corrosion at joints — any of those signs means the clock is running. These pipes have a useful life of 40 to 70 years, and most are well past it.

**Polybutylene pipes** (installed from the mid-1970s through the mid-1990s) are a known failure-prone material. They degrade when exposed to chlorine in municipal water, leading to sudden, catastrophic leaks. If your home has gray, blue, or black flexible pipes marked "PB," replacement is overdue — not optional.

**Copper with pinhole leaks** can indicate that your local water chemistry is corroding the pipes. One pinhole leak is a patch job. Recurring pinhole leaks in different locations mean the system is failing, and spot repairs are throwing money at a deteriorating asset.

## PEX Material Costs

PEX tubing itself is inexpensive — that's one of its biggest advantages:

| PEX Type | Cost per Linear Foot | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| PEX-A (Uponor/Wirsbo) | $0.75–$1.50 | Most flexible, easiest to work with, expansion fittings |
| PEX-B (Standard) | $0.40–$0.90 | Most common, crimp fittings, slightly stiffer |
| PEX-C (Irradiated) | $0.50–$1.00 | Less common, properties between A and B |

For a 1,500-square-foot home with 2 bathrooms, a kitchen, and a laundry room, you'll need roughly 250 to 400 linear feet of PEX tubing depending on layout. The material cost for tubing alone runs $150 to $500.

Fittings, manifolds, and connectors add another $200 to $600. A PEX manifold system — where individual lines run from a central manifold to each fixture, like a circuit breaker panel for plumbing — costs more in material but simplifies future maintenance and lets you shut off water to individual fixtures without affecting the rest of the house.

## Labor: Where the Real Money Goes

Material costs are almost irrelevant compared to labor. A licensed plumber charges $45 to $85 per hour in most markets, and a whole-house repipe takes 2 to 5 days depending on:

- **Foundation type.** Homes with basements or crawl spaces are cheaper to repipe because pipes are accessible from below. Slab foundations require either tunneling under the slab or rerouting pipes through the attic and walls — both add significant labor.
- **Number of fixtures.** Each fixture (sink, toilet, shower, tub, dishwasher, washing machine, hose bibs) needs its own connection. A 1-bathroom home with 6 fixtures is a different job than a 3-bathroom home with 15 fixtures.
- **Wall access.** Plumbers need to cut into walls to access existing pipes and route new ones. Drywall repair after the repipe is either included in the plumber's quote or billed separately — always clarify upfront.
- **Permit and inspection requirements.** Most jurisdictions require a plumbing permit for a repipe ($100 to $300), and the inspection means the plumber may need to leave walls open until the inspector signs off, adding a day or two.

## PEX vs. Copper: The Real Comparison

Copper repiping costs 40 to 100 percent more than PEX for the same home. A job that costs $7,000 in PEX would run $10,000 to $14,000 in copper. The material cost difference is significant, but the bigger factor is labor — copper requires soldering every joint, which takes substantially longer than PEX crimp or expansion connections.

**Where copper still wins:**
- Outdoor exposure (PEX degrades in UV light)
- Some local codes still require copper in certain applications
- Perception — some buyers in high-end markets expect copper

**Where PEX wins:**
- Cost (30–50% savings over copper)
- Installation speed (fewer connections, flexible routing)
- Freeze resistance (PEX expands slightly without bursting, unlike copper)
- Corrosion resistance (unaffected by acidic water that attacks copper)
- Quieter operation (no water hammer)

The [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) accepts PEX for residential water distribution in all standard applications. It's code-compliant everywhere in the US, though some local amendments may have specific requirements about type (PEX-A vs. PEX-B) or fitting methods.

## The Slab Foundation Challenge

If your home sits on a concrete slab, repiping gets more expensive because existing pipes run through or under the slab. You have two main options:

**Tunneling** means digging under the slab to access and replace buried pipes. This preserves the existing pipe routing but costs $1,500 to $4,000 in additional tunneling fees. It's less disruptive to the interior of the home.

**Rerouting through attic and walls** means abandoning the under-slab pipes entirely and running new PEX lines through the ceiling and down through walls to each fixture. This avoids disturbing the slab but requires more wall penetrations and drywall repair. Hot water lines in the attic also need insulation in cold climates.

Most plumbers now recommend the reroute approach for slab homes because it puts all pipes in accessible locations for future maintenance. Tunneling solves the immediate problem but leaves you with pipes in an inaccessible location again.

## Can You DIY a PEX Repipe?

PEX is one of the more DIY-friendly plumbing materials. The tools are simple — a PEX crimping tool ($50 to $80), a tubing cutter ($10 to $15), and the fittings. If you're handy, replacing individual runs or adding a fixture connection is within reach.

A whole-house repipe is a different matter. It involves:
- Working with the water main shutoff for extended periods
- Cutting into multiple walls and ceilings
- Understanding proper support and routing to avoid kinks
- Pressure testing the entire system before closing walls
- Passing a plumbing inspection

If you're experienced with plumbing and comfortable with the scope, you could save $3,000 to $6,000 in labor. If this is your first plumbing project, hire a pro and focus your DIY energy on the [drywall repair and patching](/blog/plumbing-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/) afterward — that's $500 to $2,500 in savings that requires no plumbing knowledge.

The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) has information on home improvement financing if a whole-house repipe exceeds your cash reserves. A plumbing failure is an emergency; a planned repipe is a controlled investment — and financing a planned project is always cheaper than financing [an emergency repair](/blog/emergency-plumbing-repairs-a-homeowners-guide/).

## How to Save Money on a PEX Repipe

- **Do your own drywall repair.** This is $500 to $2,500 of the total that requires no plumbing knowledge.
- **Bundle with other work.** If you're already [renovating a bathroom](/blog/plumbing-water-leak-repair-guide-what-to-know/) or kitchen, adding pipe replacement to the open walls saves the plumber from cutting access holes separately.
- **Choose PEX-B over PEX-A** unless your plumber recommends PEX-A for your application. The material cost difference is real, and for most residential repipes, PEX-B performs identically.
- **Get a manifold system.** It costs slightly more upfront but eliminates fittings inside walls, reducing potential leak points and simplifying future repairs.
- **Schedule in the off-season.** Plumbers are busiest in summer and during freeze events. Scheduling a repipe in early spring or late fall may get you better pricing and faster scheduling.

## What to Expect During the Work

A whole-house PEX repipe takes 2 to 5 working days:

**Day 1:** Cut access holes in walls and ceilings. Begin removing old pipe where it can be accessed. Install the manifold (if using a manifold system). You'll be without water for portions of this day.

**Days 2–3:** Run new PEX lines to each fixture. Connect to the manifold or main trunk lines. This is the core of the work and where most of the labor cost lives.

**Day 4:** Pressure test the entire system. The inspector comes to verify the work before walls are closed. Connect to fixtures and water heater. Restore water service.

**Day 5 (if needed):** Close walls, patch drywall, and clean up. Some plumbers include this; others don't.

You'll need water shut off for at least 4 to 8 hours on the first day and intermittently on subsequent days. Plan accordingly — fill containers for drinking water and flushing toilets, and schedule showers around the work.
