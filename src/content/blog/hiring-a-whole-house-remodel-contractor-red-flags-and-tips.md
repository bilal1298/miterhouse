A whole house remodel is the most complex residential construction project you can take on, and the contractor you hire determines whether it finishes on budget and on time or spirals into a years-long nightmare. Whole house remodels run $100 to $300 per square foot with a general contractor managing the project, putting a typical 2,000-square-foot home at $200,000 to $600,000. The contractor's fee is 15% to 25% of that total, which means you're paying $30,000 to $150,000 for their management. That fee needs to earn itself every day.

The decision comes down to three things: who to hire, how to structure the contract, and which warning signs to act on before you're locked in.

## What a Whole House Remodel Contractor Does

A whole house remodel GC (general contractor) is a project manager who coordinates:

- Demolition sequencing
- Structural modifications (removing walls, opening floor plans, adding or modifying openings)
- Plumbing rough-in and finish
- Electrical rough-in and finish (must comply with [NFPA 70](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70))
- HVAC modifications and ductwork
- Framing and carpentry
- Insulation and drywall
- Flooring installation across multiple rooms and materials
- Kitchen and bathroom renovation
- Painting and finishing
- Fixture and appliance installation
- Permit management and inspection scheduling
- Budget tracking and change order management
- Quality control across all trades

The GC does not perform all of this work personally. They hire and coordinate subcontractors for each trade while maintaining responsibility for the project timeline, budget, and quality.

## Finding Qualified Contractors

### Where to Look

**Architect referrals** are the most reliable source. If you're working with an architect (recommended for whole-house projects), they have contractors they've built with before and trust. These relationships are built on completed projects and are the strongest vetting available.

**Trade organization directories.** The National Association of the Remodeling Industry (NARI) and the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) remodelers council both maintain directories of members with verified credentials.

**Previous project visits.** Ask any prospective contractor to let you visit a completed whole-house remodel. Walk through the finished home looking at fit, finish, and detail quality. If the contractor can't show you a completed whole-house project, they may not have done one.

### Where Not to Look

**Lead-generation websites** that sell your information to multiple contractors simultaneously result in aggressive sales calls, not thoughtful proposals.

**Social media ads** from contractors who spend more on marketing than on project management are a consistent warning sign.

**The cheapest quote** in any batch of three or more is the most likely to result in problems. Whole house remodels have thin margins for error, and a significantly low bid usually reflects missed scope, underqualified subcontractors, or a contractor who will make up the difference in change orders.

## The Vetting Process

### License Verification

Check your state's contractor licensing board for a valid general contractor license. For whole house remodels, you want a contractor licensed for residential general contracting (not just a specialty trade). The [IRC](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) establishes the baseline code that governs the structural, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems your contractor will be modifying.

### Insurance Requirements

For a whole house remodel, verify:
- **General liability:** $2 million minimum (higher than for single-trade projects due to the project scope)
- **Workers' compensation:** Covering all employees and subcontractors on site
- **Builder's risk insurance:** Covers the structure and materials during construction. This may be carried by the contractor or by you; clarify who is responsible.

### Financial Stability

Whole house remodels take 4 to 12 months. Your contractor needs the financial stability to manage cash flow, pay subcontractors, and absorb the normal timing mismatches between your payments and their expenses.

Ask for:
- A bank reference or financial reference
- How many projects they run simultaneously (more than 3 to 4 is a yellow flag for residential specialists)
- Whether they have a line of credit for material purchases

A contractor who goes bankrupt mid-project leaves you with an unfinished house, subcontractor liens, and no recourse. Financial stability matters more on a whole-house project than on any smaller renovation.

### Track Record

Request references from the last 5 whole-house remodel projects. Call every one and ask:
- Was the project completed within 15% of the original budget?
- Was the timeline within 20% of the original estimate?
- How were change orders handled?
- Was the contractor responsive to problems and quality concerns?
- Would you hire them again for a similar scope?

A contractor with excellent references on small bathroom remodels may not be equipped for a whole house project. The skills are related but the complexity is different. Insist on references for comparable scope.

## Structuring the Contract

A whole house remodel contract needs more detail than any other residential construction agreement. The pricing model you choose shapes the entire financial relationship.

### Pricing Models

**Fixed price:** The contractor gives you a total price for the defined scope. You know exactly what you owe. The risk is that the contractor builds a larger contingency into the price to protect themselves. Best when the scope is well-defined and unlikely to change significantly.

**Cost-plus:** You pay the actual cost of materials and subcontractors plus a markup (15% to 25%) for the contractor's overhead and profit. This is more transparent but means your final cost is unknown until the project is done. Best when the scope is uncertain or likely to evolve (common in older homes where hidden conditions are expected).

**Guaranteed maximum price (GMP):** A hybrid where you pay cost-plus up to a ceiling. If costs come in under the ceiling, savings are shared or returned to you. If costs exceed the ceiling, the contractor absorbs the overage. This balances transparency with budget certainty.

### Payment Schedule

Tie payments to completion milestones, not calendar dates:

| Milestone | Payment |
|-----------|---------|
| Contract signing | 5–10% |
| Demolition complete | 10% |
| Framing and structural complete | 15% |
| Mechanical rough-in (plumbing, electrical, HVAC) passes inspection | 15% |
| Drywall, insulation, and interior framing complete | 15% |
| Finish work begins (cabinets, flooring, tile) | 15% |
| Substantial completion | 10% |
| Final inspection and punch list complete | 5–10% |

### Change Order Process

Change orders are inevitable on whole-house projects. The contract must specify:
- Changes require written documentation with cost and timeline impact
- Both parties must sign before work on the change begins
- Change orders that affect structural, mechanical, or electrical systems require updated permit review
- A clear mechanism for resolving disagreements on change order pricing

Without a change order process, disputes accumulate and poison the contractor-homeowner relationship.

## Red Flags Specific to Whole House Remodels

### No Written Communication System

A whole house remodel involves hundreds of decisions, dozens of specification changes, and a daily flow of information. If the contractor communicates only by phone or in-person conversation, critical details will get lost.

Look for contractors who use project management software (BuilderTrend, CoConstruct, or similar) that provides a written record of decisions, selections, change orders, and schedule updates.

### Unwillingness to Phase the Work

Smart remodel sequencing minimizes the period when your house is uninhabitable. A contractor who can't articulate a phasing plan that allows you to live in part of the house during construction (or clearly defines when you need to move out and for how long) hasn't thought through the project logistics.

### Vague Allowances

"Kitchen fixtures — $5,000 allowance" in a contract is meaningless without specifying what the allowance covers. Does it include the sink, faucet, and disposal? Just the faucet? When allowances are vague, the difference between the allowance and the actual cost becomes a surprise change order.

Every allowance should specify exactly what it covers and what price level it targets (builder grade, mid-range, or premium).

### No Pre-Construction Investigation

Older homes hide problems: inadequate structural framing, non-code-compliant electrical, deteriorated plumbing, asbestos insulation, lead paint. A contractor who prices a whole-house remodel from a walkthrough without any exploratory demolition or investigation is pricing based on assumptions rather than conditions.

Budget for and insist on a pre-construction investigation phase where the contractor opens walls at strategic locations to assess hidden conditions before finalizing the budget.

## Living Through a Whole House Remodel

If you stay in the house during construction:
- Designate one zone as your living quarters and seal it from the construction area with heavy plastic and temporary walls
- Expect dust, noise, and disrupted utilities (plan for periods without running water or working kitchen)
- Establish clear start and end times for construction activity each day
- Maintain access to a working bathroom at all times

If you move out:
- Budget $1,500 to $4,000 per month for temporary housing (rental, extended stay, or staying with family)
- A 6-month project with temporary housing adds $9,000 to $24,000 to the total cost
- Visit the site at least weekly to review progress and make decisions

## Timeline Expectations

| Project Scope | Duration |
|---------------|-----------------|
| Cosmetic whole-house (paint, flooring, fixtures) | 2–4 months |
| Moderate remodel (kitchen, bathrooms, flooring, paint) | 4–8 months |
| Full gut renovation (structural changes, new systems) | 8–14 months |

Add 20% to any timeline estimate as a contingency. Weather delays, material shortages, inspection scheduling, and hidden conditions all extend projects. No whole house remodel in history has finished early.

## After the Project

Retain all project documentation: contracts, change orders, permits, inspection reports, material warranties, lien releases from all subcontractors, and as-built drawings showing where pipes and wiring run inside walls.

The [plumbing repair guide](/blog/plumbing-toilet-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/) on our site covers one of the systems your contractor will have touched — having this documentation makes future maintenance far simpler.

A 1-year workmanship warranty from the GC is standard. Walk through the house thoroughly at 6 months and 11 months to identify any issues before the warranty expires. Cracks in drywall joints (from the house settling after renovation), sticking doors, and minor plumbing leaks are common warranty items.
