Blown insulation runs $0.50 to $1.25 per square foot for materials alone and $1.00 to $3.00 per square foot installed by a professional. The exact number you will pay depends on three factors: the material (cellulose vs. fiberglass), the target depth (which ties to your climate zone's R-value requirement), and whether you are doing the work yourself or hiring out.

Here is a precise cost breakdown with the line items that quotes often lump together, so you can see exactly where your money goes.

## Material Cost Per Square Foot: Line by Line

The per-square-foot material cost varies with depth because you are paying for volume, not area. Here is how the math works.

### Blown Cellulose

A standard 25-pound bag of cellulose costs $12 to $16 and covers approximately 40 square feet at a depth of 10 inches (roughly R-38). The coverage changes with depth:

| Target | Depth | Bags per 1,000 sq ft | Material Cost per sq ft |
|---|---|---|---|
| R-30 | 8.5 in | 18–22 | $0.40–$0.55 |
| R-38 | 10.5 in | 22–27 | $0.50–$0.70 |
| R-49 | 13.5 in | 28–35 | $0.60–$0.90 |
| R-60 | 16.5 in | 34–42 | $0.75–$1.10 |

These are material costs only, assuming bag prices of $12 to $16 depending on the brand and retailer. Prices are fairly consistent across the country because cellulose is lightweight and inexpensive to ship.

### Blown Fiberglass

Blown fiberglass comes in larger bags (typically 30 to 35 pounds) and costs $25 to $40 per bag. Coverage per bag at R-49 depth is approximately 25 to 35 square feet.

| Target | Depth | Bags per 1,000 sq ft | Material Cost per sq ft |
|---|---|---|---|
| R-30 | 11 in | 20–25 | $0.55–$0.80 |
| R-38 | 14 in | 25–32 | $0.70–$1.00 |
| R-49 | 17 in | 30–40 | $0.85–$1.25 |
| R-60 | 21 in | 38–48 | $1.05–$1.50 |

Fiberglass requires more depth for the same R-value because its R-value per inch (R-2.2 to R-2.7) is lower than cellulose (R-3.2 to R-3.8). This means more bags per project, which offsets the apparent similarity in per-bag pricing.

## Labor Cost Breakdown

Professional installation labor for blown insulation typically breaks down as:

**Setup and prep (1–2 hours):** The crew sets up the blowing machine, runs hoses to the attic, and prepares the work area. Some of this time is also spent doing a quick assessment of the attic conditions — checking for obstacles, identifying recessed lights, and locating soffit vents.

**Air sealing (1–3 hours, if included):** Sealing penetrations through the attic floor before blowing insulation. This is the most variable part of the job and the most impactful for your energy savings.

**Blowing (2–4 hours for a typical attic):** The actual insulation application. A two-person crew — one running the machine and loading bags, one directing the hose in the attic — can insulate a 1,200-square-foot attic to R-49 in about 3 hours.

**Cleanup (30 min–1 hour):** Picking up, removing equipment, and cleaning any insulation that landed outside the attic space.

Total labor on a standard attic project: 4 to 8 hours for a two-person crew. At a labor rate of $50 to $80 per person per hour, labor costs run $400 to $1,200 for the crew.

For a 1,200-square-foot attic insulated to R-49:

| Cost Component | Cellulose | Fiberglass |

| Labor (2-person crew, 5 hrs) | $500–$800 | $500–$800 |

## What Makes Your Project More or Less Expensive

### Factors That Increase Cost
**Low-clearance attics.** When the attic height drops below 3 feet, the installer is crawling and working much slower. Some areas near the eaves may be only 12 to 18 inches high. This can add 25% to 40% to labor costs.

**Obstacle density.** Attics with lots of HVAC ductwork, cross-bracing, electrical junction boxes, and plumbing vents take longer to insulate thoroughly. The installer has to work around each obstacle rather than making smooth passes.

**Old insulation removal.** If existing insulation is contaminated with rodent droppings, mold, or moisture damage, it needs to come out before new material goes in. Removal typically costs $1.00 to $2.00 per square foot and roughly doubles the project timeline.

**Access difficulty.** A pull-down attic stair makes access easy. A small ceiling hatch that requires a ladder and contortion to enter makes everything take longer.

**Recessed light covers.** Non-IC-rated recessed lights need insulation dams or covers installed before insulation is blown around them. At $10 to $25 per fixture plus installation time, this adds up in homes with many recessed lights.

### Factors That Decrease Cost
**Straightforward attic layout.** An open attic with consistent joist spacing, full standing headroom, and easy access is the fastest and cheapest to insulate. A crew can insulate 1,000 square feet in 2 to 3 hours.

**Bulk material pricing.** Buying 40+ bags often qualifies for contractor pricing or volume discounts at big-box stores, reducing material cost by 10% to 15%.

**Off-season scheduling.** Booking installation in late winter or early summer (off-peak for insulation contractors) may get you a better rate.

**DIY installation.** Doing the work yourself saves the entire labor component — $500 to $1,200 on a typical project. Blown cellulose is the most DIY-friendly option because the loaner machine programs and procedures are well-established at major retailers.

## DIY Cost Savings: Real Numbers

For the same 1,200-square-foot attic to R-49:

| Component | DIY Cost |
|---|---|
| Cellulose (33 bags at $14 avg) | $462 |
| Baffles for soffit vents (20 at $2) | $40 |
| Air sealing materials | $50–$150 |
| Depth markers | $10 |
| PPE (respirator, goggles, etc.) | $30–$50 |
| Blowing machine rental | $0 (free with purchase) |
| **Total DIY** | **$590–$710** |

Compared to $1,470 to $2,480 installed, DIY saves $880 to $1,770. That is a meaningful savings for a project that a reasonably handy homeowner can complete in a single day with a helper.
