Your water heater should cycle on and off throughout the day, not run non-stop like a furnace in a blizzard. When it keeps running without reaching temperature or cycles so frequently that the burner or elements barely get a break, you are wasting energy, shortening the unit's lifespan, and possibly missing a more serious underlying problem. The cause is almost always one of a handful of specific issues, most of which you can diagnose yourself even if you end up calling a pro for the repair.

Fixing a water heater that keeps running typically costs between $0 and $400 depending on the root cause. The energy waste from ignoring it runs $20 to $60+ per month, so even the more expensive repairs pay for themselves quickly.

## Quick Diagnostics: Narrow It Down Fast

Before diving into each possible cause, answer these three questions to save time:

**Is the water getting hot at all?** If you get lukewarm water but never truly hot water, the issue is likely a failed dip tube, undersized unit, or heavy sediment buildup. If the water eventually gets hot but the heater takes an unusually long time, suspect sediment or a failing heating element.

**How old is the unit?** If it is under 5 years old, the usual suspects are thermostat misadjustment, a hidden leak, or external factors (cold location, increased demand). If it is 8 to 12 years old, component wear and sediment accumulation are more likely. Over 12 years, you may be looking at replacement rather than repair.

**Do you hear unusual noises?** Popping, rumbling, or sizzling sounds point to sediment issues. A high-pitched whining or humming from an electric heater suggests a failing heating element. Complete silence from a gas heater that should be firing suggests an ignition or gas valve problem.

## The Top Reasons Your Water Heater Will Not Stop

### Sediment Has Taken Over the Tank Bottom

This is the most common reason a water heater runs longer than it should, and it is the easiest to fix. Every gallon of water that enters your tank carries dissolved minerals. Over months and years, those minerals settle as a layer of calcium carbonate sludge on the tank bottom.

On gas heaters, this layer sits between the burner flame and the water. The heater works overtime because it is heating rock before it heats water. On electric heaters, the lower element gets buried in sediment, reducing its effective surface area and forcing longer run times.

The solution is a thorough tank flush. If you have been [maintaining your heater with annual flushes](/blog/how-often-should-i-flush-my-water-heater-honest-guide/), sediment is probably not your issue. If the heater has never been flushed, this should be your first step — it costs nothing and takes 30 minutes.

### A Lower Heating Element Has Failed (Electric Heaters)

Electric water heaters use two elements — an upper and a lower. They heat in sequence: the upper element heats the top of the tank first (for faster hot water delivery), then the lower element takes over to heat the rest.

When the lower element fails, the upper element alone cannot heat the full tank. The thermostat keeps calling for heat, but only the top third of the tank gets warm. The heater runs and runs without ever satisfying the thermostat.

Test the elements with a multimeter set to continuity. A good element shows continuity; a broken one shows an open circuit. Replacement elements cost $15 to $40 and take about an hour to swap. Shut off the power and drain the tank below the element level before removing it.

### The Thermostat Is Malfunctioning

A thermostat stuck in the closed (on) position never tells the heater to stop. The water gets progressively hotter — potentially dangerously hot — while the heater runs continuously.

Check the actual water temperature at a faucet with a thermometer. If it is significantly above your set point (more than 10 degrees higher), the thermostat is not doing its job.

On electric heaters, there are two thermostats (upper and lower), each behind an access panel on the side of the tank. Replacement is straightforward — remove two wires, unclip the old thermostat, clip in the new one, reconnect the wires. Parts cost $10 to $25. On gas heaters, the thermostat is integrated into the gas control valve, making it a more expensive repair ($100 to $250 for the valve).

### Hot Water Is Leaking Out of the System

A hidden leak anywhere in your hot water system causes the tank to constantly refill with cold water, triggering continuous heating cycles. The tricky part is that the leak may not be obvious.

Check these locations systematically:

- **The TPR valve.** Follow the discharge pipe to where it exits the house. Any dripping means the valve is releasing water. This could indicate excessive tank pressure, a failed expansion tank, or a thermostat malfunction allowing overheating.
- **Under-slab pipes.** If your home is on a slab foundation with embedded hot water lines, a leak underground can be invisible from inside the house. Signs include warm spots on the floor, an unexplained increase in water bills, or the sound of running water when everything is off.
- **Recirculation system.** Homes with recirculation pumps can develop check valve failures that allow hot water to bleed into the cold water system, wasting enormous amounts of energy.
- **Fixtures with cross-connections.** Single-handle faucets and mixing valves in showers can allow hot water to pass into the cold water line when the cartridge fails.

Turn off every hot water fixture in the house, then watch the cold water inlet valve on the heater. If you see the pipe vibrate or hear water flowing, hot water is leaving the system somewhere.

### The Unit Is Simply Too Small

If your household has grown — more people, a new bathroom, a bigger dishwasher — but the water heater has not, it may be running constantly because it genuinely cannot meet demand. A 40-gallon tank serves 1 to 2 people well but struggles with 3 to 4 people taking back-to-back showers.

The solution is either managing demand (staggering hot water use) or [upgrading to a larger unit](/blog/installing-tank-water-heater-what-to-know-first/).

## The Cost of Doing Nothing

A water heater that runs 30% to 50% more than it should adds $20 to $60 per month to your energy bill. Over a year, that is $240 to $720 in wasted energy. On a gas heater, the excess cycling also accelerates wear on the burner assembly, heat exchanger, and flue. On electric heaters, elements fail faster under continuous use.

The [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) sets standards for water heater efficiency and safety. A constantly running heater may indicate it no longer meets the performance specifications it was designed for, which can also create safety concerns around overheating and pressure buildup.

## DIY vs. Professional: An Honest Assessment

**Handle yourself:**
- Adjusting the thermostat ($0)
- Flushing sediment ($0)
- Replacing an electric heating element ($15 to $40)
- Replacing an electric thermostat ($10 to $25)
- Adding insulation ($20 to $40)
- Checking for visible leaks ($0)

**Call a professional:**
- Replacing a gas control valve (gas connections involved)
- Diagnosing under-slab leaks (requires specialized equipment)
- Working on the gas supply line or burner assembly
- Any situation where you smell gas or see signs of carbon monoxide ([find a licensed plumber](/blog/plumbing-companies-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/))

## The Systematic Approach

Start with the free checks: thermostat setting, visible leaks, and listening for unusual noises. Move to the low-cost fixes: flushing sediment, testing elements with a multimeter. If those do not resolve the issue, the problem is likely a failed thermostat, gas control valve, or hidden leak — at which point you have enough diagnostic information to give a [plumber](/blog/home-plumbing-repairs-a-homeowners-guide/) a head start when they arrive, saving you the cost of an exploratory diagnostic visit.

A water heater that runs right costs less, lasts longer, and does not wake you up at 3 AM with a burst pipe or a flooded utility room. The fix is almost always simpler and cheaper than you expect.
