Blow-in insulation costs $1.00 to $3.00 per square foot professionally installed, or $0.50 to $1.25 per square foot for materials if you do the work yourself. The wide range reflects the difference between cellulose (cheaper, higher R-value per inch) and fiberglass (slightly more expensive, better moisture resistance), as well as the depth required for your climate zone.

For a concrete example: a 1,000-square-foot attic insulated to R-49 with blown cellulose costs roughly $550 to $900 DIY or $1,200 to $2,500 with professional installation.

## Why Blow-In Insulation Wins on Value

Blow-in insulation occupies a sweet spot that other insulation types cannot match. It is dramatically cheaper than spray foam, performs better than batts in real-world conditions, and is the only insulation type where DIY genuinely saves significant money without sacrificing quality.

Here is the comparison that matters:

| Insulation Type | Installed $/sq ft | Effective R-49 Cost (1,000 sq ft) | DIY Viable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blown cellulose | $1.00–$2.50 | $1,000–$2,500 | Yes |
| Blown fiberglass | $1.25–$3.00 | $1,250–$3,000 | Yes |
| Fiberglass batts | $0.75–$2.00 | $750–$2,000 | Yes, but quality suffers |
| Open-cell spray foam | $2.50–$5.00 | $2,500–$5,000 | No |
| Closed-cell spray foam | $4.50–$8.00 | $4,500–$8,000 | No |

The batt comparison deserves a closer look. While batts appear cheaper, real-world studies consistently show that batt insulation achieves only 60% to 80% of its rated R-value when installed in typical residential conditions. Gaps, compression, and incomplete contact with the air barrier are nearly universal. Blown-in insulation fills cavities completely and achieves close to its rated performance, making the effective cost per R-value competitive with or better than batts.

## Regional Cost Variations

Geographic location creates real pricing differences, primarily through labor costs.

**Northeast (New England, Mid-Atlantic):** $1.50 to $3.00 per square foot installed. High labor costs, higher demand (colder climate means more insulation needed), and shorter installation seasons push prices up.

**Southeast:** $1.00 to $2.25 per square foot installed. Lower labor costs and lower R-value targets (R-30 to R-38 in most areas) make this the most affordable region for insulation work.

**Midwest:** $1.00 to $2.50 per square foot installed. Moderate labor costs, but higher R-value targets (R-49 to R-60) in northern states increase material quantities.

**Mountain West:** $1.25 to $2.75 per square foot installed. Higher R-value needs combined with growing labor demand from population influx.

**Pacific Northwest and California:** $1.50 to $3.00 per square foot installed. High labor costs and stringent energy codes push prices to the top of the national range.

Material costs are relatively uniform nationwide — a bag of cellulose costs about the same in Maine as in Texas. The regional variation comes almost entirely from labor rates.

## Breaking Down the Per-Square-Foot Number

When a contractor quotes you $2.00 per square foot, here is approximately how that breaks down:

- **Materials:** $0.60 to $0.90 (insulation, baffles, air sealing supplies)
- **Direct labor:** $0.70 to $1.00 (installer wages for the time in your attic)
- **Equipment and overhead:** $0.15 to $0.25 (blowing machine wear, fuel, insurance, office costs)
- **Profit margin:** $0.15 to $0.30

This math explains why DIY saves as much as it does — you eliminate the labor and most of the overhead, cutting the cost roughly in half.

## The Variables That Swing Your Per-Square-Foot Cost

### Target R-Value
This is the biggest variable. Insulating to R-30 requires roughly 9 inches of cellulose. R-49 requires about 14 inches. R-60 requires about 17 inches. More depth means more bags, which means higher material cost per square foot. The difference between R-30 and R-60 is roughly $0.30 to $0.50 per square foot in materials.

### Existing Insulation
If your attic already has 6 inches of insulation (around R-19), you only need to add enough to reach your target. This reduces the material needed by roughly 40% compared to starting from bare joists.

If existing insulation needs removal first (due to contamination, pest damage, or complete degradation), that adds $1.00 to $2.00 per square foot to the total project cost.

### Air Sealing (The Hidden Cost That Matters Most)
Air sealing the attic floor before insulating is the highest-value step in the entire project. Sealing the gaps around wiring, plumbing, ductwork, and other penetrations can improve the insulation's effective performance by 25% to 40%. But it takes 1 to 3 hours of labor and $100 to $300 in materials.

Some contractors include air sealing in their per-square-foot rate. Others quote it separately at $0.25 to $0.75 per square foot of attic area. When comparing quotes, always ask whether air sealing is included — a higher-seeming per-square-foot rate that includes air sealing is a better deal than a lower rate without it.

### Attic Conditions
Low ceilings, poor access, extreme temperatures, and dense obstacle courses (lots of ductwork, wiring, and cross-bracing) all increase the time required and therefore the labor cost. A difficult attic might add 30% to the labor component.

## Calculating Your Project Cost

Here is a straightforward way to estimate your total project cost:

1. **Measure your attic area.** Length times width of the insulated footprint. If your home is 1,400 square feet and one story, your attic is approximately 1,400 square feet.

2. **Determine your target R-value.** Check the [Department of Energy's insulation guide](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/insulation) for your climate zone recommendation.

3. **Assess existing insulation.** Measure the depth and identify the type. Estimate its current R-value (approximately R-3 per inch for most materials).

4. **Calculate the gap.** Target R-value minus existing R-value equals the additional insulation R-value needed.

5. **Multiply area by per-square-foot cost.** Use $1.00 to $2.50 for professional cellulose or $0.50 to $1.00 for DIY cellulose.

**Example:** 1,400-square-foot attic, currently R-19, target R-49, using blown cellulose.
- Additional R-value needed: R-30
- Additional depth: roughly 8 inches
- Professional cost: 1,400 sq ft x $1.25 to $2.00 = $1,750 to $2,800
- DIY cost: 1,400 sq ft x $0.55 to $0.75 = $770 to $1,050

## Making the Investment Worthwhile

Blow-in insulation is one of the few home improvements where the math works clearly in the homeowner's favor. An investment of $1,000 to $3,000 reduces annual heating and cooling costs by $200 to $600 per year in most homes, with a payback period of 2 to 7 years. After that, the savings are pure return.

Federal tax credits (30% of project cost, up to $1,200 for insulation in 2026) and utility rebates can reduce the effective cost by a third or more. The [Energy Star program](https://www.energystar.gov/) maintains current information on available incentives. The [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) sets the minimum insulation standards that your project should meet or exceed.
