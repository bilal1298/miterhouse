Flush your water heater once a year — or every six months if you have hard water. That's the short answer. Skip this $0, 30-minute task and you'll knock 3 to 5 years off a $1,000+ appliance while paying 10 to 15 percent more in energy costs the entire time.

The standard "once a year" recommendation doesn't fit every household, though. Your actual schedule depends on water hardness, heater type, and usage patterns. Here's the reference data and the process, with editorial notes on what matters most.

## Why Sediment Matters

Every time your water heater fills, dissolved minerals — primarily calcium carbonate and magnesium — settle to the bottom of the tank as the water heats. This sediment layer builds over time and causes three specific problems:

| Problem | What Happens | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Reduced efficiency | Sediment insulates water from the burner (gas) or buries the lower element (electric), forcing the system to work harder | 10–15% higher energy bills per the [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/water-heating) |
| Tank noise | Water trapped under the sediment layer boils and creates steam bubbles that pop through — sounds alarming, but it's a symptom of neglect, not imminent explosion | $0 immediate, but accelerates corrosion |
| Internal corrosion | Sediment traps moisture against the tank bottom, accelerating rust from the inside out | $1,000–$2,000 for premature replacement |

**The editorial take:** Half an inch of sediment cuts efficiency by 10 to 15 percent. In a home spending $400 per year on water heating, that's $40 to $60 wasted annually. Over five years of neglect, the wasted energy alone costs more than most plumber service calls. And that doesn't count the shortened tank life.

## Your Flushing Schedule by Water Hardness

Water hardness varies dramatically by location, and it's the single biggest factor in how fast sediment accumulates:

| Water Hardness | Grains per Gallon | Flush Frequency | Regions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard | 10+ GPG (171+ ppm) | Every 6 months | Southwest, Midwest, parts of Southeast |
| Moderate | 3–10 GPG | Annually | Most of the US |
| Soft (or softened) | Under 3 GPG | Every 18–24 months | Pacific Northwest, homes with softeners |

**How to check yours:** Look up your municipal water utility's annual water quality report (available online for every public water system) or pick up a $5 test strip kit from any hardware store. This one piece of information tells you more about your maintenance schedule than anything else.

**If you see white scale buildup on faucets and showerheads,** you have hard water — don't wait for a test to confirm it. Start flushing every 6 months now.

## The Complete Flushing Process

This is a beginner-friendly DIY task. No special skills needed, just a hose and some patience.

### What You Need

- Garden hose long enough to reach a floor drain, driveway, or exterior
- Flat-head screwdriver (some drain valves use this instead of a handle)
- Bucket (optional, for checking sediment quantity)
- Gloves (the water is hot)

### Step by Step

**1. Turn off the heat source.** For gas heaters, turn the thermostat to "pilot" or "vacation." For electric heaters, switch off the breaker. You don't want the burner firing or elements heating while the tank drains.

**2. Turn off the cold water supply.** This is the valve on the pipe entering the top of the heater. Turning it off prevents new water from flowing in while you drain.

**3. Connect a garden hose to the drain valve.** The drain valve is near the bottom of the tank — it looks like a hose bib (outdoor faucet). Route the hose to your drain location. This water is 120 to 140 degrees. Keep pets and children away from the discharge point.

**4. Open a hot water faucet inside the house.** This breaks the vacuum in the system and lets the tank drain freely. The nearest bathroom or kitchen hot faucet works fine.

**5. Open the drain valve.** Water and sediment start flowing through the hose. The first gallon or two will look rusty or cloudy — that's the sediment. If the flow seems weak, sediment may be clogging the drain valve. Close and open it a few times to break up the blockage.

**6. Flush until the water runs clear.** Two options:

- **Full drain:** Let the entire tank empty (takes 20 to 45 minutes for a 40- to 50-gallon tank), then briefly open the cold water supply to stir up and flush remaining sediment. Repeat until the water runs clear. More thorough.
- **Quick flush:** Leave the cold water supply on and drain until the outflow runs clear. Faster but less thorough. Adequate for homes that flush on schedule.

**7. Close the drain valve.** Remove the hose. Close the hot water faucet you opened inside. Turn the cold water supply back on and let the tank fill completely.

**8. Restore the heat source.** For gas, turn the thermostat back to your normal setting (120°F is recommended). For electric, turn the breaker back on. **Important:** Running an electric element with an empty or partially empty tank destroys the element — wait until the tank is full and you can feel warm water at a faucet.

## Troubleshooting Common Problems

**The drain valve won't open or is stuck.** On heaters that have never been flushed, the plastic drain valve can seize. Don't force it — the valve can snap off, releasing 40+ gallons of hot water. Try gently working it back and forth. If it won't budge, call a [plumber for help](/blog/plumbing-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/).

**The drain valve leaks after you close it.** A small amount of dripping after the first flush is common as sediment prevents a complete seal. Try opening and closing the valve several times to clear debris. If it keeps leaking, cap the valve with a brass hose cap ($3 at any hardware store) and plan to replace the valve next time you drain the tank.

**Very little sediment comes out.** If you flush on schedule and have soft water, minimal sediment is normal — you're doing things right. If you have hard water and see minimal sediment, the drain valve may be clogged. The sediment is still in there, just not coming out through the valve.

**The water never clears up.** If the water stays rusty after extended flushing, the tank itself may be corroding internally. That's a sign the anode rod is depleted and the tank lining is compromised. Have a plumber inspect it — you may be looking at [replacement rather than maintenance](/blog/installing-tank-water-heater-what-to-know-first/).

## The Anode Rod: The Other Maintenance Item Nobody Does

While you're thinking about water heater upkeep, check your anode rod. This sacrificial metal rod (usually magnesium or aluminum) corrodes in place of the tank itself. When it's depleted, the tank starts rusting from the inside.

Check the anode rod every 3 to 5 years by removing it from the top of the heater with a 1-1/16 inch socket. If it's less than 1/2 inch in diameter or coated with calcium, replace it. Rods cost $20 to $50 and add years to your tank's life.

**The editorial take:** The anode rod is the single most overlooked component in residential plumbing. A $30 rod every 4 years is the cheapest insurance against a $1,500 tank replacement. Most homeowners have never heard of it, and most plumbers won't mention it unless you ask.

## When Flushing Isn't Enough

If your heater is over 8 years old and has never been flushed, proceed with caution. Years of sediment buildup can be so thick that it blocks the drain valve entirely. Aggressive flushing at this point can also dislodge chunks of sediment that damage the drain valve or clog pipes.

For neglected heaters, a [professional plumber](/blog/emergency-plumbing-repairs-a-homeowners-guide/) can perform a more thorough cleaning using a deliming solution and a shop vacuum to extract sediment through the drain valve opening. This costs $100 to $250 but can salvage a heater that's otherwise heading for early failure.

Per [NFPA electrical safety codes](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70), always ensure your water heater's electrical connections are properly grounded and that you follow the manufacturer's shutdown procedure before performing any maintenance.

## Setting a Reminder

The biggest obstacle to regular flushing is simply forgetting. Set a recurring calendar reminder — January and July work well because they're easy to remember. Each flush takes 20 to 30 minutes and costs nothing. That small investment of time is the single most effective thing you can do to keep your water heater running efficiently and avoid a premature $1,000+ replacement.
