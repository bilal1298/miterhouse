A tank water heater installation is one of those projects that sits right on the line between DIY-possible and call-a-pro-mandatory, depending on your fuel type, local codes, and comfort level with plumbing and gas connections. Getting it wrong is not just inconvenient — a poorly installed water heater can leak hundreds of gallons, produce carbon monoxide, or in rare cases, fail catastrophically. Here is what you need to understand before you start.

A new 40 to 50-gallon tank water heater costs $400 to $1,200 for the unit itself. Professional installation adds $300 to $1,000 for a straightforward swap, or $1,000 to $3,000 if the job involves moving the location, converting fuel types, or bringing the installation up to current code.

## Gas vs. Electric: The Fundamental Choice

This decision affects everything from cost to installation complexity to operating expense.

**Gas water heaters** (natural gas or propane) heat water faster, cost less to operate in most markets, and continue working during power outages. But they require a gas line, combustion air supply, and a flue or vent for exhaust gases. Installation is more complex and typically requires a permit and inspection.

**Electric water heaters** are simpler to install — no venting, no gas connections, no combustion air requirements. They just need a dedicated 240-volt circuit (typically 30 amps for a 40 to 50-gallon unit). They cost less upfront but more to operate in areas where electricity is expensive relative to natural gas.

According to [the Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/water-heating), water heating accounts for roughly 20% of a home's energy budget. Over a 12-year lifespan, a gas heater typically costs $3,000 to $5,000 to operate, while an electric unit costs $4,000 to $7,000, though this varies significantly by region and utility rates.

## Sizing the Tank

An undersized heater runs out of hot water during peak use. An oversized one wastes energy keeping water hot that nobody uses. Here is a practical sizing guide:

- **1-2 people:** 30 to 40 gallons
- **2-3 people:** 40 to 50 gallons
- **3-4 people:** 50 to 60 gallons
- **5+ people:** 60 to 80 gallons (or consider a tankless unit)

First-hour rating (FHR) matters more than tank size. FHR tells you how much hot water the heater can deliver in the first hour of use when starting with a full tank. A 50-gallon tank with a 67-gallon FHR can serve a family of four during a busy morning (two showers, a dishwasher run, and hand washing) without running cold.

## What Installation Actually Involves

### Removing the Old Unit

1. Turn off the fuel supply — gas valve to "off" or electric breaker to "off."
2. [Shut off the cold water supply](/blog/how-to-shut-off-water-heater-without-wrecking-anything/) to the heater.
3. Connect a garden hose to the drain valve and drain the tank. This takes 20 to 45 minutes for a full tank. The water is scalding — route the hose outside or to a floor drain.
4. Disconnect the water supply lines (hot and cold).
5. For gas units: disconnect the gas line and flue.
6. For electric units: disconnect the wiring at the junction box on the heater.
7. Remove the old unit. A full 50-gallon tank weighs about 150 pounds empty — have a helper and a dolly.

### Preparing the Location

Before the new unit goes in, address these items:

**Drain pan.** Code in most areas requires a drain pan under the heater with a drain line routed to a floor drain or the exterior. If your existing installation lacks one, add it now. A pan costs $10 to $25.

**Expansion tank.** If your home has a closed water system (a pressure-reducing valve or check valve on the main), thermal expansion can stress pipes and the water heater tank. An expansion tank ($30 to $80, plus $50 to $100 for installation) absorbs this pressure.

**Seismic straps.** Required in earthquake-prone areas (all of California, parts of the Pacific Northwest, and other zones). Two straps, one in the upper third and one in the lower third of the tank. Cost: $15 to $30 for the kit.

**Code updates.** If your existing installation predates current code, you may need to add or update the temperature-pressure relief (TPR) valve discharge pipe, combustion air venting, or gas line sizing. This is where costs can escalate — bringing an old installation up to code sometimes adds $500 to $1,500 to the project.

### Setting the New Heater

1. Position the new tank on the drain pan. Ensure it is level — shim if necessary.
2. Connect the cold water inlet and hot water outlet. Use dielectric unions if you are connecting copper pipes to a steel tank — this prevents galvanic corrosion.
3. Install the TPR valve (if not pre-installed) and route the discharge pipe to within 6 inches of the floor or to the exterior. The discharge pipe must not be reduced in size, capped, or threaded at the end.
4. For gas units: connect the gas supply using a flexible gas connector (approved for your fuel type), reconnect the flue, and verify the draft with a match or smoke test after startup.
5. For electric units: connect the wiring per the manufacturer's diagram. This is a 240-volt connection — if you are not comfortable with electrical work, this is where to call a licensed electrician.
6. Open the cold water supply and let the tank fill completely before energizing the heater. Running a gas burner or electric element on an empty tank destroys the unit.

## The DIY vs. Professional Decision

Here is my honest breakdown:

**DIY is reasonable when:**
- You are swapping electric for electric, same size, same location
- You have basic plumbing skills and are comfortable with pipe connections
- Your local code allows homeowner self-installation (many areas do)
- The existing installation is already up to code

**Hire a pro when:**
- You are dealing with gas connections (a gas leak is not a DIY learning opportunity)
- You are changing fuel types, locations, or sizes
- The existing installation needs code updates
- You need a permit and your area does not allow homeowner permits for this work
- The old unit is in a tight space that makes maneuvering difficult

For gas installations, [OSHA safety standards](https://www.osha.gov/) and local codes are strict for good reason. An improperly connected gas line or a backdrafting flue can introduce carbon monoxide into your living space. This is one of those areas where the cost of professional installation is genuinely a safety investment.

## What a Professional Install Costs

Here is what you should expect to pay in the current market:

- **Basic swap (same size, type, location):** $300 to $800 for labor, plus the cost of the unit
- **Swap with code updates:** $800 to $1,500 for labor
- **Relocation or fuel conversion:** $1,000 to $3,000+ depending on the scope of new plumbing, gas, electrical, or venting runs

Most plumbers include the removal and disposal of the old unit in their price. If they do not, disposal costs $25 to $75, or you can haul it to a scrap metal recycler yourself.

## Maintenance That Extends the Lifespan

A tank water heater should last 8 to 12 years with proper maintenance. The two most important tasks:

**Annual flushing** removes sediment that builds up on the tank bottom, insulating the water from the burner or element and reducing efficiency. Connect a hose, open the drain valve, and flush until the water runs clear. Takes 15 to 20 minutes. Check out our detailed [flushing guide](/blog/how-often-should-i-flush-my-water-heater-honest-guide/) for the step-by-step process.

**Anode rod inspection** every 3 to 5 years. The anode rod is a sacrificial metal rod that corrodes in place of the tank itself. When it is depleted, the tank starts corroding. Replacement rods cost $20 to $50 and take about 30 minutes to swap with a socket wrench.

## Efficiency Options Worth Considering

If you are already replacing the heater, consider these upgrades per [EPA WaterSense recommendations](https://www.epa.gov/watersense):

**Heat pump water heaters** (hybrid electric) cost $1,200 to $2,500 for the unit but use 60% to 70% less electricity than standard electric resistance heaters. The federal tax credit (currently up to $2,000) can offset most of the price premium. These need installation in a space with adequate air volume and moderate temperatures — a garage or large utility room works well; a small closet does not.

**High-efficiency gas** models with an energy factor of 0.67 or higher (standard is 0.58 to 0.62) cost $100 to $300 more than standard models and save $50 to $100 per year on gas. The payback period is 2 to 4 years.

Getting the installation right the first time — whether you do it yourself or hire a professional — means reliable hot water for the next decade and avoids the kind of failures that turn a utility room into a wading pool.
