Replacing a gas valve typically costs between $150 and $600 for the complete job — parts and labor — when done by a licensed plumber or HVAC technician. The valve itself runs $20 to $200 depending on the type and size, with labor making up the bulk of the bill at $100 to $400 for one to two hours of work.

That range covers the most common residential scenarios: a shut-off valve under a gas range, a control valve on a water heater, or an appliance connector valve on a dryer line. More complex situations — main line valves, buried valves, or corroded connections that need additional pipe work — can push costs higher.

## Types of Gas Valves and Their Costs

Not all gas valves are the same, and the type you need directly affects the price:

**Ball valves** are the most common residential gas shut-off valves. They use a quarter-turn handle and provide a reliable, tight seal. Cost: $15 to $50 for the part. These are what you will find behind stoves, dryers, and at individual appliance connections.

**Gate valves** are older-style valves that use a round wheel handle. They are being phased out in most jurisdictions because they can corrode internally and fail to seal completely. If you have gate valves on your gas lines, replacement with modern ball valves is recommended. Cost: $10 to $30 for the valve, but the job often involves pipe modifications.

**Gas control valves** (on water heaters and furnaces) are integrated components built into the appliance. These are more expensive — $100 to $200 for the part alone — because they are appliance-specific and include thermostat and safety features. Replacing one is really an appliance repair job, not a simple plumbing swap.

**Earthquake/excess flow valves** are code-required in seismic zones. They automatically shut off gas flow if they detect a sudden surge (like a broken line). Cost: $50 to $150 per valve, installed during new construction or repiping.

## What the Labor Covers

When a plumber quotes $200 to $400 in labor for a gas valve replacement, here is what you are paying for:

1. **Shutting off gas supply.** Either at the individual appliance or at the meter if the valve being replaced is the first valve in the line.
2. **Disconnecting the existing valve.** This may involve cutting pipe or breaking corroded fittings.
3. **Preparing the connection.** Cleaning threads, inspecting pipe condition, and checking for corrosion that could cause future leaks.
4. **Installing the new valve.** Using proper gas-rated thread sealant (yellow Teflon tape or pipe joint compound specifically rated for natural gas).
5. **Pressure testing.** After reassembly, the plumber pressurizes the gas line and checks every connection with leak detection fluid or an electronic gas detector. This step is non-negotiable and code-required in most areas.
6. **Relighting pilot lights.** If any appliances had pilot lights or needed to be restarted.

The testing and verification phase is why professional installation matters for gas work. A tiny leak that you cannot smell can accumulate in an enclosed space over days or weeks with serious consequences.

## When Replacement is Necessary

Gas valves do not have a set replacement schedule, but several conditions indicate it is time:

- **The valve leaks.** Even a small hiss or the smell of gas (the mercaptan additive) near a valve handle or connection means immediate replacement. Do not try to tighten it and hope for the best.
- **The valve is stuck.** If a shut-off valve will not turn — or turns but does not actually stop gas flow — it has failed. In an emergency, you need these to work.
- **Visible corrosion.** Heavy rust or green patina on brass valves indicates internal degradation. Replace before it fails.
- **You are replacing the appliance.** If you are installing a new gas range or water heater, replacing the shut-off valve at the same time is good practice and adds minimal cost since the line is already being disconnected.
- **Code compliance.** If your home has old gate valves on gas lines, many jurisdictions now require replacement with ball valves during any permitted work on the gas system.

## DIY vs. Professional: An Honest Assessment

I am going to be direct: gas valve replacement is not a good DIY project for most homeowners. The risks are too high and the cost savings too small.

A licensed plumber charges $150 to $400 for the complete job. Doing it yourself saves maybe $100 to $300 in labor. The valve itself is cheap. What you are paying the plumber for is their license, insurance, gas detection equipment, knowledge of code requirements, and the pressure test that confirms the system is leak-free.

The consequences of a bad gas connection include:
- Gas leaks that can cause explosions or carbon monoxide poisoning
- Failed inspections that require re-work (often at higher cost)
- Insurance complications if an incident traces back to unlicensed work
- Code violations that surface during home sale inspections

Some states and municipalities explicitly require a licensed professional for any gas line work. Check your local building codes before considering DIY.

If you are comfortable with plumbing and have experience working on gas lines, you already know whether this is within your skill set. If you are looking up "how to replace a gas valve" for the first time, hire a pro.

## Finding and Vetting a Gas Line Professional

For gas valve work, you want either a licensed plumber or a licensed HVAC technician (for appliance-specific valves). Here is how to find a good one:

**Ask for their gas work license specifically.** A general plumbing license may not cover gas work in your jurisdiction. Some states require a separate gas fitter certification.

**Verify insurance.** Gas work carries liability that a contractor's general liability policy should explicitly cover. Ask to see a certificate of insurance.

**Get written quotes.** A gas valve replacement is a small job, and many plumbers quote it over the phone based on a description. Get that quote in writing before work begins, including whether it covers the pressure test and any permit fees.

**Check for a permit.** Most jurisdictions require a plumbing permit for gas line modifications. The permit fee ($50-$150) may be included in the plumber's quote or billed separately. Either way, permitted work protects you.

The [FTC's guide on hiring contractors](https://www.consumer.ftc.gov/articles/hiring-contractor) covers the basics of verifying credentials and protecting yourself with written agreements.

## Emergency Situations

If you smell gas or suspect a leak:

1. Do not flip any light switches, use phones, or create any spark source
2. Leave the house immediately
3. Call your gas utility's emergency line from outside or a neighbor's phone
4. Do not re-enter until the utility has cleared the home

Gas utility emergency response is free. They will locate the leak, shut off gas if needed, and advise on repairs. The repair itself is your responsibility (and cost), but the emergency inspection and shutoff are not.

After an emergency, replacement costs may be higher because the plumber is responding urgently and may need to make temporary repairs before a permanent fix. Budget $300 to $700 for emergency gas valve service, compared to $150 to $400 for a planned replacement.

## Preventive Steps

You can extend the life of your gas valves and catch problems early with minimal effort:

- **Exercise each valve annually.** Turn each gas shut-off valve off and back on once a year. Valves that sit untouched for decades are the ones that seize up when you need them most.
- **Check connections visually.** Look for corrosion, moisture, or discoloration around valve bodies and connections every time you clean behind appliances.
- **Install a gas detector.** A plug-in natural gas detector near your furnace and water heater costs $25 to $50 and provides early warning of slow leaks that you might not smell.
- **Know your main shutoff.** Every household member should know where the main gas shut-off is and how to operate it. Keep a gas shut-off wrench near the meter.
