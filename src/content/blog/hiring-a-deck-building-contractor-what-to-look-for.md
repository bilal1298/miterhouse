The worst deck in your neighborhood wasn't built by a bad crew. It was approved by a homeowner who skipped the vetting process and hired whoever called back first.

Hiring a deck building contractor isn't just about finding someone who can swing a hammer. It's about finding someone who understands structural loading, knows your local code, will pull permits without being asked, and won't disappear after the framing goes up. Those things aren't common. They're earned by checking credentials, asking the right questions, and reading contracts carefully before you sign anything.

Here's how to do that properly.

## Start With the Three Non-Negotiables

Before you talk design, price, or materials, confirm three things:

**1. They're licensed in your state.** Go to your state's contractor licensing board and search their name and license number. Verify it's active and hasn't lapsed. Some states require a specific deck or structural license; others issue general contractor licenses that cover deck work. What you're looking for is a current, verifiable credential — not a business card that says "licensed and insured."

**2. They carry both types of insurance.** General liability (minimum $1 million per occurrence) covers damage to your property during the build. Workers' compensation covers their crew if someone gets injured on your site. Without workers' comp, an injured worker can file a claim against your homeowner's policy. Don't accept a contractor's word — ask for a certificate of insurance and call the issuing company to confirm the policy is active. Certificates can be months old, and policies lapse.

**3. They pull their own permits.** Any deck attached to your house almost certainly requires a structural permit. A contractor who says "you don't really need a permit for this" is either wrong about your local code or trying to avoid inspection. Neither is acceptable. An unpermitted deck creates problems when you sell the house, and you're personally liable for bringing it up to code later — not the contractor.

If someone fails on any of these three, stop the conversation. There are enough legitimate contractors that you don't need to rationalize around the basics.

## What Three Quotes Actually Tells You

Get three quotes. Not two, not five. Three gives you a real price range without wasting your time or contractors' time in a competitive bid circus.

Before you reach out, have a clear picture of what you want: rough dimensions, your preferred decking material (or two options you're deciding between), whether you're demoing an existing structure, and any features like stairs, built-in benches, or pergola framing. The more specific you are, the more useful the quotes will be.

When the quotes come back, a legitimate contractor breaks them out: decking materials by species and grade, framing lumber, hardware (joist hangers, ledger bolts, post bases), railing system by brand, labor as its own line, permit fees, demo and site prep, and cleanup. They name the brands. They specify the lumber grade. If someone quotes you "build 16x20 composite deck: $14,800" as a single line item, they've told you nothing useful — and given themselves room to substitute materials later.

If all three quotes land within 10–15% of each other, you have a good read on market rate. If one quote is 30% or more below the others, don't celebrate. Figure out what's different: Are they using a lighter lumber grade? Skipping permit fees? Subcontracting work they didn't disclose? The gap usually means something was left out, not that you found a deal.

## The Questions That Reveal Who You're Actually Hiring

Past the paperwork, the real signal is technical knowledge. Push on the structural specifics.

**Ask how they size their joists and beams for your span.** A competent deck builder can walk you through the span tables — they know that a 2x10 can span roughly 14 feet at 16-inch on-center with southern yellow pine, and less with hem-fir. If they shrug and say "we just do what the inspector approves," that's not confidence-inspiring.

**Ask about footing depth.** Your footings need to go below the frost line for your area — 36–42 inches in the northern half of the country. A contractor who doesn't ask where you are or doesn't know your local frost depth is guessing.

**Ask how they handle the ledger connection.** This is where more decks fail than anywhere else. The ledger — the board bolted to your house — needs proper flashing to prevent water from getting behind your siding and rotting the rim joist. Ask them to describe their flashing detail. If they don't have a specific answer, their decks are going to cause moisture problems within a few years.

**Ask about joist tape.** Self-adhesive joist tape applied to the top edge of every joist is a straightforward detail that dramatically extends deck life. It's not expensive. A contractor who uses it knows what they're doing. One who doesn't or hasn't heard of it is skipping maintenance prevention basics.

## References — What to Actually Ask

Every established contractor can hand you a reference sheet. What you do with it matters.

Call two or three of them. Ask: Did they show up on the scheduled start date? Did the final cost match the quote, or were there extras? Did they handle the permit process themselves? Would you hire them again? That last question — especially the pause before the answer — tells you a lot.

Ask if you can see the completed deck. Photos on a contractor's website are curated. Seeing real work in person reveals finish quality, how the railing posts are mounted, whether the boards are gapped consistently, and what the underframing looks like from the yard.

Better yet, ask to visit a current job site. A clean, organized site — materials stacked neatly, tools put away when not in use, no debris piled at the edge of the property — reflects how a crew thinks about their work. A chaotic site usually produces chaotic work.

## Red Flags Worth Walking Away From

Some patterns reliably predict problems:

**They're available to start immediately, in May.** Deck season in most of the country runs March through September. Legitimate deck contractors are booked 4–8 weeks out during that window. Someone who can start next week in peak season either had a cancellation or has no referrals. A cancellation is fine — just ask why. No referrals is the answer you want to avoid.

**They want more than 20% upfront.** Standard practice is 10–20% deposit at signing, progress payments tied to inspection milestones, and 10–15% holdback at the end. Anyone asking for 50% before materials arrive, or demanding cash only, is either in financial trouble or running a setup for a partial job or no-show.

**They can't explain what happens if there's a problem.** Ask how they handle disputes or warranty claims. A vague answer — "we stand behind our work" — is not a process. You want to know who to call, what the timeline is, and what recourse you have in writing.

**The contract is one page.** A real deck contract should cover scope of work, material specs, total price with itemized breakdown, payment schedule tied to milestones, start and completion dates, the change order process, permit responsibility, cleanup terms, workmanship warranty (minimum one year), and dispute resolution. If what they hand you doesn't hit most of those, negotiate the contract before you sign — or find someone else.

## What the Contract Must Include

Put everything in writing. Every conversation you've had, every material you've agreed on, every timeline you've been promised — if it isn't in the contract, it doesn't exist.

The contract must specify:

- Scope of work in detail (dimensions, framing plan, features, finishes)
- Material specs by brand, type, and grade — not "composite decking" but "Trex Enhance Naturals, Foggy Wharf, grooved edge"
- Itemized price with a clear total
- Payment schedule tied to project milestones, not calendar dates
- Start date and expected completion date (with allowances for weather)
- Change order requirement — any scope or cost change in writing and signed before work proceeds
- Contractor handles permit application, inspections, and closeout
- Cleanup and disposal terms
- Workmanship warranty period
- Dispute resolution process

Both parties sign. Both parties get a copy. No exceptions, and no "we'll sort out the details later."

## Staying on Top of the Project Without Getting in the Way

Once work starts, stay involved without hovering. Check in at each major milestone: after footings are poured and inspected, after framing is complete and inspected, after decking and railing are installed. If something looks wrong at any stage, raise it immediately. Don't wait until the final walkthrough to mention that the stairs feel narrow or the railing posts look misaligned.

Expect change orders during construction — some are unavoidable as site conditions emerge. What's not acceptable is a contractor making scope changes and mentioning them afterward, or presenting a bill for extras that weren't discussed in advance. Your contract's change order clause exists precisely for this.

## The Handoff

When the project is done, don't release that final payment until you have:

- A passed final building inspection
- The closed permit number
- Copies of any material warranties (composite decking, railing systems, and hardware all carry manufacturer warranties)
- The contractor's workmanship warranty in writing
- A clear point of contact for warranty claims

A good deck contractor wants you to have all of this. It protects them as much as it protects you. Anyone who resists providing documentation at closeout is telling you something about how they'll handle a warranty call six months from now.

Keep that contractor's number. A builder you've vetted and worked with successfully is one of the most useful contacts you'll have as a homeowner — whether it's deck maintenance, an addition, or pointing a friend toward someone reliable.
