Professional fence installation is one of those projects where the cost gap between DIY and hiring out is smaller than you might expect — typically 30 to 50 percent, not the 100+ percent markup you see with roofing or electrical work. A pro crew can install 150 feet of fence in 1 to 2 days, while the same project takes a homeowner 3 to 5 weekends. Factor in tool rental, wasted materials, and the learning curve, and the math often favors hiring.

Here is what professional installation actually costs across every common fence type, plus the practical details that help you get maximum value from the money spent.

## Complete Cost Guide by Fence Type

Professional installation pricing includes materials, labor, concrete for post setting, standard hardware, cleanup, and typically one walk gate. Driveway gates, old fence removal, and unusual site conditions are extra.

### Wood Privacy Fence

The most popular residential choice. A standard 6-foot dog-eared cedar or pressure-treated pine privacy fence runs:

- **Materials:** $10 to $20 per linear foot
- **Labor:** $12 to $25 per linear foot
- **Total installed:** $22 to $45 per linear foot

For 150 linear feet with one walk gate: **$3,500 to $7,500**.

Cedar lasts 15 to 20 years but costs 30 to 40 percent more than pressure-treated pine. Pine treated with MCA (micronized copper azole) lasts 10 to 15 years in ground contact and is the budget pick. Board-on-board and shadowbox styles cost 10 to 15 percent more than standard dog-ear due to the extra material.

### Vinyl/PVC Fence

No-maintenance alternative to wood. Higher upfront cost but zero staining or painting.

- **Total installed:** $28 to $55 per linear foot

For 150 linear feet: **$4,200 to $8,250**.

Vinyl fences are inherently weaker in wind than wood or metal. In high-wind areas, request aluminum-reinforced bottom rails and posts. The additional cost is minimal ($3 to $5 per linear foot) and dramatically improves wind resistance.

### Chain Link

The value option for utilitarian fencing — pet containment, property lines, security.

- **Total installed (4 ft):** $15 to $28 per linear foot
- **Total installed (6 ft, vinyl-coated):** $22 to $38 per linear foot

For 150 linear feet of 6-foot vinyl-coated: **$3,300 to $5,700**.

See our detailed [chain link fence installation guide](/blog/professional-chain-link-fence-installation-costs-and-tips/) for specification choices and cost breakdowns.

### Ornamental Iron/Steel

Premium curb appeal with security. Most residential "iron" is actually powder-coated tubular steel.

- **Total installed:** $30 to $65 per linear foot

For 150 linear feet: **$4,500 to $9,750**.

Our [iron fence installation guide](/blog/installing-iron-fence-what-to-know-first/) covers material differences, DIY feasibility, and maintenance expectations.

### Aluminum Ornamental

Looks like iron, weighs less, never rusts. Costs 15 to 25 percent less than steel.

- **Total installed:** $25 to $55 per linear foot

For 150 linear feet: **$3,750 to $8,250**.

### Composite

Engineered wood-plastic composite. Minimal maintenance, consistent appearance, but expensive.

- **Total installed:** $35 to $70 per linear foot

For 150 linear feet: **$5,250 to $10,500**.

## What Drives Professional Costs Up

Beyond the base price per foot, several site-specific factors affect your final bill.

**Old fence removal.** Budget $3 to $8 per linear foot depending on the existing fence type. Chain link is cheapest to remove; wood and concrete-set posts are the most labor-intensive. Some contractors include removal in the quote if you ask — it is worth negotiating.

**Terrain.** Slopes add 15 to 25 percent to labor because panels must be racked or stepped, posts are harder to set at consistent depth on a grade, and the crew works less efficiently on uneven ground. Rocky soil that requires hand-digging or rock drill adds $10 to $20 per post.

**Access limitations.** If the crew cannot get a truck and auger to the fence line — narrow side yards, gated communities with vehicle restrictions, heavily landscaped areas — everything is carried by hand and post holes may be dug manually. This slows production and increases labor cost by 20 to 40 percent.

**Permit and survey costs.** A fence permit runs $50 to $300. A property survey costs $300 to $600. The contractor typically pulls the permit (and includes the fee in the quote), but you are usually responsible for the survey. Get the survey done before requesting quotes — the contractor needs accurate property lines to bid accurately.

**Corner count.** Every corner requires a reinforced post with deeper concrete and sometimes bracing. A simple rectangular lot with 4 corners costs meaningfully less per foot than an irregularly shaped lot with 8 corners. Each corner adds $50 to $150 in materials and labor.

## Getting Accurate Quotes

**Measure your own perimeter first.** Walk the fence line with a measuring wheel or tape measure and count corners, gate locations, and note any slopes or obstructions. This gives you a baseline to evaluate quotes against.

**Request itemized proposals.** A quote that says "$5,800 for fence installation" tells you nothing. A good proposal breaks out materials (by type and specification), labor, gates (each), concrete, permits, and any site-specific charges. This makes comparison between contractors straightforward and reveals where each one is making different assumptions.

**Schedule site visits.** Do not accept phone or email quotes for fence work. A contractor who quotes without seeing the site is guessing about soil conditions, slope, access, and obstacles. That guess will turn into change orders after the work starts.

**Ask the hard questions.** How deep are post holes? What diameter? Concrete in every hole, or just corners? What is the post spacing? What is the warranty — workmanship and materials separately? Are cleanup and debris removal included?

## Timing Your Project

Fence installation has a definite seasonal cycle that affects both scheduling and pricing.

**Peak season (April through September):** Highest demand, longest wait times (3 to 6 weeks for scheduling), and full pricing. If you need a fence before summer, book by March.

**Shoulder season (March, October):** Moderate demand, reasonable scheduling (1 to 3 weeks), and contractors may negotiate 5 to 10 percent off.

**Off-season (November through February):** Lowest demand. Many contractors offer 10 to 20 percent discounts to keep crews working. The trade-off is weather-related delays and frozen ground that may complicate post setting. In mild climates, this is the sweet spot for savings.

If your project is not time-sensitive, mentioning schedule flexibility to contractors often gets you a better price — they can fit you in between larger jobs or during slow periods.

## DIY vs. Professional: When Each Makes Sense

**DIY makes sense when:**
- You have the time (3 to 5 weekends for 150 feet).
- The terrain is flat and accessible.
- You are installing a simple fence type ([woven wire](/blog/installing-woven-wire-fence-what-to-know-first/), basic [electric](/blog/electric-fence-installation-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/), or straightforward wood picket).
- You already own or can borrow a post-hole auger.

**Professional makes sense when:**
- The fence line has slopes, corners, or difficult access.
- You are installing iron, vinyl, or composite (tight tolerances, heavy materials).
- The project exceeds 200 linear feet (time commitment gets significant).
- Permit requirements are complex.
- You value your weekends more than the 30 to 50 percent DIY savings.

## Protecting Your Investment

After installation, a few simple practices extend fence life significantly:

- Stain or seal wood fences within 6 months of installation, then every 2 to 3 years.
- Keep soil and mulch from contacting the bottom of wood posts (it accelerates rot).
- Trim vegetation away from all fence types — it traps moisture and adds wind load.
- Tighten hardware annually (gate hinges, rail brackets, fence clips).
- Address damage promptly — a single loose board or leaning post stresses adjacent sections and the problem cascades.

A properly installed and maintained residential fence adds real value to your property. Industry estimates put the return on investment at 50 to 65 percent for quality fencing. More importantly, it adds daily function — privacy, security, pet containment — that you enjoy for the life of the fence. Spend the money on correct installation upfront and the fence pays for itself in avoided repairs and premature replacement.
