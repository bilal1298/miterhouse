A general contractor for a kitchen remodel typically charges 15-25% of the total project cost as their management fee, which means on a $40,000 kitchen renovation, you are paying $6,000 to $10,000 specifically for the contractor to coordinate the project. That fee covers scheduling subcontractors, pulling permits, managing materials, handling inspections, and solving the inevitable problems that come up behind your walls.

Whether that is worth it depends entirely on the contractor you hire. A great GC saves you money by preventing mistakes and keeping the project on schedule. A bad one costs you double through delays, poor workmanship, and change-order games. Here is how to tell the difference before you sign anything.

## What a General Contractor Actually Does on a Kitchen Remodel

A kitchen remodel touches nearly every trade: demolition, framing, electrical, plumbing, HVAC (if you are relocating a vent or adding a range hood), drywall, painting, flooring, cabinetry, countertops, tile, and finish carpentry. A GC coordinates all of those trades in the right sequence.

The sequencing matters more than most homeowners realize. Electrical rough-in has to happen before drywall closes up the walls. Countertop templates cannot happen until cabinets are fully installed and level. Flooring installation needs to happen either entirely before cabinets go in or entirely after — not half and half.

A GC who has done dozens of kitchen remodels knows this sequencing instinctively. A homeowner managing subs directly is learning it for the first time, usually through expensive mistakes.

## GC Fees: How the Numbers Break Down

General contractors structure their fees in a few ways:

**Fixed-price contracts (most common for kitchens):** The GC quotes a total price that includes all labor, materials, and their markup. Their profit is built into the number. This gives you cost certainty but less transparency.

**Cost-plus contracts:** You pay the actual cost of all labor and materials, plus a percentage (usually 15-20%) or a flat management fee. More transparent, but your total cost is uncertain until the project is complete.

**Hybrid approach:** Fixed price for defined scope, cost-plus for anything outside the original scope. This is increasingly common and often the fairest structure for both parties.

On a typical mid-range kitchen remodel:

| Cost Component | Percentage of Total | Dollar Range |
|---|---|---|
| Cabinets and installation | 30-40% | $9,000-$20,000 |
| Countertops | 10-15% | $3,000-$8,000 |
| Appliances | 10-15% | $3,000-$7,000 |
| Labor (all trades) | 20-30% | $6,000-$15,000 |
| GC overhead and profit | 15-25% | $5,000-$12,000 |
| Permits and fees | 1-3% | $300-$1,500 |

The GC's margin is how they stay in business, pay for insurance, maintain their license, and cover their overhead between projects. A suspiciously low bid often means the contractor is cutting corners on insurance, using unlicensed subs, or planning to make it up through change orders.

## The Red Flags That Matter

After watching dozens of kitchen remodels go sideways — including one of my own early on — these are the red flags that actually predict problems:

**Demands large upfront payment.** A reasonable deposit is 10% of the project cost, or the cost of materials that need to be ordered. Any contractor asking for 30-50% upfront is either cash-flowing other projects with your money or is financially unstable. Both are bad.

**No written contract or vague scope.** The contract should specify exactly what work is included, what materials are being used (brand, model, color), the payment schedule tied to milestones, the estimated timeline, and the process for handling changes. "Kitchen remodel per discussion" is not a scope of work.

**Cannot or will not pull permits.** This is non-negotiable. Work done without permits creates legal liability for you, can void your homeowner's insurance, and will cause problems when you sell. The [NEC electrical code](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70) requires permits for any new circuits or electrical modifications — a kitchen remodel almost always involves electrical work.

**Pressures you to decide immediately.** "This price is only good today" is a sales tactic, not a business practice. A legitimate contractor's pricing is based on actual costs, and those do not change overnight.

**No references from recent kitchen projects.** Ask for 3-5 references specifically from kitchen remodels completed in the last two years. Call them. Visit at least one finished kitchen if possible — ideally one that has been lived in for a year, not just photographed the day it was completed.

**Significantly lower bid than all other quotes.** If you get three quotes at $42,000, $45,000, and $38,000, the range is normal. If you get one at $28,000, something is missing from that bid. Either the scope is different, they are planning change orders, or they are underinsured.

## Green Flags That Signal a Good GC

**They ask detailed questions during the estimate.** A good contractor spends 60-90 minutes in your kitchen during the initial visit, opens cabinet doors, checks under the sink, looks at your electrical panel, and asks about your daily routines. They are building a real understanding of the project, not just measuring and leaving.

**Clear communication style.** They return calls or emails within 24 hours, explain their process unprompted, and tell you honestly when something is outside their wheelhouse.

**Licensed, bonded, and insured — and they prove it.** Ask for their license number and verify it with your state's licensing board. Ask for a certificate of insurance showing both general liability (minimum $1 million) and workers' compensation coverage. A legitimate contractor provides this without hesitation.

**Defined change-order process.** Changes happen on every kitchen remodel. The contractor should have a written change-order process: what constitutes a change, how pricing is determined, and how approval works before the work is done. This prevents the most common source of disputes.

**They manage expectations honestly.** A contractor who tells you "6 weeks, maybe 8 depending on the countertop fabricator and inspection scheduling" is being honest. One who promises "4 weeks, guaranteed" on a full kitchen gut is selling you something.

## How to Structure the Bidding Process

Get a minimum of three bids. Here is how to make them actually comparable:

1. **Create a detailed scope document** before contacting any contractors. List every element: cabinet brand/line or quality level, countertop material, appliances (specific models if you have chosen them), flooring type, backsplash material, lighting fixtures, and exactly which walls or features are changing.

2. **Give every contractor the identical scope.** When contractors bid on different scopes, you cannot compare their prices meaningfully. The cheapest bid might just be the least complete.

3. **Ask each contractor to itemize their bid.** You want line items for demolition, framing, electrical, plumbing, cabinets, countertops, flooring, tile, painting, and their management fee. Some will resist itemizing — push for it anyway. You need to see where the money is going.

4. **Compare the allowances.** Many bids include "allowances" for selections you have not made yet (lighting fixtures, tile, hardware). Check what each contractor is assuming. A bid with a $500 lighting allowance and one with a $2,000 allowance are not apples to apples.

## The Contract Must Include These Items

Do not sign a contract without these elements:

- **Detailed scope of work** with specific materials, brands, and models
- **Total price** or fee structure with complete transparency
- **Payment schedule** tied to completion milestones, not dates
- **Start date and estimated completion date** with a reasonable buffer
- **Change-order process** including markup percentage on changes
- **Warranty terms** — both on workmanship and materials
- **Cleanup expectations** — daily cleanup, final cleanup, debris removal
- **Insurance certificate** attached to the contract
- **Permit responsibility** explicitly assigned (it should be the GC)
- **Dispute resolution clause** — mediation before litigation

For homes built before 1978, the contract should also address [EPA lead paint requirements](https://www.epa.gov/lead). Your contractor must be EPA Lead-Safe certified for any work that disturbs painted surfaces in pre-1978 homes.

## GC vs. Self-Managing Subcontractors

Some homeowners consider hiring individual subcontractors directly to save the GC markup. This can work, but understand what you are taking on:

**You become the project manager.** You are scheduling 5-8 different tradespeople, resolving conflicts when the electrician needs access at the same time as the plumber, and making real-time decisions about issues discovered during construction.

**You are responsible for permit coordination.** You pull the permits, schedule the inspections, and ensure every sub's work passes code.

**Warranty and liability land on you.** When the countertop installer chips the new backsplash tile, you are negotiating between two subs who each blame the other. A GC absorbs that liability.

**Realistic savings: 10-15%** if you manage well. But if poor scheduling adds two weeks to the project, you may lose those savings to extended appliance rentals, eating out costs, and the value of your own time.

Self-managing works best for smaller, simpler kitchen updates — [countertop replacements](/blog/kitchen-countertop-install-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/), cabinet refacing, or cosmetic refreshes. For a full gut remodel involving multiple trades, a good GC is usually worth the markup.

## Payment Schedule That Protects You

Structure payments around completed milestones, not calendar dates:

1. **10% at contract signing** (deposit)
2. **20% at completion of demolition and rough framing**
3. **25% at completion of rough electrical and plumbing (after inspection pass)**
4. **20% at completion of cabinet installation**
5. **15% at substantial completion** (countertops, flooring, appliances installed)
6. **10% held back for 30 days after final completion** (punch list hold-back)

That final 10% hold-back is your leverage to ensure the contractor finishes every detail on the punch list. Without it, you are calling and emailing for weeks trying to get them back for touch-ups and adjustments.

## Questions to Ask References

When calling a contractor's references, go beyond "were you happy with the work?" and ask:

- How closely did the final cost match the original estimate?
- Were there any significant delays, and how did the contractor handle them?
- How did the contractor handle unexpected problems discovered during construction?
- Was the job site kept clean and safe?
- How was communication throughout the project?
- Is there anything you would change about the experience?
- Would you hire them again without hesitation?

The answers to the unexpected-problems question and the communication question tell you the most. Every remodel hits snags — it is how the contractor handles them that separates the professionals from the amateurs.

## The Bottom Line

Hiring a general contractor for a kitchen remodel is one of the bigger financial decisions you will make as a homeowner. Take the time to vet thoroughly, get detailed bids, and insist on a comprehensive contract. The contractor who earns your trust during the bidding process — through transparency, knowledge, and honest communication — is almost always the right choice, even if they are not the cheapest option.

Your kitchen remodel will take 6-12 weeks and cost $25,000 to $75,000 or more. The contractor you choose determines whether that experience is stressful or smooth. Invest the time upfront to get this decision right.
