Remodeling the kitchen and bathroom together costs $30,000–$80,000 for most mid-range projects, and doing them simultaneously (or at least planning them as one project) saves 10–20% compared to tackling each room separately. The savings come from shared contractor mobilization, overlapping material orders, and coordinated plumbing and electrical work. Here's how to plan a combined kitchen and bath remodel that stays on budget and timeline.

## Why Combine Kitchen and Bath Remodeling

The practical benefits of bundling:

**Shared demolition and disposal costs.** A dumpster rental ($300–$500 for a 10-yard) serves both rooms. Demo labor for both rooms in one mobilization is cheaper than two separate trips.

**Coordinated plumbing work.** If both rooms need plumbing updates — and in homes over 20 years old, they usually do — the plumber prices a package deal lower than two separate service calls. Expect to save $500–$1,500 on plumbing labor by combining.

**Coordinated electrical work.** Same principle. Bringing an electrician out once for both rooms versus twice saves a trip charge ($75–$150) and allows more efficient panel work if both rooms need new circuits.

**General contractor discount.** A larger project justifies a contractor assigning a dedicated crew rather than fitting your small bathroom job between other projects. The overhead percentage (typically 15–25% of project cost) may come down 2–5 points on a combined project.

**Living disruption consolidation.** You're without a functioning kitchen for 2–4 weeks during a kitchen remodel anyway. Adding a bathroom remodel during that same window means one period of disruption instead of two.

## Sequencing: Which Room Goes First

**Bathroom first** is usually the right call. Here's why:

The bathroom is smaller, faster to complete, and less disruptive to daily life. Starting there lets the crew establish a rhythm, reveals any hidden issues (rotten subfloor, outdated plumbing) early when there's still budget flexibility, and gives you one completed room to feel good about while the kitchen is torn apart.

Most contractors complete the bathroom in 1–2 weeks, then shift to the kitchen for 3–5 weeks. Total project timeline: 5–8 weeks for both rooms at a mid-range scope.

**Exception:** If the kitchen and bathroom share a plumbing wall (common in many floor plans), the plumber may want to open both sides simultaneously to run new lines efficiently. In that case, both rooms get demoed at the same time, with finish work proceeding room by room.

## Common Design Decisions That Affect Both Rooms

### Flooring Continuity

Using the same flooring in the kitchen and bathroom creates visual cohesion, especially if they're near each other or visible from the same sightline. Porcelain tile and luxury vinyl plank both work in wet environments and come in styles suitable for both rooms.

**Cost savings:** Ordering one material for both rooms means larger quantity, potentially reaching a volume discount tier. Plus, the installer sets up once rather than learning two different materials.

### Hardware and Fixture Finishes

Choose one metal finish family for both rooms. If the kitchen gets brushed nickel faucet and cabinet pulls, the bathroom should match or complement. This doesn't mean identical products — just the same finish tone.

Buying hardware for both rooms at once also lets you take advantage of bulk pricing. A set of 40 cabinet pulls (enough for both rooms) often costs less per piece than two separate orders of 20.

### Lighting Temperature

A subtle but important coordination point. If you install 3000K (warm white) LED lighting in the kitchen and 4000K (cool white) in the bathroom, the difference is jarring when moving between rooms. Pick one color temperature and use it consistently. For most homes, 2700K–3000K feels natural and flattering.

## The Plumbing and Electrical Reality Check

Kitchen and bath remodels almost always require plumbing and electrical upgrades in homes built before 2000. Budget accordingly.

**Plumbing minimums:**
- New shut-off valves under every sink ($10–$20 each, plus $50–$100 labor)
- Supply line replacement if copper shows corrosion or polybutylene is present ($200–$500 per fixture)
- New drain connections if relocating any fixture ($150–$400 per drain)
- Water heater capacity check — a bigger kitchen sink and a rain showerhead may exceed your water heater's recovery rate

Code compliance per the [IRC plumbing standards](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) means your plumber must bring the work up to current code, even if the original installation was code-compliant at the time.

**Electrical minimums:**
- GFCI protection on all kitchen and bathroom receptacles (required by [NEC](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70))
- Dedicated 20-amp circuits for kitchen countertop receptacles (minimum two circuits)
- Dedicated circuit for dishwasher, garbage disposal, refrigerator, and microwave
- Bathroom exhaust fan on a switch or humidity sensor (code-required in bathrooms without operable windows)
- Under-cabinet lighting circuit in kitchen

If your existing electrical panel is full, adding a sub-panel ($500–$1,000) or upgrading the main panel ($1,500–$3,000) may be necessary to accommodate new circuits.

## Where Combined Remodeling Goes Wrong

**Scope creep.** "While we have the walls open..." is the most expensive phrase in remodeling. Set a firm scope before starting and require any additions to be priced and approved in writing before work proceeds.

**Cash flow problems.** Two rooms means materials and labor invoices arriving simultaneously. Payment schedules matter. Structure payments as milestone-based: 10% at signing, 30% at rough-in completion, 30% at fixture installation, 30% at final walkthrough. This keeps cash flow manageable and gives you leverage if issues arise.

**Decision fatigue.** A combined project requires selecting twice as many materials, fixtures, and finishes in a compressed timeline. Do your material selection before the project starts — not during. Delays caused by waiting for a homeowner to pick a backsplash tile cost $200–$500 per day in idle labor.

**Living arrangements.** Without a kitchen, you're eating out or using a makeshift cooking setup (coffee maker, microwave, hot plate in the dining room). Without a bathroom, you need a second bathroom in the house. If your home has only one bathroom, either sequence the work so the toilet is functional each evening or rent a portable restroom ($100–$200/month).

## Financing a Combined Project

The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) recommends comparing these options:

- **Home equity loan:** Fixed rate, lump sum. Best for projects with a firm budget.
- **HELOC:** Variable rate, draw as needed. Better for phased projects where total cost is less certain.
- **FHA 203(k) loan:** Rolls renovation costs into the mortgage. Works for purchase-and-renovate scenarios.
- **Personal loan:** Higher rates but no home equity required. Suitable for smaller combined projects under $30,000.
- **Contractor financing:** Convenient but read the terms — deferred interest can be expensive.
