Hot water is the simplest and cheapest first step for unclogging a drain — it costs nothing, takes 5 minutes, and works on about 30–40% of slow or partially clogged drains. Boiling or near-boiling water dissolves grease, soap scum, and minor organic buildup that causes most kitchen and bathroom sink blockages. But it's not a universal fix, and using it incorrectly can damage certain types of plumbing. Here's exactly when it works, when it doesn't, and what to try when hot water alone isn't enough.

## When Hot Water Works (and Doesn't)

### Works well on:
- **Grease and fat buildup.** Kitchen sinks that drain slowly due to cooking grease coating the inside of the drain pipe. Hot water melts the grease and flushes it downstream.
- **Soap scum accumulation.** Bathroom sinks and tub drains where soap residue narrows the pipe over time.
- **Minor organic matter.** Food particles, toothpaste, and similar soft materials that partially obstruct the drain.
- **Seasonal cold-weather slowdowns.** Drains that run through uninsulated exterior walls can slow down in winter as grease solidifies faster in cold pipes. Hot water warms the pipe and restores flow.

### Doesn't work on:
- **Hair clogs.** Hair doesn't dissolve in hot water at any temperature. A bathroom sink or tub drain clogged with hair needs physical removal.
- **Mineral scale buildup.** Hard water deposits inside pipes don't respond to hot water. These require chemical treatment or mechanical cleaning.
- **Object blockages.** A dropped earring, toy, or other solid item blocking the drain won't dissolve. You need to retrieve it.
- **Tree root intrusion.** Roots growing into sewer lines require professional cutting. No amount of hot water helps.
- **Complete blockages.** If water isn't draining at all, the clog is too dense for hot water alone. You need a plunger, snake, or professional service.

## The Correct Hot Water Method

Not all pipes handle boiling water equally. Start by identifying your drain pipe material.

### For Metal Pipes (Copper, Brass, Cast Iron)

These handle boiling water without issues.

1. Boil a full kettle or large pot (at least half a gallon)
2. Remove the drain stopper or strainer
3. Pour boiling water directly down the drain in 2–3 stages, waiting 5–10 seconds between pours
4. Wait 5 minutes for the water to work on the blockage
5. Run the hot tap for 30 seconds to flush loosened material
6. Repeat up to 3 times if drainage is improving but still slow

### For PVC or ABS Plastic Pipes

**Do not pour boiling water into PVC pipes.** PVC pipe is rated for a maximum of 140°F continuous use. Boiling water (212°F) can soften PVC joints and loosen cement connections, especially at the trap and first few connections below the sink. Over time, repeated boiling water use can cause PVC joints to fail, leading to leaks inside walls — a far more expensive problem than the original clog.

Instead:

1. Run the hot tap at full temperature (typically 120–140°F from the water heater) for 2–3 minutes
2. Turn off the tap and wait 5 minutes
3. Repeat
4. If needed, heat water on the stove to roughly 150°F (hot but not boiling — you should be able to touch the pot briefly without burning) and pour that down the drain

Most homes built after 1980 use PVC drain pipes. If you're not sure what material your drains are, assume PVC and use the lower-temperature approach. The [IRC plumbing code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) specifies temperature limits for different pipe materials — PVC schedule 40 (the most common residential drain pipe) should not routinely receive water above 140°F.

## Boosting Hot Water With Common Household Items

When hot water alone isn't quite getting the job done, these additions improve effectiveness:

### Baking Soda + Vinegar + Hot Water

The classic DIY drain cleaner. Despite what social media suggests, the chemical reaction between baking soda and vinegar is fairly mild — it's not dissolving the clog so much as the fizzing action helps loosen debris while the hot water flush pushes it through.

1. Pour 1/2 cup baking soda down the drain
2. Follow with 1/2 cup white vinegar
3. Cover the drain opening with a wet rag or plate to contain the fizzing action inside the pipe
4. Wait 15–30 minutes
5. Flush with hot water (appropriate temperature for your pipe type)

Cost: approximately $1.00. Effectiveness: moderate. Works well on soap scum and light grease. Won't touch hair clogs.

### Dish Soap + Hot Water

For grease clogs specifically, dish soap is more effective than baking soda and vinegar because it's designed to emulsify (break down) grease.

1. Squirt a generous amount (2–3 tablespoons) of dish soap directly into the drain
2. Wait 10 minutes for the soap to reach the greasy buildup
3. Follow with hot water — pour slowly and steadily for 1–2 minutes
4. Run the hot tap for another 2 minutes

Cost: pennies. Effectiveness: good for kitchen grease specifically. This should be your first approach for a slow kitchen sink.

### Salt + Hot Water

Salt acts as a mild abrasive and draws moisture from organic materials (which can help break down certain types of buildup).

1. Pour 1/2 cup table salt down the drain
2. Follow with hot water
3. Wait 15 minutes
4. Flush with more hot water

Cost: negligible. Effectiveness: mild. Works as a maintenance treatment more than a clog buster.

## When to Escalate Beyond Hot Water

If three rounds of hot water treatment don't restore normal drainage, it's time for more aggressive approaches:

### Step 1: Plunger ($5–$15)

A flat-bottom cup plunger (not the flanged toilet plunger) creates suction and pressure that dislodges clogs in the trap and nearby pipe. Fill the sink with 2–3 inches of water, place the plunger over the drain, and plunge vigorously 15–20 times. If you have a double-basin sink, plug the other drain with a wet rag to maintain pressure.

### Step 2: Remove and Clean the Trap ($0)

Place a bucket under the P-trap (the U-shaped pipe under the sink). Unscrew the slip nuts by hand or with channel-lock pliers. Pull the trap down and dump the contents. You'll often find the clog right here — a mass of hair, soap, or food debris. Clean the trap, reinstall, and test.

This takes 10 minutes and costs nothing. It also solves about 60% of sink clogs that hot water doesn't fix.

### Step 3: Drain Snake ($10–$30 for a hand snake)

Feed a 25-foot drain snake (also called an auger) into the drain opening or through the wall pipe after removing the trap. Crank the handle while pushing forward until you hit resistance — that's the clog. Continue cranking to break through or grab the blockage, then pull it out.

A hand-cranked drain snake handles most household drain clogs within 25 feet of the drain opening. For deeper clogs, a power auger ($50–$100 to rent, or $200–$400 to buy) extends 50–100 feet.

### Step 4: Call a Plumber ($150–$400)

If DIY methods fail, the clog is likely deeper in the main drain line, involves tree roots, or indicates a more systemic plumbing issue. Professional drain cleaning costs:

- **Basic snake service:** $150–$250
- **Motorized auger (main line):** $200–$400
- **Camera inspection + cleaning:** $250–$500
- **Hydro-jetting (serious blockages):** $350–$600

## What NOT to Use on Clogged Drains

**Chemical drain cleaners (Drano, Liquid-Plumr).** These products contain sodium hydroxide (lye) or sulfuric acid, which generate heat and chemical reactions to dissolve organic clogs. The problems:

- They can damage older pipes, especially cast iron with existing corrosion
- They don't work on hair clogs (the main culprit in bathroom drains)
- They create a chemical hazard if you later need a plumber to work on the drain — the residue is dangerous
- They're terrible for septic systems — the chemicals kill the beneficial bacteria your septic tank needs
- They work slowly and often incompletely, requiring repeated applications that compound pipe damage

The plumbing industry almost universally advises against chemical drain cleaners. A $10 drain snake does the job better, faster, and without damaging your pipes or the environment. The [EPA's WaterSense program](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) focuses on water conservation but also promotes plumbing maintenance practices that protect infrastructure.

## Preventing Drain Clogs: The $0 Maintenance Plan

Prevention is genuinely easier than any cure:

**Kitchen sinks:** Never pour cooking grease down the drain. Let it cool and solidify in a container, then trash it. Run hot water for 30 seconds after each use to flush residual grease. Use a sink strainer ($3–$5) to catch food particles.

**Bathroom sinks:** Install a drain hair catcher ($3–$8) and clean it weekly. The TubShroom or similar mushroom-style drain catches work well in tubs. Remove collected hair before it migrates into the drain pipe.

**Monthly maintenance:** Once a month, flush each drain with the dish soap + hot water method described above. This prevents grease and soap buildup from accumulating to the point of causing a slow drain.

**Annual maintenance:** Remove the P-trap under each sink once a year and clean it. Takes 10 minutes per sink and catches buildup before it becomes a clog.
