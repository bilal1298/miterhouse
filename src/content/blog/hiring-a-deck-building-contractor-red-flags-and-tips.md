Hiring the wrong deck building contractor costs more than building the deck twice. Bad contractors leave behind structural problems that are invisible until the deck starts failing — sagging beams, undersized footings, ledger boards bolted into nothing. Finding a good one requires asking the right questions and recognizing the warning signs that most homeowners miss until the deposit is gone.

Here is how to vet deck contractors, what the red flags look like, and how to structure the deal to protect yourself.

## Where to Find Deck Contractors

Skip the lead-generation sites that sell your contact information to whoever pays. Instead, start with these sources that have some built-in accountability:

**Local building material suppliers.** Lumber yards that sell to contractors know which builders do quality work and which ones return material because they measured wrong. Ask the counter staff who they would hire.

**Your building department.** Many permit offices maintain lists of licensed contractors who regularly pull permits in the jurisdiction. These builders have a track record of code compliance.

**Neighbors with decks you admire.** Walk the neighborhood. If you see a deck that looks well-built and well-maintained, knock on the door and ask who built it and whether they would hire them again.

**Trade associations.** The North American Deck and Railing Association (NADRA) maintains a directory of members who have agreed to build to code and follow industry standards.

For information on what permits your project will need, our [deck building permits guide](/blog/the-real-deal-on-deck-building-permits/) walks through the requirements.

## Vetting Contractors: The Non-Negotiable Checks

Before you invite anyone to your property for a quote, verify these three items.

### License

Check your state's contractor licensing board for a valid, current license. In states that do not license general contractors at the state level, check for a local business license and any required trade registrations. An unlicensed contractor operating in a state that requires licensing is already cutting a corner that matters.

### Insurance

Request a certificate of insurance showing:
- **General liability** with a minimum of $1 million per occurrence. This covers damage to your property during construction.
- **Workers' compensation** covering all employees. If an uninsured worker falls off your deck frame and gets injured, you can be liable.

Call the insurance company listed on the certificate to verify it is current. Expired or fabricated certificates are not uncommon.

### References

Ask for 5 to 10 references from the last 2 years, not just 3 cherry-picked ones from 5 years ago. Call at least 3. Ask:
- Was the project completed on the agreed timeline?
- Were there unexpected cost increases?
- Did the contractor pull the permit and schedule inspections?
- Would you hire them again?

Visit at least one completed project in person if possible. Look at the underside of the deck (framing quality, hardware, connections) not just the top.

## Getting Accurate Quotes

Request detailed, written quotes from at least three contractors. A useful quote itemizes:

| Line Item | What to Expect |
|-----------|---------------|
| Materials (decking) | Listed by product, quantity, and unit price |
| Materials (framing, hardware) | Itemized separately from decking |
| Labor | Listed as per-square-foot rate or total hours |
| Permit fee | Specific dollar amount |
| Demolition (if replacing) | Per-square-foot rate |
| Subfloor/grading prep | Described and priced |
| Railing and stairs | Separately priced with material specified |
| Cleanup and disposal | Included or separate |
| **Total** | **Everything above, clearly totaled** |

A quote that says "Deck construction: $12,000" with no breakdown is not a quote. It is a guess.

### Comparing Quotes Fairly

The lowest price is not automatically the best value. When one quote is significantly lower than the others, check:

- Are they using the same materials? Substituting thinner decking or fewer hardware connectors drops cost and quality.
- Is subfloor prep included? A low quote may assume a perfectly prepared site.
- Is the permit included? Some contractors do not pull permits (which is a red flag in itself).
- What is the railing specification? Railing can be 20% to 30% of the total cost. Downgrading railing materials is a common way to lower a bid.

[Simpson Strong-Tie](https://www.strongtie.com/) publishes minimum hardware requirements for code-compliant deck connections. Compare what your contractor specifies against these minimums.

## Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

### No Permit, No Problem

Any contractor who suggests skipping the permit to save time or money is telling you they either do not build to code or do not want their work inspected. Walk away. Unpermitted decks create liability exposure, insurance problems, and resale complications that far exceed any permit savings.

### Large Upfront Deposits

A reasonable deposit is 10% to 25% of the total project cost, or the cost of materials if the contractor is buying them on your behalf. A demand for 50% or more upfront, especially in cash, is a classic scam pattern.

### No Written Contract

If the contractor will not put the scope, price, timeline, and payment schedule in writing, they are preserving their ability to change any of those things later. No contract means no recourse.

### Pressure to Decide Quickly

"I can only hold this price until Friday" is a pressure tactic, not a legitimate constraint. Material prices do not change weekly, and good contractors have enough work that they do not need to manufacture urgency.

### No Physical Address or Permanent Signage

A contractor operating out of a cell phone number, a PO Box, and an unmarked truck may not be around when you have a warranty claim. Established contractors have a physical shop or office, lettered vehicles, and a web presence that predates your project.

### Cash-Only Payment

Legitimate businesses accept checks, credit cards, or bank transfers. Cash-only payment avoids paper trails and makes disputes unresolvable.

## Structuring the Contract

A good deck building contract includes these elements:

**Scope of work.** Every element of the project described in enough detail that both parties agree on what "done" means. Include materials by brand and specification, dimensions, railing type, stair count, and any features like lighting or built-in seating.

**Price and payment schedule.** Total price with a payment schedule tied to milestones, not dates. Example:
- 15% deposit at contract signing
- 25% when footings pass inspection
- 25% when framing passes inspection
- 25% when deck is substantially complete
- 10% after final inspection and punch list completion

Tying payments to inspections protects you because the work must pass code review before you pay for it.

**Timeline.** Start date and estimated completion date with a reasonable allowance for weather delays. A 300-square-foot deck should take a professional crew 1 to 2 weeks.

**Change order process.** How changes to the scope or materials are handled, including who initiates, how they are priced, and that both parties must sign off before work proceeds.

**Warranty.** Minimum 1-year workmanship warranty. Many quality contractors offer 2 to 5 years on structural work. Material warranties come from the manufacturer, not the contractor.

**Permit responsibility.** The contract should state that the contractor will pull the permit, schedule all inspections, and obtain the final inspection sign-off.

**Cleanup and disposal.** Explicitly state that the contractor is responsible for daily cleanup and final debris removal.

## During Construction: What to Watch

You do not need to supervise every nail. But periodic checks protect your investment.

**Check footing depth before concrete is poured.** This takes 2 minutes and prevents the most common structural shortcut.

**Verify the ledger connection.** The ledger should be bolted through the house rim joist with flashing above it. If you see the ledger lag-screwed into siding without penetrating the structural framing, stop the job and discuss.

**Count joist hangers.** Every joist-to-beam and joist-to-ledger connection should have a metal hanger. If you see joists simply resting on beams or toe-nailed to the ledger, that is below code.

**Attend inspections.** Be present when the building inspector visits. Listen to any notes and confirm the contractor addresses them.

## After the Project

**Walk the punch list.** Before the final payment, walk the entire deck with the contractor. Note any incomplete items, visible defects, or deviation from the plans. Agree on a timeline to address each item. Withhold the final payment until the punch list is complete.

**Get the permit closed.** Confirm that the final inspection has been scheduled and passed. Request a copy of the inspection report or the closed permit for your records. An open permit (not finalized) creates the same problems as no permit.

**Save everything.** Keep the contract, all change orders, payment receipts, permit documents, inspection reports, material warranties, and the contractor's insurance certificate. Store these with your home records. You will need them when you sell the house.

**Review the contractor.** If they did good work, leave an honest review. Good deck contractors are hard to find, and referrals are how they sustain their business. If they did poor work, document it and file a complaint with your state's contractor licensing board.
