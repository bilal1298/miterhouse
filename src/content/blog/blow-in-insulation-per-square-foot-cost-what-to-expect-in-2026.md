Blow-in insulation costs $1.00 to $2.50 per square foot installed professionally, or $0.50 to $1.40 per square foot for DIY installation. The exact number depends on the material (cellulose vs. fiberglass), the target R-value depth, and your geographic location. For most attic projects in 2026, the sweet spot is blown cellulose at $1.20 to $1.80 per square foot — balancing cost, performance, and ease of installation.

Here is how to calculate the per-square-foot cost for your specific project, compare materials, and decide between DIY and professional installation.

## Per-Square-Foot Pricing: The Complete Picture

The per-square-foot cost is not a single number — it scales with depth. Blowing insulation to R-30 requires less material than R-49 or R-60. Here is what each target depth actually costs:

### Blown Cellulose

| Target R-Value | Depth Needed | Bags per 1,000 sq ft | Material Cost/sq ft | Installed Cost/sq ft |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R-30 | ~10 inches | ~20 bags | $0.55–$0.80 | $1.00–$1.60 |
| R-38 | ~13 inches | ~25 bags | $0.70–$1.00 | $1.20–$1.80 |
| R-49 | ~17 inches | ~32 bags | $0.90–$1.30 | $1.50–$2.20 |
| R-60 | ~21 inches | ~40 bags | $1.10–$1.60 | $1.80–$2.50 |

Each bag of cellulose covers approximately 30 to 50 square feet at R-30 depth (varies by manufacturer and settling factor). Bags cost $10 to $14 each at retail.

### Blown Fiberglass

| Target R-Value | Depth Needed | Bags per 1,000 sq ft | Material Cost/sq ft | Installed Cost/sq ft |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R-30 | ~13 inches | ~22 bags | $0.65–$0.90 | $1.10–$1.70 |
| R-38 | ~16 inches | ~28 bags | $0.80–$1.15 | $1.30–$2.00 |
| R-49 | ~21 inches | ~36 bags | $1.00–$1.50 | $1.60–$2.40 |
| R-60 | ~26 inches | ~44 bags | $1.25–$1.80 | $2.00–$2.80 |

Fiberglass bags typically cost $12 to $18 each. The material requires more depth than cellulose to achieve the same R-value, which is why the per-square-foot cost is higher despite similar bag pricing.

## Cellulose vs. Fiberglass: Which to Choose

Both materials work well for attic floors. The differences are practical, not dramatic:

**Choose cellulose if:**
- Budget is the primary concern (lower cost per R-value)
- Your attic has many obstructions (pipes, wires, irregular framing) — cellulose flows around them better
- You want the environmental angle (recycled content)
- Pest resistance matters (borate treatment deters insects and rodents)

**Choose fiberglass if:**
- Moisture is a concern in your attic — fiberglass does not absorb water
- You want slightly less settling over time
- Weight matters (fiberglass is lighter, putting less load on ceiling joists)

For most homeowners, cellulose is the better value. The per-square-foot cost advantage of $0.10 to $0.30 adds up quickly across a full attic, and the performance differences are minimal in a properly ventilated attic.

## What Affects Your Per-Square-Foot Cost

### Geographic Location

Insulation material prices are fairly consistent nationally because the products ship from regional plants. But labor costs vary significantly:

- **High-cost metros (SF, NYC, Boston, Seattle):** Add 20% to 35% to the installed cost
- **Mid-cost areas (Denver, Atlanta, Dallas):** Add 5% to 15%
- **Lower-cost markets (Midwest, rural South):** At or below national average

The per-square-foot material cost stays roughly the same. It is the labor component that creates the geographic spread.

### Existing Insulation

If you are adding blown insulation on top of existing material (the most common scenario), the per-square-foot cost is straightforward — you are only paying for the additional depth needed.

If the old insulation needs [removal first](/blog/removing-insulation-from-attic-cost-breakdown-real-numbers/), add $1.00 to $2.50 per square foot for that phase. The total per-square-foot cost for a full replacement (remove old + install new) runs $2.00 to $4.50.

### Air Sealing Add-On

Air sealing the attic floor before blowing insulation costs $0.30 to $1.00 per square foot as an add-on to the insulation project. It is not technically part of the insulation cost, but it is the single most impactful upgrade you can pair with blow-in insulation.

A well-sealed attic with R-38 insulation outperforms a leaky attic with R-60 insulation. If your budget forces a choice between more insulation depth or air sealing, choose air sealing every time.

### Minimum Job Size

Many insulation contractors have minimum charges of $1,000 to $2,000. For small attics (under 500 sq ft), the per-square-foot effective cost may be higher because you are paying the minimum rather than the actual square footage rate. In these cases, DIY becomes especially attractive.

## DIY Blow-In Insulation: Real Costs

The DIY path for blow-in insulation is well established and genuinely achievable for most homeowners. Here is the full cost picture:

**Materials:**
- Insulation bags: $10 to $14 each (20 to 40 bags for a typical attic)
- Vent baffles: $1 to $3 each (one per rafter bay at the eaves, typically 15 to 30)
- Depth markers: $5 to $10 (rulers stapled to joists)
- Air sealing supplies (spray foam cans, caulk): $30 to $100

**Equipment:**
- Insulation blower: Often free with purchase of 20+ bags at Home Depot, Lowe's, or Menards
- N95 respirator: $5 to $15
- Safety glasses: $5 to $10
- Headlamp: $10 to $20 (you likely already own one)
- Long sleeves and pants you can discard

**Total DIY cost for 1,000 sq ft to R-49:**
- Material: $320 to $550
- Equipment and supplies: $50 to $150
- **Total: $370 to $700**

Compare that to $1,500 to $2,200 professionally installed. The savings are $800 to $1,500 — significant, and the work is physically manageable for most people.

### DIY Process Overview

1. **Prep the attic.** Install vent baffles in each rafter bay at the eaves. Seal air leaks around all penetrations. Set up depth markers every 6 feet by stapling rulers to the sides of joists.

2. **Set up the blower.** Position it outside or in the garage near the exterior. Feed the hose through the house and up into the attic. You need a helper — one person feeds bags into the blower while the other controls the hose in the attic.

3. **Blow from the far end.** Start at the farthest point from the attic access and work toward it. This way you are always standing on clear joists, not on top of fresh insulation.

4. **Fill to the target depth.** Use your depth markers as guides. Blow material evenly, working each section to full depth before moving on. Pay special attention to corners, edges, and areas around obstructions.

5. **Clean up.** Disconnect the hose, remove the blower, and clean up any spilled material. The attic hatch or pull-down stair should be insulated and weatherstripped as a final step.

The whole process takes 3 to 6 hours for a 1,000 sq ft attic with two people. Plan for a full morning or afternoon.

## The Tax Credit Advantage in 2026

The Inflation Reduction Act provides a 30% tax credit for insulation projects, covering both materials and professional labor, up to $1,200 per year. This credit applies in 2026 and significantly improves the per-square-foot economics.

**Example:** Professional installation at $1.80 per square foot for a 1,000 sq ft attic = $1,800. The 30% credit returns $540 on your tax filing, reducing the effective cost to $1,260, or $1.26 per square foot.

The credit also applies to DIY material purchases — just save your receipts. A $500 DIY material cost yields a $150 tax credit, bringing the effective cost down to $350 for a full attic.

Utility rebates add additional savings. Many electric and gas companies offer $200 to $500 in rebates for attic insulation upgrades. Check [your utility's energy efficiency programs](/blog/remove-attic-insulation-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/) and factor in available rebates when calculating your per-square-foot cost.

## Comparing Quotes

When evaluating professional quotes on a per-square-foot basis, make sure you are comparing equivalent scope:

- **Material type** should be the same across quotes (cellulose to cellulose)
- **Target R-value** should be specified, not just depth (R-value is the performance metric; depth is the means)
- **Air sealing** inclusion — a quote at $1.80/sq ft with air sealing beats $1.50/sq ft without
- **Existing insulation handling** — some quotes assume blowing over existing; others include removal
- **Baffle installation** — confirm whether included
- **Attic hatch insulation** — a small detail that matters

The [IRC building code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) sets minimum R-values by climate zone. Make sure your project meets or exceeds these requirements, especially if you are claiming tax credits or utility rebates.

## The Bottom Line

For blow-in insulation in 2026, plan on $1.00 to $2.50 per square foot installed professionally, or $0.50 to $1.40 per square foot for materials alone in a DIY installation. Cellulose is the better value for most attic applications, air sealing should always be part of the project, and the federal tax credit makes the per-square-foot economics more favorable than in any recent year. Whether you rent a blower and spend a Saturday or hire a crew and have it done by lunch, the [per-square-foot investment in insulation](/blog/attic-insulation-removal-pricing-guide-for-homeowners/) pays for itself in energy savings within a few years.
