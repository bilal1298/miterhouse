If your home relies on a well system, the water pump is the heart of your plumbing. When it fails, you have no water — no showers, no toilets, no kitchen sink. The good news is that many common water pump problems have straightforward fixes that a reasonably handy homeowner can handle. The less good news is that some repairs involve electrical work or well components that genuinely require a professional.

Here is how to diagnose the problem and decide your next move.

## How Your Well Water System Works

Understanding the basics helps you troubleshoot effectively. A typical residential well system has these components:

- **The well pump** — either a submersible pump down in the well (most common in wells deeper than 25 feet) or a jet pump mounted in the basement or pump house (common for shallow wells)
- **The pressure tank** — a steel tank with a rubber bladder inside that maintains water pressure between pump cycles, usually in the 30 to 50 PSI or 40 to 60 PSI range
- **The pressure switch** — a small electrical switch on the plumbing near the pressure tank that turns the pump on and off based on water pressure
- **The check valve** — prevents water from flowing backward into the well when the pump is off
- **The pitless adapter** — connects the well pipe to the underground line running to your house, keeping the connection below the frost line

When you turn on a faucet, pressure drops in the tank. The pressure switch detects this and turns on the pump. Water flows from the well through the check valve, into the pressure tank and through your pipes. When pressure reaches the upper limit (50 or 60 PSI), the switch turns the pump off.

## Diagnosing Common Pump Problems

### No Water at All

**Check the circuit breaker first.** The pump runs on a dedicated 240-volt circuit (for submersible pumps) or 120-volt circuit (for some jet pumps). A tripped breaker is the most common — and easiest — cause of total water loss. Reset it once. If it trips again immediately, stop — that indicates a short or ground fault that needs professional diagnosis.

**Check the pressure switch.** The contacts inside the switch can corrode or become pitted over time. Remove the cover (after turning off the breaker) and inspect the contacts. If they are burned or heavily pitted, the switch needs replacing. A replacement switch costs $15 to $40 and is a 20-minute swap if you are comfortable with basic electrical connections per [NFPA 70 standards](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70).

**Check the pressure gauge.** If it reads zero, either the tank has lost all pressure or the gauge is faulty. Try tapping the gauge gently — old gauges can stick. If it is genuinely at zero, the problem is either a failed pump, a broken check valve (water drained back into the well), or a waterlogged pressure tank.

### Pump Runs Constantly (Short Cycling)

This is one of the most common complaints. The pump turns on and off every few seconds or runs without stopping.

**Waterlogged pressure tank.** The rubber bladder inside the tank can rupture, allowing the tank to fill entirely with water. Without the air cushion, the pump has to run every time pressure drops slightly. Test by pressing the Schrader valve (like a tire valve) on top of the tank. If water comes out instead of air, the bladder is failed and the tank needs replacing.

**Leaking check valve.** If water drains back into the well between pump cycles, the pump has to reprime each time. A failed check valve causes the pressure to drop immediately after the pump shuts off.

**Pressure switch issues.** Corroded contacts or an incorrectly adjusted switch can cause rapid cycling. The cut-in and cut-out pressures need a minimum 20 PSI differential to prevent short cycling.

**Actual leak in the system.** A leak anywhere — in the supply line from the well, in the house plumbing, or even a running toilet — makes the pump run to compensate. Check our [water leak repair guide](/blog/plumbing-water-leak-repair-guide-what-to-know/) for help tracking it down.

### Low Water Pressure

**Check the pressure tank's air charge.** Turn off the pump, drain the system by opening a faucet, and check the air pressure at the Schrader valve with a tire gauge. It should be 2 PSI below the cut-in pressure (if your switch is set to 30/50, the tank should read 28 PSI). If it is low, add air with a bicycle pump or compressor.

**Clogged sediment filter.** If your system has an inline sediment filter (and it should), a clogged filter restricts flow significantly. Check and replace the filter cartridge — this should be done every 3 to 6 months anyway.

**Partially closed valve.** Walk the line from the pressure tank to the house and verify every gate valve and ball valve is fully open. A valve left partially closed after a repair is a surprisingly common cause of pressure complaints.

**Declining well yield.** Wells can produce less water over time due to aquifer changes, mineral deposits on the well screen, or a dropping water table. If you have ruled out equipment issues, a well service company can perform a yield test.

## Repairs You Can Handle Yourself

### Replacing the Pressure Switch

**Difficulty: Moderate. Time: 30 minutes. Cost: $15–$40.**

1. Turn off the circuit breaker to the pump
2. Verify power is off with a non-contact voltage tester
3. Open a faucet to relieve pressure in the system
4. Note the wire positions on the old switch (take a photo)
5. Disconnect the wires
6. Unscrew the old switch from the plumbing fitting (it threads onto a 1/4-inch NPT port)
7. Apply Teflon tape to the threads of the new switch
8. Thread the new switch on and tighten
9. Reconnect the wires in the same configuration
10. Close the faucet, turn on the breaker, and test

Set the cut-in and cut-out pressures to match your previous settings (typically 30/50 or 40/60). The adjustment nut inside the switch controls the cut-in pressure; a smaller nut controls the differential.

### Replacing the Pressure Tank

**Difficulty: Moderate. Time: 1–2 hours. Cost: $150–$400 for the tank.**

The most common sizes for residential use are 20-gallon, 32-gallon, and 44-gallon. Bigger tanks mean fewer pump cycles, which extends pump life. If you are replacing anyway, consider sizing up.

1. Turn off the pump breaker
2. Close the valve between the well line and the tank
3. Drain the tank by opening a faucet and the tank drain valve
4. Disconnect the plumbing connections (typically unions or threaded fittings)
5. Remove the old tank (they are heavy even when drained — get help)
6. Set the new tank and pre-charge the bladder to 2 PSI below your cut-in pressure
7. Reconnect plumbing, open valves, turn on breaker, and test

### Replacing the Pressure Gauge

**Difficulty: Easy. Time: 10 minutes. Cost: $8–$15.**

Unscrew the old gauge, wrap the threads with Teflon tape, screw on the new one. Make sure the pump is off and pressure is relieved first.

## When to Call a Well Professional

Certain repairs are outside DIY territory:

- **Submersible pump replacement** — the pump is 100 to 400 feet down in the well. Pulling it requires specialized equipment and usually a well truck with a hoist. Cost: $1,000 to $3,000 installed.
- **Well cap or pitless adapter issues** — these are sanitary seals that keep contaminants out of your water supply. Improper installation creates a health risk.
- **Electrical wiring from the panel to the pump** — 240-volt circuit work requires a licensed electrician in most jurisdictions. The [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) has specific requirements for well pump circuits.
- **Declining well yield** — this requires a well service company to evaluate. Solutions range from hydro-fracturing the well ($2,000 to $4,000) to drilling a new one ($5,000 to $15,000+).
- **Water quality issues** — if your water suddenly changes color, taste, or odor, get it tested before making any repairs. A compromised well casing or contaminated aquifer is a health issue that needs professional assessment.

## Extending Your Pump's Life

A well-maintained pump and pressure system can last 15 to 25 years. Here is what makes the difference:

**Prevent short cycling.** Short cycling (rapid on-off) overheats the motor and burns out the pump prematurely. A properly charged pressure tank with an intact bladder is the primary protection.

**Install a cycle stop valve.** For about $150, this device maintains constant pressure and prevents the pump from short cycling. It is especially worthwhile in homes with variable water demand.

**Protect against lightning.** Well pumps are vulnerable to power surges. A surge protector rated for 240-volt circuits ($50 to $100) on the pump circuit can prevent a $2,000 pump replacement.

**Check the air charge seasonally.** Four times a year, turn off the pump, drain the pressure, and check the bladder pressure with a tire gauge. Top off as needed. This two-minute task prevents waterlogging.

**Replace the sediment filter on schedule.** A clogged filter makes the pump work harder to maintain pressure, shortening its life.

## Sump Pumps: A Related but Different Animal

If you are searching for "home water pump repair" because your basement is flooding, you may actually need sump pump help rather than well pump repair. Sump pumps remove groundwater from a pit in your basement floor and discharge it away from the foundation.

Common sump pump issues include stuck float switches, burned-out motors, and clogged discharge lines. For basement water issues, our [trenchless plumbing guide](/blog/trenchless-plumbing-repairs-a-homeowners-guide/) covers underground drainage, and our [plumbing snake guide](/blog/plumbing-snake-repairs-a-homeowners-guide/) addresses clogged discharge lines.

The most important thing with any water pump — well or sump — is to act fast. Running a pump dry destroys the motor, and letting a pump cycle excessively does the same thing slowly. When in doubt, turn the pump off until you can diagnose the problem. No water for an hour is inconvenient. A burned-out pump motor is expensive.
