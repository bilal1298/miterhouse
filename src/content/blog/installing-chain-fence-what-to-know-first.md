Chain link fence installation runs $8 to $18 per linear foot for materials alone, or $15 to $40 per linear foot fully installed by a contractor. For a standard 150-linear-foot backyard fence at 4 feet tall, expect to pay $1,200 to $2,700 for materials or $2,250 to $6,000 installed. The final number depends on fence height, gauge of wire, gate count, and how rocky your soil is.

Before you start pricing contractors or loading posts into your truck, there are a few things that trip up almost every first-timer. Here is what you actually need to know.

## Start with Your Property Line — Not Your Tape Measure

The single most expensive mistake in chain fence installation is building on the wrong side of the property line. A survey costs $300 to $800. Tearing down and rebuilding a fence because your neighbor disputes the line costs far more.

Check your plat survey first. If you do not have one, or it is from decades ago and markers are gone, hire a surveyor before you dig a single hole. Most municipalities also require setbacks — the fence must sit a certain distance from the property line, typically 2 to 6 inches on your side.

While you are at the building department, ask about fence height limits. Most residential zones cap front-yard fences at 3 to 4 feet and backyard fences at 6 feet. Some HOAs add their own restrictions on top of that.

## Materials Breakdown: What You Are Actually Buying

A chain link fence system has more components than most people expect. Here is what goes into a typical 150-foot, 4-foot-tall residential installation:

**Terminal posts (end, corner, and gate posts):** These are thicker than line posts — typically 2-3/8 inches in diameter for residential work. They carry the tension of the stretched mesh and anchor the system. Budget $15 to $30 each depending on height and gauge.

**Line posts:** Spaced every 10 feet along straight runs, these are 1-5/8 inches in diameter for standard residential fences. They support the mesh but do not carry the same tension load. Budget $8 to $15 each.

**Top rail:** A continuous horizontal pipe that runs along the top of the fence, threading through loop caps on each line post. It stiffens the entire assembly. Sold in 10.5-foot sections that sleeve together. Budget $6 to $12 per section.

**Chain link mesh (fabric):** Sold in 50-foot rolls. Standard residential is 11 or 11.5 gauge galvanized. Vinyl-coated mesh (typically in black or green) costs 30 to 50 percent more but looks better and resists corrosion longer. Budget $50 to $100 per 50-foot roll for galvanized, $70 to $140 for vinyl-coated.

**Hardware:** Tension bars, tension bands, brace bands, rail ends, loop caps, post caps, tie wires. These small parts add up to $100 to $200 for a typical backyard fence. Buy a fence hardware kit rather than sourcing pieces individually.

**Concrete:** Each post hole gets one to two 50-pound bags of premix concrete. For 150 feet of fencing with roughly 17 posts, budget 20 to 35 bags at $4 to $6 each.

## The Installation Process Step by Step

### 1. Mark and Dig Post Holes

Set string lines between corner stakes. Mark each post location along the string — terminal posts at ends, corners, and gate openings, line posts every 10 feet between them.

Dig holes 3 times the post diameter and one-third the above-ground fence height plus 4 inches deep. For a 4-foot fence, that means holes roughly 20 inches deep and 8 inches wide for line posts.

A manual post hole digger works for soft soil and a handful of holes. For anything more, rent a two-person gas-powered auger ($50 to $75 per day). Rocky soil might require a breaker bar or even a jackhammer attachment.

### 2. Set Terminal Posts First

Mix concrete in the holes according to package directions. Set terminal posts plumb using a post level, checking two adjacent sides. Brace them in place and let the concrete cure for at least 24 hours before you stretch mesh.

Crown the concrete slightly above grade so water sheds away from the post base. This detail prevents premature corrosion at the ground line — the spot where chain link posts most commonly fail.

### 3. Run String Line and Set Line Posts

Stretch a string between the tops of your set terminal posts. Set each line post to this height, plumbing and concreting as you go. The tops of all posts should align in a straight, level line (or follow the grade if you are stepping the fence down a slope).

### 4. Install Top Rail and Hardware

Slide the top rail through the loop caps on each line post. The rail sections sleeve into each other — push them together firmly. Attach rail ends at each terminal post using brace bands.

Attach tension bands to each terminal post before the mesh goes on. You will need three bands per 4-foot terminal post, evenly spaced.

### 5. Stretch the Mesh

Unroll the chain link mesh along the outside of the posts. Slide a tension bar through the end links and bolt it to the tension bands on the first terminal post.

Walk the roll to the opposite end, pulling it snug. Use a fence stretcher (rent one — do not try to muscle it by hand) to pull the mesh taut, then insert the final tension bar and bolt it to the far terminal post.

The mesh should be tight enough that it barely deflects when pushed but not so tight that it pulls the posts inward. You will develop a feel for this after the first section.

### 6. Tie Off and Hang Gates

Use aluminum tie wires every 12 to 18 inches along the top rail and every 24 inches down each line post to secure the mesh. Twist them tight with pliers — loose ties will rattle in the wind.

Hang gates using the hinge hardware that came with them. Most residential gate kits include a self-closing hinge set and a fork latch. Adjust the hinges so the gate swings freely without dragging.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Contractor

Chain link fencing is one of the more DIY-friendly fence types, but that does not mean it is easy. The stretching process in particular requires specific tools and some muscle.

**Realistic DIY timeline:** Two people working full days can install 150 feet of 4-foot chain link fence in a long weekend — one day for layout and post holes, one day for setting posts and letting concrete cure, and one day for rail, mesh, and gates. Add another day if you hit rocky soil or have more than two gates.

**When to hire out:** If your yard has significant grade changes (slope fencing requires stepped or racked panels), if you have more than 300 linear feet to install, or if your soil is full of rocks or tree roots, the labor savings of DIY diminish quickly. Contractors have hydraulic augers and pipe cutters that make fast work of difficult conditions.

**Getting quotes:** Get three quotes minimum. A good fence contractor will visit the site, measure, identify potential problems (root systems, buried utilities, drainage issues), and provide a written quote that specifies gauge of materials, concrete method, and a timeline. Any contractor who quotes over the phone without seeing the property is guessing. For help evaluating contractors, check resources from organizations like [Simpson Strong-Tie](https://www.strongtie.com/) for structural specifications and best practices.

## Common Problems and How to Avoid Them

**Frost heave:** In cold climates, post holes that do not extend below the frost line will shift and lean over winter. The frost line varies from 12 inches in the South to 60+ inches in the far North. Set posts below the frost line or use gravel backfill below the concrete to provide drainage.

**Utility strikes:** Call 811 at least three business days before digging. It is free, and it is the law in all 50 states. Hitting a gas line is not just expensive — it is dangerous.

**Gate sag:** Gates wider than 5 feet tend to sag over time unless they are properly braced with a diagonal truss rod. If you need a wider opening (for mower access, for example), consider a double gate with a center drop rod rather than one large single gate.

**Bottom gap:** Chain link mesh needs to sit 1 to 2 inches above the ground to avoid rust, but that gap lets small animals through. If you need to block that gap, tension wire along the bottom or bury the mesh 4 to 6 inches underground. For pet containment, the buried mesh approach is the most reliable.

## What About Water-Efficient Landscaping Along the Fence?

Chain link fences work well as a trellis for climbing plants that can soften the industrial look while adding privacy. If you are [planning water-efficient landscaping](https://www.epa.gov/watersense), consider drought-tolerant vines like trumpet vine, crossvine, or climbing hydrangea that will not need supplemental irrigation once established.

## Maintenance That Actually Matters

Chain link fencing is low-maintenance, but not zero-maintenance. Here is what extends the lifespan from 15 years to 25+:

- **Annual inspection:** Walk the fence line in spring. Look for leaning posts, loose tie wires, and rust spots. Fix them immediately — small problems compound fast.
- **Rust treatment:** Sand rust spots down to bare metal and apply cold galvanizing compound spray. A $12 can of spray covers a lot of spots.
- **Tighten mesh:** If sections start to sag, you can re-tension by removing the tension bar on one end, pulling the mesh tighter with a come-along, and re-attaching.
- **Gate adjustment:** Hinges loosen over time. Check gate swing and latch alignment twice a year.

A galvanized chain link fence that gets this basic attention every spring will outlast most wood fences by a decade, for a fraction of the initial cost. That is the fundamental value proposition here — it is not the prettiest fence, but it is honest, functional, and built to last.
