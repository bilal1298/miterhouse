Attic insulation costs between $1 and $5 per square foot installed, depending on the insulation type and your attic's accessibility. For a standard 1,000-square-foot attic, that puts the total project between $1,000 and $5,000. The payoff is real: properly insulated attics reduce heating and cooling costs by 10% to 50%, depending on your starting point, and the investment typically pays for itself within 2 to 5 years through energy savings.

Here is a complete breakdown of what each insulation type costs, how the installation works, and the factors that move your project toward the high or low end of those ranges.

## Insulation Types and Their Real Costs

### Blown-In Cellulose
- **Material cost:** $0.50 to $1.00 per square foot
- **Installed cost:** $1.00 to $2.50 per square foot
- **R-value per inch:** R-3.2 to R-3.8
- **Best for:** Existing attics where you are adding insulation over what is already there, or filling attics with lots of obstructions (wiring, plumbing, irregular joist spacing)

Cellulose is made from recycled newspaper treated with fire retardants. It is the most cost-effective option for most attic insulation projects. The material is blown in through a hose using a machine that most big-box stores will loan you free with purchase (you typically need 20+ bags to qualify). It fills around obstacles, covers irregularities, and achieves consistent coverage more easily than batts.

The installed cost includes labor for a professional, which makes sense for large attics. For smaller spaces, DIY is genuinely achievable — you need a helper to feed the machine while you direct the hose.

### Blown-In Fiberglass
- **Material cost:** $0.50 to $1.25 per square foot
- **Installed cost:** $1.00 to $3.00 per square foot
- **R-value per inch:** R-2.2 to R-2.7
- **Best for:** Attics where moisture is a concern (fiberglass does not absorb water like cellulose)

Blown-in fiberglass is lighter than cellulose and does not settle as much over time. It has a lower R-value per inch, which means you need more depth to achieve the same insulation level. For a target of R-49 (the recommended level for most climate zones), you need roughly 16 to 18 inches of blown fiberglass compared to 13 to 14 inches of cellulose.

### Fiberglass Batts
- **Material cost:** $0.30 to $1.00 per square foot
- **Installed cost:** $0.75 to $2.00 per square foot
- **R-value per inch:** R-3.1 to R-3.4
- **Best for:** New construction or attics with standard joist spacing and few obstructions

Batts are the traditional pink (or yellow, or white) rolls of insulation cut to fit between joists. They are affordable and available everywhere. The challenge is that they must be cut precisely to fit — gaps, compression, and incomplete coverage are common installation problems that reduce effectiveness significantly. A batt compressed to half its thickness loses roughly 40% of its R-value.

### Spray Foam (Open-Cell)
- **Material cost:** $1.50 to $3.00 per square foot
- **Installed cost:** $2.50 to $4.50 per square foot
- **R-value per inch:** R-3.6 to R-3.8
- **Best for:** Attics being converted to conditioned (livable) space, cathedral ceilings, situations where air sealing is the primary goal

Open-cell spray foam is applied wet and expands to fill cavities, creating both insulation and an air barrier in one step. It is the premium option for attic insulation, and the higher cost is justified when air sealing is as important as insulation value — which it often is. In many homes, air leaks through the attic floor (around light fixtures, plumbing penetrations, and access hatches) waste more energy than insufficient insulation.

### Spray Foam (Closed-Cell)
- **Material cost:** $3.00 to $5.00 per square foot
- **Installed cost:** $4.00 to $7.00 per square foot
- **R-value per inch:** R-6.0 to R-7.0
- **Best for:** Situations requiring both insulation and structural reinforcement, moisture-prone environments, or where space is limited (high R-value per inch)

Closed-cell is the most expensive option but delivers the highest R-value per inch and adds structural rigidity to the roof deck. It is overkill for most standard attic floor insulation but makes sense for specific applications.

## How Much Insulation Do You Need?

The target R-value depends on your climate zone:

| Climate Zone | Recommended Attic R-Value |
|---|---|
| Zone 1 (South Florida, Hawaii) | R-30 |
| Zone 2 (Southern tier) | R-38 |
| Zone 3 (Mid-South) | R-38 |
| Zone 4 (Mid-Atlantic, Midwest) | R-49 |
| Zone 5-8 (Northern states) | R-49 to R-60 |

If your attic already has some insulation, measure its depth and estimate the existing R-value (roughly R-3 per inch for most materials). Add enough new insulation to reach the target for your zone.

## Cost Factors That Move the Needle

**Attic accessibility.** A walk-up attic with full-height clearance is straightforward. A low-clearance attic accessed through a small hatch requires working in cramped conditions, which takes longer and costs more. Some attics require temporary decking removal to access all areas.

**Existing insulation condition.** If existing insulation is contaminated (rodent damage, mold, water damage), it may need removal before new insulation goes in. Removal adds $1 to $2 per square foot.

**Air sealing before insulation.** The single most impactful step — and one that many insulation projects skip. Before adding insulation, seal all penetrations through the attic floor: around electrical boxes, plumbing pipes, ductwork, the chimney chase, and the attic hatch. This can be done with caulk, spray foam, and metal flashing for about $200 to $500 in materials for a typical attic. Some installers include air sealing in their scope; others charge extra.

**Obstacles and complexity.** HVAC ductwork, electrical wiring, recessed lights (which need special insulation-rated covers), and complex framing all add time and cost. Budget 10% to 20% more for attics with significant obstacles.

## Safety Considerations

Attic insulation work involves real hazards that deserve respect.

**Heat.** Attics in summer can exceed 140 degrees. Schedule work for early morning, late evening, or cooler seasons. Heat exhaustion in an attic is a genuine medical risk.

**Air quality.** Fiberglass irritates skin, eyes, and lungs. Cellulose dust, while less irritating, is still a respiratory concern. Wear a proper N95 or P100 respirator (not a dust mask), safety goggles, long sleeves, and gloves. If your home was built before 1978, test for asbestos in existing insulation before disturbing it.

**Footing.** In most attics, the only safe surface to walk on is the top of the ceiling joists. Stepping between joists puts your foot through the ceiling of the room below. Use temporary plywood walkways and work carefully.

**Electrical.** Old wiring (knob-and-tube, in particular) creates fire hazards when covered with insulation. If your attic has knob-and-tube wiring, consult an electrician before insulating.

The [EPA provides guidance on radon](https://www.epa.gov/radon) that is relevant to attic projects — radon can accumulate in enclosed spaces, and insulation changes can affect how your home vents. If you have not tested for radon, consider doing so before sealing your attic tightly with spray foam. The [EPA's mold prevention resources](https://www.epa.gov/mold) are also worth reviewing, as improper insulation can create condensation problems that lead to mold growth.
