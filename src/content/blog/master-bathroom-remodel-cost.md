A master bathroom remodel runs $3,000 for a cosmetic refresh up to $60,000 or more for a full gut-and-rebuild. The reason the range is that wide isn't contractor padding — it's that these are genuinely different projects at different tiers, and confusing them is how budgets blow up.

Here's how the tiers actually break down, what each one costs in 2026, and the five variables that will move your number more than anything else.

---

## The Three Tiers (and Why the Differences Are Bigger Than You Think)

| Project Tier | Cost Range | Cost Per Sq Ft | What's Actually Happening |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cosmetic Refresh | $3,000–$8,000 | $50–$100 | Swap surfaces, keep everything else |
| Mid-Range Remodel | $10,000–$25,000 | $150–$250 | New fixtures, new tile, layout stays |
| High-End Gut Renovation | $30,000–$60,000+ | $300–$500+ | Down to the studs, plumbing moves, custom finishes |

The jump from cosmetic to mid-range isn't a slider — it's a different category of work. And the jump from mid-range to gut renovation involves opening walls, cutting into floors, and potentially dealing with what 15 years of trapped moisture left behind.

---

## Tier 1: The Cosmetic Refresh ($3,000–$8,000)

A refresh makes sense when the layout works, the plumbing is solid, and the room just looks tired. You're keeping the bones and replacing everything visible.

**What goes into it:**

- Paint (walls and ceiling): $200–$600 hired out; $60–$100 in materials if you do it yourself
- Toilet replacement: $300–$800 installed — a Toto Drake II or American Standard Cadet Pro at $250–$350, plus $100–$200 for a plumber
- Vanity light fixture: $150–$500 installed — the Kichler Braelyn or the Feiss Paulson are both solid mid-range options at $120–$250
- Mirror: $100–$600 depending on whether you want frameless, framed, or a medicine cabinet
- Faucets: $150–$400 installed per fixture — Delta and Moen both have solid single-hole options in the $80–$180 range that hold up
- Hardware (towel bars, TP holder, hooks): $100–$300
- Accessories: $200–$500

If you [tackle the DIY-friendly parts yourself](/blog/diy-bathroom-remodel-cost/) — painting, hardware swaps, mirror, light fixture — you can land the total at $2,000–$4,000. What a refresh won't fix: cracked or discolored tile, a cramped layout, inadequate ventilation, or a shower that's been slowly leaking behind the surround. If those are your actual problems, you're in Tier 2 territory whether you want to be or not.

---

## Tier 2: The Mid-Range Remodel ($10,000–$25,000)

This is the most common master bath scope. You're keeping the toilet where it is and the shower in the same corner, but you're replacing most major surfaces and fixtures. Everything gets ripped out; not much gets moved.

| Line Item | Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Double vanity (60"), supply and install | $1,200–$4,500 |
| Quartz or cultured marble countertop | $500–$2,000 |
| Tile flooring (materials + labor) | $1,500–$4,000 |
| Shower surround tile (materials + labor) | $2,000–$6,000 |
| Shower valve/fixture replacement | $400–$1,200 |
| Toilet replacement | $300–$800 |
| Mirror(s) | $200–$800 |
| Light fixtures | $300–$1,000 |
| Exhaust fan (Broan or Panasonic WhisperCeiling) | $200–$500 |
| Permits | $200–$500 |
| Labor overhead | $3,000–$7,000 |

**The tile decision is where mid-range budgets diverge most.** A shower surround in 3×6 subway tile is a fundamentally different job — in both material cost and labor — from the same shower done in 24×48 large-format porcelain. Large-format tile requires more precise setting, harder cuts, and a perfectly flat substrate. Figure $8–$15 per square foot installed for simple subway; $12–$25 per square foot for large-format or complex patterns. That difference alone can swing the total by $3,000–$6,000.

My take: spend on the tile. It's what you look at every day, and cheap tile installed well looks better than expensive tile installed badly. If you're watching budget, buy the $4–$6 per square foot porcelain tile from MSI or Floor & Decor and put the savings toward a solid Kohler Artifacts shower system instead of the builder-grade pressure-balance valve.

For a full line-item breakdown of how these numbers combine, see the [bathroom remodel cost estimate guide](/blog/bathroom-remodel-cost-estimate/).

**Where mid-range remodels go over budget:** opening the shower surround. Old tile hides a lot. Water that's been wicking into backer board for years leaves rotted studs and failed waterproofing that has to be fixed before anything else goes on the wall. Budget $500–$2,500 for this contingency. It's not rare — it's expected in any bathroom older than 15 years.

## Tier 3: The High-End Gut Renovation ($30,000–$60,000+)

A gut renovation goes to the studs. You're relocating fixtures, specifying luxury finishes, and rebuilding the room from scratch. This is how you get the curbless walk-in shower with a Schluter linear drain, the freestanding Victoria + Albert tub, the heated floors, and the custom floating vanity.

**Here's what drives costs at this tier:**

**Plumbing relocation** is the single biggest variable, and the one most people underestimate. Moving a toilet: $500–$1,500. Moving a drain — required for almost any shower relocation — means cutting into the subfloor. On a slab foundation, that means a jackhammer and concrete work: $1,000–$3,000 per fixture. On a second floor with wood framing, it's less invasive but still $600–$1,500. Keep fixtures where they are and you save $2,000–$8,000 immediately.

**Walk-in shower** with frameless glass, a linear drain, and large-format tile: $8,000–$20,000 depending on size and material. Frameless glass alone — a 3/8" or 1/2" panel from a company like Coastal Shower Doors or a local glass shop — runs $1,500–$4,000 for a standard opening. See the [walk-in shower remodel cost breakdown](/blog/walk-in-shower-remodel-cost/) for a deeper look at what moves that number.

**Freestanding soaking tub:** $1,500–$6,000 for the tub, plus $500–$1,200 for a floor-mount filler and rough-in work. A Kohler Underscore or American Standard Cadet runs $900–$1,500. Victoria + Albert and MTI start at $2,500 and go well past $5,000. One honest opinion: freestanding tubs look excellent in photos and get used less than you'd expect. Unless you're a bath person, put that $4,000 toward a better shower experience.

**Heated tile floors (electric radiant):** $8–$15 per square foot for the mat system (Nuheat or Warmup are both well-regarded), plus $200–$500 for thermostat and electrician time. An 80-square-foot bathroom comes out to $900–$1,700 installed. This is one of the highest satisfaction-per-dollar upgrades in the house — minimal structural work, real comfort every morning. I'd prioritize it over a freestanding tub in almost any scenario.

**Vanity options — where to spend and where not to:** A stock 60" double vanity from Home Depot or Lowe's (James Martin, Foremost) runs $600–$1,200. A semi-custom option from a kitchen-and-bath dealer runs $1,500–$3,500 and gets you soft-close hardware, better drawer boxes, and real wood faces. A fully custom built-in: $4,000–$10,000+. My recommendation: go semi-custom unless your layout demands something non-standard. You get 80% of the look for half the price, and the money saved is better spent on tile or a quality shower system.

**Full gut renovation cost breakdown (100 sq ft example):**

| Category | Low End | High End |
|---|---|---|
| Demolition | $800 | $1,500 |
| Plumbing (with fixture relocation) | $2,000 | $5,000 |
| Electrical (GFCI, heated floor, new circuits) | $1,500 | $3,500 |
| Waterproofing | $800 | $1,800 |
| Tile — floors + shower, materials + labor | $6,000 | $18,000 |
| Walk-in shower (fixtures + frameless glass) | $5,000 | $14,000 |
| Freestanding tub + filler | $2,500 | $8,000 |
| Vanity + countertop + mirrors | $3,000 | $9,000 |
| Toilet | $400 | $1,200 |
| Lighting | $600 | $2,500 |
| Ventilation | $300 | $800 |
| Paint + trim finishing | $500 | $1,200 |
| Permits + inspections | $400 | $800 |
| General contractor markup (15–25%) | $3,500 | $10,000 |
| **Total** | **$27,800** | **$77,300** |

Most homeowners doing a genuine high-end gut land between $35,000 and $55,000. Numbers above $60,000 mean natural stone, a steam shower, luxury fixtures, or a large room. Numbers below $30,000 for a gut renovation usually mean something got skipped — usually waterproofing or proper subfloor work.

## Five Variables That Move the Number More Than Anything Else

**1. Whether you relocate plumbing.** This is the biggest lever. Keep drains and supply lines where they are and you save $2,000–$8,000. Move them, especially on a slab, and you're in a different budget tier whether you planned on it or not.

**2. Tile selection and pattern.** Tile is simultaneously a material cost and a labor multiplier. Natural stone in a herringbone pattern costs 3–4x more than large-format porcelain in a straight stack — same square footage, wildly different bill. Porcelain performs comparably to stone in a wet environment and doesn't require sealing. If you love the look of Calacatta marble, the Marazzi or MSI porcelain versions are genuinely indistinguishable at six feet. See the [bathroom tile installation cost guide](/blog/bathroom-tile-installation-cost/) for a full breakdown.

**3. Your labor market.** In New York, San Francisco, or Seattle, expect labor rates 30–50% above the national averages quoted here. In the Midwest and Southeast, you'll often come in under. This alone can swing a $20,000 project by $5,000 in either direction.

**4. Permit requirements.** Most jurisdictions require permits for any plumbing or electrical work. Skipping them creates title problems when you sell — buyers' attorneys find unpermitted work in disclosure searches. Pull the permits. It's $200–$600 and it protects you.

**5. What you find in the walls.** Mold remediation adds $500–$3,000. Replacing a deteriorated subfloor adds $500–$1,500. Neither is rare in a bathroom that hasn't been touched since the late 2000s. Build a 15% contingency into any mid-range or gut renovation budget before you start — not after you've already committed to a contractor.

## The ROI Question

A master bath remodel isn't a pure financial play, but it's one of the better-returning renovations in the house. Mid-range projects recoup roughly 60–70% of cost at resale according to the Remodeling Magazine Cost vs. Value report. Upscale projects come in slightly lower because high-end finishes narrow your buyer pool.

The practical framing: if you're selling within two years, do the mid-range work that makes the room competitive with comparable listings. Don't do the $50,000 gut renovation to add $30,000 to your sale price. If you're staying five-plus years, the daily-use return — a shower that doesn't drive you crazy, a vanity with real storage, a floor that's warm in February — matters more than the resale math. ## Before You Call a Contractor

Have these four things sorted before the first call:

- **Scope decision:** cosmetic, mid-range, or gut? Are you moving any plumbing?
- **Fixture tier:** you don't need to have picked the exact Kohler model, but know whether you're in the $300 or $800 toilet category
- **Tile direction:** subway, large-format porcelain, or natural stone — this alone affects the quote significantly
- **Timeline flexibility:** rush jobs cost more; being flexible on start date often buys you a better rate

Get three quotes minimum, and ask each contractor for a line-item breakdown, not a lump-sum number. A lump sum tells you nothing; a line-item bid tells you where the money is going and makes comparison possible. Any contractor who won't break it out is a red flag.

The cheapest quote rarely wins in a bathroom. Plumbing, waterproofing, and tile are the three areas where cutting corners creates the most expensive future problems. Look for the contractor who explains their process, not just their price.
