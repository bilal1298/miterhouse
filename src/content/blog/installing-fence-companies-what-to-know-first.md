Finding a fence installation company is the easy part. Finding one that shows up when they say they will, builds a fence that stays standing for 20 years, and does not hit you with surprise charges after the fact takes more homework. The fence industry has low barriers to entry, which means the range between excellent and terrible is wider than most home improvement trades.

Here is what to look for, what to ask, and how to protect yourself before signing anything.

## Types of Fence Installation Companies

Not all fence companies operate the same way, and understanding the business model helps you evaluate the quote.

**Dedicated fence contractors.** These companies do nothing but fencing. They buy materials in bulk, have specialized equipment (auger trucks, pneumatic nail guns, concrete mixers), and their crews install fences daily. This is usually where you get the best combination of price and quality. Typical crew: 2 to 3 installers.

**General contractors who offer fencing.** Landscapers, handymen, and general remodeling companies sometimes include fencing in their service list. Quality varies widely. Some are excellent; others are learning on your job. Ask how many fences they install per month. If the answer is "a few," dig deeper into their process.

**National franchises.** Companies like Lowe's, Home Depot, and national fence brands subcontract the installation to local crews. The franchise handles sales and customer service; the subcontractor does the work. Pricing is often 10 to 20 percent higher than going direct to a local fence company because the middleman takes a cut. However, the franchise may offer financing and a more structured warranty.

**Material suppliers with installation services.** Some lumber yards and fence supply companies offer installation. Their advantage is material expertise --- they know exactly what they are installing and can source materials quickly. Their disadvantage is that installation may be a secondary business.

## The Vetting Checklist

Run every potential fence company through these checks before inviting them to bid.

### Non-Negotiables

**Active contractor license.** Check your state's contractor licensing board online. In most states, fence installation requires a general contractor license or a specialty fencing license. An unlicensed contractor means no recourse if something goes wrong.

**General liability insurance.** Ask for a certificate of insurance and verify it is current. If a worker gets hurt on your property and the company has no insurance, you could be liable. Minimum coverage should be $1 million per occurrence.

**Workers' compensation insurance.** If the company has employees (not just owner-operators), workers' comp is required in most states. Verify it.

**Written contracts.** No contract, no deal. Period. The contract should specify materials (species, grade, dimensions), fence height and style, post spacing and depth, concrete type and volume, gate specifications, start and completion dates, payment schedule, warranty terms, and permit responsibility.

### Strong Indicators of Quality

**Established business (5+ years).** Fly-by-night fence companies are common. A company with five or more years of verifiable history has survived the quality test of time.

**Consistent online reviews.** Check Google, Yelp, and the BBB. Look for patterns, not individual reviews. A company with 50 reviews averaging 4.3 stars is more telling than a company with 5 reviews averaging 5.0 stars.

**Industry associations.** Membership in the [American Fence Association](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) or similar trade organizations suggests professional commitment, though it is not a guarantee of quality.

**Portfolio of recent work.** Ask to see photos of recent installations, ideally ones you can drive by. Even better, ask for 2 to 3 client references from the past 6 months and call them.

**Local references.** The [AARP housing resources](https://www.aarp.org/livable-communities/housing/) directory and community boards are good starting points for finding reputable local contractors.

## What to Expect During the Estimate Process

A good fence company's estimate process tells you a lot about how they will handle the actual job.

**The site visit should take 20 to 45 minutes.** The estimator should walk the entire fence line, measure it (not eyeball it), note obstacles and grade changes, ask about your property line, discuss material preferences, and inquire about pets, kids, and specific concerns.

**The quote should arrive within 3 to 5 business days.** Longer delays suggest disorganization. Same-day quotes suggest a canned price that does not account for your specific site conditions.

**The quote should be itemized.** Here is what a good quote includes:

- Linear footage and fence style/height
- Material specification (species, grade, treatment)
- Post spacing and depth
- Number and type of gates
- Old fence removal (if applicable)
- Grading or clearing (if needed)
- Permit fees
- Labor
- Total
- Payment schedule
- Start date and estimated completion
- Warranty terms

A quote that says "300 feet of wood fence --- $7,500" without any breakdown is not a real quote. It is a guess with a number attached.

## Comparing Quotes Effectively

Getting three to five quotes only works if you compare them on equal terms.

**Standardize the materials.** Tell every company the same thing: "6-foot dog-ear cedar privacy fence, 4x4 posts set 30 inches deep in concrete, 8-foot on center spacing, two 4-foot walk-through gates." Identical specs make quotes comparable.

**Watch for scope differences.** One quote might include old fence removal and another might not. One might include permit fees and another might list them as an extra. Normalize the scope before comparing totals.

**Ask about concrete.** There are two methods: dry-pack (pour dry concrete mix in the hole and let moisture cure it) and wet-set (mix concrete before pouring). Wet-set is stronger and cures more predictably. Dry-pack is faster and cheaper for the installer. If the quote does not specify, ask.

**Check the post size.** Budget installers sometimes use 4x4 posts for 6-foot fences. The proper spec for a 6-foot privacy fence is 4x4 at minimum, with many professionals using 4x6 or 6x6 for corner and gate posts. Using [Simpson Strong-Tie](https://www.awc.org/) or equivalent post brackets adds strength and longevity.

**Compare warranties apple to apple.** A 5-year labor warranty from a company that has been in business for 15 years is credible. A "lifetime warranty" from a 2-year-old company is worthless if they fold in year 3.

## Contract Red Flags

These contract terms should give you pause:

**Payment in full before work starts.** Standard payment structure is 0 to 25 percent at signing, 25 to 50 percent at materials delivery, and the balance at completion. Any company demanding full payment upfront is either cash-strapped or a scam risk.

**No cancellation clause.** Life happens. You should be able to cancel within a reasonable window (typically 3 to 5 business days) without penalty.

**Vague material descriptions.** "Wood fence" is not a specification. The contract should specify the exact material: species (cedar, pine, spruce), treatment (pressure-treated, untreated), grade, and dimensions.

**"Subject to change" pricing.** Material prices do fluctuate, but a contract that allows the company to increase the price after signing transfers all risk to you. A good contract locks the price for a defined period (60 to 90 days is standard).

**No permit mention.** If the contract does not address permits, ask directly. You do not want to discover mid-project that no permit was pulled and the inspector shuts down the job.

## Managing the Project

Once you have signed with a company, stay engaged without micromanaging.

**Before installation day:**
- Confirm the start date 3 to 5 days in advance
- Clear the fence line of personal items, furniture, and anything that could obstruct access
- Mark sprinkler heads, landscape lighting, and anything buried that is not a utility (811 only marks public utilities, not your irrigation)
- Inform your neighbors about the project and the approximate timeline

**During installation:**
- Be available by phone for questions
- Do a brief check at the end of each work day
- Note any concerns and raise them immediately --- do not wait until the project is finished

**At completion:**
- Walk every foot of the fence before making final payment
- Check that posts are plumb (use a level)
- Verify gates open and close smoothly
- Confirm the fence is on your property (compare to survey stakes if available)
- Photograph the completed work for your records

## Dispute Resolution

If something goes wrong after installation, follow this escalation path:

1. **Document the issue.** Photos, measurements, and a written description.
2. **Contact the company in writing.** Email creates a paper trail.
3. **Reference the contract.** Point to the specific terms that were not met.
4. **Allow reasonable repair time.** Most legitimate companies will fix genuine issues promptly.
5. **File a complaint with the state contractor licensing board** if the company is unresponsive.
6. **Contact your credit card company** if you paid by card. Chargebacks are a powerful motivator.
7. **Small claims court** for amounts under your state's limit (typically $5,000 to $15,000).

The goal is to never reach step 5. Thorough vetting upfront, a detailed contract, and staged payments protect you from the vast majority of problems. The 30 minutes you spend checking licenses, reading reviews, and comparing itemized quotes is the cheapest insurance available.
