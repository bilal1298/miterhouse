Exterior home painting costs between $3,500 and $12,000 for most homes, with the national average sitting around $5,500 to $7,000 for a two-story house. That wide range is not contractor padding — it reflects real differences in home size, siding material, paint condition, and the labor market in your area. Here is how to figure out where your house falls in that range.

## Cost by Home Size

Home size is the primary cost driver. Painters measure paintable exterior surface area, not floor square footage, but the two correlate closely enough for budgeting purposes.

| Home Size (floor sq ft) | Approximate Exterior Surface | Pro Cost Range |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000–1,500 (ranch) | 1,200–1,800 sq ft | $2,500–$5,500 |
| 1,500–2,500 (two-story) | 2,000–3,500 sq ft | $4,000–$9,000 |
| 2,500–3,500 (larger two-story) | 3,000–4,500 sq ft | $6,000–$13,000 |
| 3,500+ (large/custom) | 4,500+ sq ft | $10,000–$20,000+ |

Single-story homes cost less per square foot to paint because no scaffolding or tall ladders are needed. Two-story homes add ladder and scaffold costs, plus slower production because painters spend time repositioning equipment.

A ranch-style home with 1,500 square feet of exterior surface might cost $3,500 from a professional crew. That same crew would charge $7,000 for a two-story colonial with 3,000 square feet of surface — double the area, double the cost, but also slower per square foot because of the height.

## What Professional Exterior Painting Includes

A thorough professional exterior paint job includes these steps:

**Pressure washing.** The entire exterior gets washed to remove dirt, mildew, chalking paint, and cobwebs. Professional pressure washing runs $200 to $500 depending on home size. The surface then needs 24 to 48 hours to dry completely before any paint goes on.

**Scraping and sanding.** Any loose, flaking, or peeling paint gets scraped back to a sound surface and feathered smooth with sandpaper. On a home with paint in fair condition, this takes a day. On a badly peeling home, scraping alone can take 2 to 3 days and is often the largest labor cost in the project.

**Caulking.** All joints between siding and trim, around windows and doors, and at penetrations (vents, faucets, light fixtures) get fresh caulk. Painters use exterior-grade caulk rated for the temperature range in your climate. Material cost is modest ($30 to $80), but the labor to do it thoroughly is significant.

**Priming.** Bare wood, patched areas, and stain-bleed spots need primer. Some painters prime the entire exterior on a color change; others spot-prime only. Full priming adds a day of labor and $200 to $400 in materials.

**Two coats of paint.** The industry standard is two coats. One coat of even the best paint does not provide adequate coverage or durability. Any contractor quoting a one-coat exterior job is cutting a corner that will show within 2 to 3 years.

**Detail work.** Trim, fascia, soffits, gutters, shutters, window frames, and doors each require different handling. Multi-color schemes (body color plus trim color plus accent) take 30 to 50 percent longer than single-color jobs.

## Siding Material: The Variable Nobody Mentions First

Your siding material affects cost in two ways: how much paint it absorbs and how much prep it requires.

**Wood (clapboard, shingles, board and batten):** Costs $2.00 to $4.00 per square foot. Wood is the most demanding substrate — it needs the most prep, requires the most paint (especially shingles, which have massive surface area), and any rot must be replaced before painting. The upside: paint adheres well to properly prepped wood and the finish looks excellent.

**Vinyl:** Costs $1.00 to $2.50 per square foot. Vinyl can be painted, but the color selection is limited — you cannot go darker than the original color because dark colors absorb heat and may warp the panels. Vinyl-specific paint (100% acrylic latex with good adhesion to plastics) is required. Prep is minimal since vinyl does not peel or rot.

**Aluminum:** Costs $1.50 to $3.00 per square foot. Older aluminum siding develops chalking (a powdery residue) that must be thoroughly washed off before painting. The siding also dents easily, and dents are visible through paint. Adhesion primer is strongly recommended.

**Stucco:** Costs $2.00 to $4.00 per square foot. Stucco's rough texture absorbs 25 to 40 percent more paint than smooth surfaces. Cracks need filling with elastomeric caulk or patching compound. Elastomeric paint, which stretches to bridge hairline cracks, is the preferred product for stucco and costs $40 to $65 per gallon.

**Fiber cement (HardiePlank):** Costs $1.50 to $3.00 per square foot. Fiber cement holds paint well and requires moderate prep. Factory-primed panels need a finish coat only; unprimed panels need a quality primer first.

## The DIY Reality Check

Exterior painting is one of the more physically demanding home improvement projects. Before committing to DIY, honestly assess these factors:

**Height.** A single-story home is entirely reachable from a 6-foot stepladder and a 16 to 24-foot extension ladder. A two-story home requires extension ladders up to 32 feet and possibly scaffolding for the gable ends. Working on a 28-foot ladder is genuinely dangerous if you do not have experience with it.

**Time commitment.** A solo DIYer should budget 60 to 100 hours for a two-story exterior, including pressure washing, scraping, priming, and two coats. That is 6 to 10 full weekends. Add weather delays and you might be looking at an entire summer.

**Equipment.** Beyond ladders, you may need a pressure washer ($60 to $100/day rental or $250 to $400 to buy), paint sprayer ($75 to $150/day rental), scrapers, sanding tools, caulking guns, and extensive drop cloth coverage for landscaping and hardscaping. Equipment costs $200 to $600 for rentals.

**DIY cost for a two-story home:**
- Paint (12–18 gallons): $500–$1,200
- Primer (3–5 gallons): $60–$200
- Caulk and patching: $30–$80
- Supplies and equipment rental: $200–$600
- **Total: $790–$2,080**

The savings versus professional ($4,000 to $9,000) are substantial. But only pursue this if you are comfortable with heights, have the physical stamina for extended ladder work, and can dedicate the time.

## Factors That Increase Cost

Several conditions push exterior painting costs toward the upper end:

**Paint condition.** A home that was last painted 5 years ago in similar colors might need light scraping and can often skip priming. A home with 15-year-old peeling paint needs days of scraping and full priming. The prep difference alone can be $1,000 to $3,000.

**Lead paint (pre-1978 homes).** The [EPA's RRP rule](https://www.epa.gov/lead/renovation-repair-and-painting-program) requires certified lead-safe practices for any work that disturbs painted surfaces in homes built before 1978. Proper containment, HEPA vacuuming, and compliant disposal add $500 to $3,000 to the project. This is a federal requirement with real penalties for non-compliance — and real health consequences if ignored.

**Architectural complexity.** Dormers, gable peaks, bay windows, decorative brackets, and multi-level rooflines all increase scaffolding needs and slow production. A simple colonial box is significantly cheaper to paint than a Victorian with ornate trim.

**Height and access.** Three-story sections, steep hillside lots where ladder placement is difficult, and homes with limited access between structures all increase labor costs. If the crew needs scaffolding instead of ladders, add $500 to $1,500 for setup and rental.

**Wood rot repair.** Rot discovered during prep is almost always an additional charge. Replacing a few boards costs $200 to $800. Extensive rot in trim, fascia, or siding corners can add $1,000 to $3,000 to the project.

## Getting and Comparing Quotes

Request at least three bids. For exterior work specifically, insist on an on-site visit — anyone quoting an exterior paint job from photos or square footage alone cannot account for the real condition of your surfaces.

Compare these specific elements:
- **Prep scope:** Is pressure washing included? How much scraping?
- **Primer:** Full prime or spot prime? (Full prime adds longevity but costs more)
- **Paint:** Brand, product name, and number of coats
- **Colors:** Is the quote for a single body color or multiple colors?
- **Exclusions:** Are gutters, soffits, fascia, or trim excluded? These are sometimes quoted separately
- **Warranty:** Reputable painters offer 2 to 5-year warranties on labor

Be cautious of the lowest bid. Exterior painting has a wide quality range, and the cheapest crews often cut corners on prep, apply one coat, or use inferior paint — all of which lead to a job that needs redoing in 3 to 4 years instead of 8 to 12.

The [Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/) and [EPA lead safety resources](https://www.epa.gov/lead) provide useful reference material for understanding safety requirements. Check that your chosen contractor follows these guidelines, especially for older homes.

## Making the Investment Last

A quality exterior paint job should last 7 to 12 years on wood siding, 5 to 7 years on stucco, and 10 to 15 years on vinyl or fiber cement. To get the full life out of your investment:

- Wash the exterior annually with a garden hose to remove dirt and mildew before they damage the finish
- Recaulk gaps within a year of any cracking — water behind paint destroys it from within
- Trim vegetation back from the house by at least 12 inches so branches and leaves do not scrub the paint surface
- Touch up any chips, scratches, or bare spots as soon as you notice them — small repairs prevent large ones
- Clean gutters regularly to prevent overflow from staining and damaging the paint below

Spending $5,000 to $8,000 on a quality exterior paint job is a significant investment. Spending $200 per year on maintenance protects that investment and pushes the next full repaint 10 to 15 years into the future. That is the real value equation: not just the upfront cost, but the total cost over the life of the paint job.
