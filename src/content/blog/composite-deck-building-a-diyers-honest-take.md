Composite decking costs 40–60% more than pressure-treated wood for the surface material, but the framing underneath is identical either way — same pressure-treated joists, beams, and posts. That means the actual premium is narrower than most people think, and the decision comes down to one question: how long are you keeping this deck?

## The Real Cost Comparison (300-Square-Foot Deck)

| Component | Pressure-Treated | Composite |
|---|---|---|
| Decking boards | $600–$1,000 | $1,500–$3,600 |
| Framing lumber (PT for both) | $500–$700 | $500–$700 |
| Hardware and fasteners | $350–$450 | $400–$550 |
| Railing (matching material) | $800–$1,200 | $1,500–$3,000 |
| Stairs | $200–$350 | $350–$600 |
| **Total materials** | **$2,450–$3,700** | **$4,250–$8,450** |

Decking boards and railing account for nearly all the premium. Framing, footings, and structural hardware cost the same regardless of surface material. Our [deck building calculator guide](/blog/deck-building-calculator-what-homeowners-should-know/) helps estimate materials for any size.

## Three Types of Composite — Pick the Right One

**Wood-plastic composite (WPC)** — the original. Trex, TimberTech, and Fiberon dominate this category. A homogeneous blend of wood fibers and plastic polymers throughout. Resists rot and insects but can absorb some moisture at cut ends and scratches. The most affordable composite option.

**Capped composite** — a WPC core wrapped in a solid polymer shell on all four sides. Prevents moisture intrusion everywhere, making it virtually maintenance-free. Most mid-range and premium products are capped now. Costs $1–$3 more per square foot than uncapped WPC. This is the sweet spot for most builds.

**PVC decking** — contains no wood at all. Lightest, most moisture-resistant, and most expensive. Won't ever rot. But it expands and contracts more with temperature, requiring careful gapping during installation, and it feels less like wood underfoot.

## The Break-Even Math

The marketing says "maintenance-free." The honest version: "dramatically less maintenance than wood, with a financial break-even at 7–10 years."

A wood deck needs staining or sealing every 2–3 years ($200–$500 per treatment for 300 square feet) plus board replacement as rot develops. Over 20 years, that maintenance total hits $3,000–$5,000 — which offsets most of composite's upfront premium.

**Keeping the deck 10+ years?** Composite wins on lifetime cost and you'll never spend a weekend staining.

**Selling in under 5 years?** The upfront savings of wood matter more since you won't be around for the maintenance payoff.

**Rental property?** Composite. You don't want to schedule and pay for staining every 2–3 years on a property you don't live at.

## What's Different About Building With Composite

If you've built with wood, composite requires a few adjustments. The framing phase is identical — the differences start when you switch to surface material.

### Gapping Is Critical

Composite expands and contracts with temperature more than wood. Leave 1/8-inch to 3/16-inch gaps between board ends (check your specific product's manual — brands differ). Many brands provide proprietary spacer tools. On a hot installation day when boards are expanded, reduce the gap slightly. Cold day? Increase it.

### Hidden Fasteners Are Standard

Most composite is designed for hidden fastener systems. Board edges have a groove that accepts a clip fastener. The clip screws into the joist and holds the board from the edge, leaving the surface unblemished.

Hidden fastener installation takes about 20% longer than face screwing but produces a cleaner look and eliminates screw-head mushrooming. Budget $150–$250 for clips on a 300-square-foot deck.

If you prefer face screwing, use composite-specific screws — wood screws mushroom the surface and create raised bumps. Pre-drill for face screwing into composite.

### Joist Spacing Matters More

Most composite requires 16-inch joist spacing for straight-laid decking and 12-inch spacing for diagonal patterns. Check your product's span rating before committing to a joist layout — cutting corners here leads to a bouncy deck that voids the warranty.

### Joist Tape: The Step Most People Skip

Before installing composite boards, apply self-adhesive joist tape to the top of every joist. This membrane protects the pressure-treated wood from moisture penetrating through screw holes and board gaps. A roll costs $15–$30 and takes 30 minutes for a full deck. Your composite boards are moisture-proof — the joists underneath are not.

## The Build Sequence

**Phase 1: Framing.** Footings, posts, beams, and joists — all pressure-treated lumber, built to the same [IRC structural requirements](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) as a wood deck. For permit and inspection details, see our [deck building permit guide](/blog/deck-building-permit-cost-breakdown-real-numbers/).

**Phase 2: Joist prep.** Apply joist tape to every joist top. This step is often skipped and it shouldn't be.

**Phase 3: Starter board.** First board goes against the house using starter clips. Face-screw at the house side (covered later by flashing or fascia), clip on the outer edge. Use a chalk line to ensure it's perfectly parallel — any deviation compounds across the deck width.

**Phase 4: Field boards.** Work outboard from the house, installing hidden clips at each joist. Check alignment every 5–6 boards by measuring from the house wall to the board edge at both ends. Stagger end joints by at least 2 joist spaces.

**Phase 5: Fascia and trim.** Composite fascia boards (thinner, about 1/2 inch) cover exposed rim joists and beam faces. Construction adhesive plus color-matched screws. Cut carefully — fascia is the visible frame of the deck and sloppy joints show.

**Phase 6: Railing.** Composite railing systems are modular: post sleeves over structural posts, rail channels, and individual balusters. Follow manufacturer instructions exactly — these are engineered to meet code when assembled correctly, and improvising connections compromises structural performance. Our [deck building cost guide](/blog/deck-building-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/) compares railing materials side by side.

## The Maintenance Truth

**What you'll still do:**
- Sweep or blow off debris monthly — leaves and pollen can stain if left wet
- Clean twice a year with composite deck cleaner or diluted dish soap and a soft brush
- Remove mold or mildew with oxygen bleach solution in shaded, damp areas
- Check and tighten any face screws annually

**What you'll never do:**
- Stain or seal
- Sand
- Replace rotted boards
- Pull splinters

## Common DIY Mistakes

**Not acclimating boards.** Leave composite on-site 24–48 hours before installation so boards reach ambient temperature. Installing at a significantly different temperature causes excessive expansion or contraction.

**Ignoring manufacturer gap specs.** Each brand has specific requirements. A universal "1/8 inch everywhere" may be wrong for your product.

**Using wood screws.** Wood screws mushroom the composite surface. Composite-specific screws have a different head geometry that seats flush.

**Skipping joist tape.** Your framing will outlast the composite boards if you protect it. Without tape, moisture penetration through screw holes accelerates rot in the pressure-treated structure underneath.
