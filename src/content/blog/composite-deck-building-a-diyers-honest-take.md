Composite deck building costs 40% to 60% more than pressure-treated wood upfront, but the material lasts 25 to 50 years without staining, sealing, or replacing rotted boards. For a 300-square-foot deck, expect to spend $8,000 to $14,000 for materials and labor with composite versus $5,000 to $9,000 for pressure-treated wood. The trade-off is higher initial cost for dramatically lower lifetime maintenance, and it is a project that a prepared DIYer can handle with standard tools and a few weekends.

## What Composite Decking Actually Is

Composite decking boards are manufactured from a blend of wood fibers and plastic polymers, typically polyethylene or polypropylene. The wood component provides stiffness and a natural feel underfoot, while the plastic provides moisture resistance, rot resistance, and color stability.

The three main types:

**Wood-plastic composite (WPC)** is the original and most common type. Brands like Trex, TimberTech, and Fiberon dominate this category. WPC boards have a homogeneous blend of wood and plastic throughout. They resist rot and insects but can absorb some moisture at cut ends and scratches.

**Capped composite** has a WPC core wrapped in a solid polymer cap on all four sides. This prevents moisture intrusion at every surface, making capped composite virtually maintenance-free. Most mid-range and premium products are now capped. Expect to pay $1 to $3 more per square foot than uncapped WPC.

**PVC (polyvinyl chloride) decking** contains no wood at all. It is the lightest, most moisture-resistant option and will never rot, but it is also the most expensive and can feel less like wood underfoot. PVC expands and contracts more with temperature changes than WPC, requiring specific gapping during installation.

## Where the Money Goes

The common misconception is that composite is twice the price of wood. The reality is more nuanced because the framing underneath is the same pressure-treated lumber regardless of what goes on top.

### Material Cost Comparison (300 sq ft deck)

| Component | Pressure-Treated | Composite |
|-----------|-----------------|-----------|
| Decking boards | $600–$1,000 | $1,500–$3,600 |
| Framing lumber (PT for both) | $500–$700 | $500–$700 |
| Hardware and fasteners | $350–$450 | $400–$550 |
| Railing (matching material) | $800–$1,200 | $1,500–$3,000 |
| Stairs | $200–$350 | $350–$600 |
| **Total materials** | **$2,450–$3,700** | **$4,250–$8,450** |

The decking boards and railing account for nearly all the premium. Framing, footings, and structural hardware cost the same.

## DIY Composite Deck Building: What Is Different

If you have built with wood, composite requires a few adjustments to your approach. The framing phase is identical — pressure-treated joists, beams, posts, and ledger. The differences start when you switch from framing to decking.

### Gapping Is Critical

Composite expands and contracts with temperature more than wood. Leave a 1/8-inch to 3/16-inch gap between board ends (check your specific product manual). Many brands provide proprietary spacer tools for this. On a hot day when the material is expanded, reduce your gap slightly. On a cold day when it is contracted, increase it.

Side-to-side gapping is handled automatically by most hidden fastener systems, which create a consistent gap between board edges as they secure the decking.

### Hidden Fasteners Are Standard

Most composite decking is designed for hidden fastener systems rather than face screws. The board edges have a groove that accepts a clip or biscuit fastener. The clip screws into the joist and holds the board in place from the edge, leaving the top surface unblemished.

Hidden fastener installation is slower than face screwing (about 20% more time) but produces a cleaner look and eliminates screw-head mushrooming on the surface. Budget $150 to $250 for hidden fastener clips on a 300-square-foot deck.

If you prefer face screwing, use composite-specific screws with a small head designed to sit flush and a cutting tip that prevents mushrooming. Pre-drilling is recommended for face screwing into composite.

### Cutting Composite

Composite cuts with standard carbide-tipped saw blades. A 40-tooth or higher blade on a miter saw or circular saw produces clean cuts. Blades dull faster on composite than on wood, so have a spare. Fine-tooth blades reduce chipping on the capped surface.

Cuts generate a fine dust that can stain concrete and other surfaces. Sweep immediately after cutting and do not let composite dust sit on concrete driveways or patios.

### Joist Spacing Matters More

Most composite decking requires 16-inch joist spacing for straight-laid decking and 12-inch spacing for diagonal patterns. Wood decking at 5/4 thickness can span 16 inches easily in either direction. Check your specific product's span rating before committing to a joist layout.

Some premium composite boards rated for 24-inch spans at straight-lay are available, which reduces framing costs. Verify the span rating before reducing joist count.

## The Build Process

### Phase 1: Framing (Same as Wood)

Footings, posts, beams, and joists are all pressure-treated lumber, built to the same code requirements as a wood deck. The [IRC](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) structural requirements do not change based on the decking surface material.

Follow the same span tables, connection hardware requirements, and inspection process as a wood deck. For details on permit and inspection requirements, see our [deck building permit requirements guide](/blog/deck-building-permit-cost-breakdown-real-numbers/).

### Phase 2: Joist Preparation

Before installing composite decking, apply joist tape to the top of every joist. Joist tape is a self-adhesive membrane that protects the pressure-treated wood from moisture that penetrates through screw holes and board gaps. A roll costs $15 to $30 and adds years to the framing life underneath your composite boards.

This step is often skipped by DIYers and some contractors. It takes 30 minutes for a full deck and pays dividends in longevity.

### Phase 3: Starter Board

Begin the first board against the house using the starter clips (different from mid-board clips). The first board is typically face-screwed at the house side and clipped on the outer edge. This face-screw row is covered by the flashing above the ledger or by a fascia board.

Use a chalk line to ensure the first board is perfectly parallel to the house. Any deviation compounds across the deck width.

### Phase 4: Field Boards

Work outboard from the house, installing hidden clips on each joist and pressing the next board groove over the clip edge. Check alignment every 5 to 6 boards with a tape measure from the house wall to the board edge at both ends. Adjust gradually if the boards are drifting.

Stagger end joints by at least 2 joist spaces (32 inches at 16-inch spacing). Some manufacturers require staggering joints by 3 or more joist bays.

### Phase 5: Fascia and Trim

Composite fascia boards cover the exposed rim joists and beam faces. These are thinner boards (typically 1/2 inch) that attach with construction adhesive and color-matched screws. Measure and cut carefully — fascia is the visible "frame" of the deck and sloppy joints are obvious.

### Phase 6: Railing

Composite railing systems are modular: post sleeves over structural wood or metal posts, top and bottom rail channels, and individual balusters. Follow the manufacturer's instructions precisely — these systems are engineered to meet code requirements when assembled correctly, but improvising connections can compromise structural performance.

## Long-Term Maintenance Reality

The marketing promise is "maintenance-free." The honest reality is "much less maintenance than wood."

**What you will still do:**
- Sweep or blow off debris monthly (leaves and pollen can stain if left wet)
- Clean twice per year with a composite deck cleaner (or diluted dish soap and a soft brush)
- Remove mold or mildew with an oxygen bleach solution if it appears in shaded, damp areas
- Check and tighten any face screws annually

**What you will never do:**
- Stain or seal
- Sand
- Replace rotted boards
- Worry about splinters

Over 20 years, the maintenance cost difference between composite and wood is significant. A wood deck needs staining or sealing every 2 to 3 years ($200 to $500 per treatment for a 300-square-foot deck) plus board replacement as rot occurs. That maintenance total easily exceeds $3,000 to $5,000 over two decades, which offsets much of composite's higher upfront cost.

## Common DIY Mistakes

**Not acclimating boards.** Leave composite boards on the job site for 24 to 48 hours before installation so they reach ambient temperature. Installing boards at a temperature significantly different from normal outdoor conditions leads to excessive expansion or contraction.

**Ignoring manufacturer gap specifications.** Each brand has specific gap requirements. Using a universal "1/8 inch everywhere" approach may be wrong for your product.

**Skipping joist tape.** Your pressure-treated framing will last longer with it. Composite boards are moisture-proof; the joists underneath are not.

**Using wood screws instead of composite-specific fasteners.** Wood screws mushroom the composite surface and create a raised bump around each screw head. Composite screws have a different head geometry that seats flush.

The [AARP Livable Communities](https://www.aarp.org/livable-communities/housing/) resource center has useful guidance on building decks with accessibility features if you are designing for aging-in-place.

## Is Composite Worth It?

For a deck you plan to keep for 10 years or more, composite is almost always the better investment. The break-even point compared to wood (including maintenance costs) is typically 7 to 10 years. After that, every year of use is essentially free maintenance savings.

For a deck you plan to sell with the house within 5 years, or a temporary structure, pressure-treated wood may be the smarter financial choice. The upfront savings matter more when the long-term maintenance benefits do not apply to you.
