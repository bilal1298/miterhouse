Blown-in insulation runs $1.00 to $3.00 per square foot installed in 2026 — cellulose at the lower end ($1.00–$2.50) and fiberglass at the higher end ($1.25–$3.00). For DIY, materials alone cost $0.50–$1.25 per square foot. On a 1,200-square-foot attic, that's $600–$1,500 DIY or $1,200–$3,600 professionally installed.

Those numbers make blown-in insulation one of the best-return home improvements you can make, with most projects paying for themselves in energy savings within 2–5 years.

## Cellulose vs. Fiberglass: Head to Head

Both get blown into place with a machine and hose, but the performance differences matter.

### Cellulose

Recycled newspaper treated with borate fire retardants. Gray or off-white, denser than fiberglass, and settles 10–20% over the first few years (good installers compensate with extra depth).

**Per square foot:**
- Material only: $0.50–$1.00
- Pro installed: $1.00–$2.50
- DIY: $0.50–$1.00

**Why it's usually the better pick:** Higher R-value per inch (R-3.2–3.8 vs. R-2.2–2.7 for fiberglass), better sound dampening, denser coverage around obstacles, and better air infiltration resistance even without separate air sealing.

**The downside:** Absorbs moisture (reducing R-value temporarily and inviting mold if the source persists), heavier on the ceiling structure, and settles over time.

### Fiberglass

Spun glass fibers. White or light pink, lighter than cellulose, and doesn't absorb moisture. Minimal settling.

**Per square foot:**
- Material only: $0.50–$1.25
- Pro installed: $1.25–$3.00
- DIY: $0.50–$1.25

**When it makes sense:** Attics with known moisture issues where cellulose absorption is a concern, and ceilings where weight load matters. Non-combustible.

**The downside:** Lower R-value per inch (needs 16–18 inches for R-49 vs. 13–14 for cellulose), lighter weight means air movement in drafty attics can displace it, and it doesn't fill around obstacles as completely.

## Depth by R-Value Target

Your climate zone's target R-value determines how much material you need, which directly drives cost:

| Target R-Value | Cellulose Depth | Fiberglass Depth |
|---|---|---|
| R-30 | 8–9 inches | 10–12 inches |
| R-38 | 10–11 inches | 13–15 inches |
| R-49 | 13–14 inches | 16–18 inches |
| R-60 | 16–17 inches | 20–22 inches |

Cellulose depths include a 15% overfill to compensate for settling.

## Real Project Costs

**Topping up an existing attic:** 1,000 sq ft with 6 inches of existing fiberglass batts (~R-19). Adding cellulose to reach R-49 needs about 8 more inches. DIY: $500–$900. Pro: $1,000–$2,000.

**Insulating a bare attic:** 1,500 sq ft, no existing insulation. Cellulose to R-49 (14 inches). DIY: $900–$1,500. Pro: $1,500–$3,500.

**Wall cavity injection:** 1,200 sq ft of exterior wall area. Dense-pack cellulose through drilled holes. Professional only: $2,400–$4,200.

**Whole-house upgrade (attic + walls):** 1,200 sq ft attic plus 1,800 sq ft walls. Pro: $4,500–$8,000.

## DIY Installation: What It Actually Takes

Blown-in attic insulation is one of the most accessible insulation DIY projects. Here's the honest version.

### Equipment

Home Depot and Lowe's loan a blowing machine free when you buy 20–25+ bags. The machine fits in a pickup truck. You'll also need:
- A helper (mandatory — one person feeds the hopper, one works the hose)
- N95 respirator and safety goggles
- Headlamp or work light
- Temporary walkway boards for the attic
- Depth markers (rulers or marked sticks) to verify coverage

### The Process

Set up the machine outside or in the garage with the hose reaching into the attic. Start at the farthest point from the access hatch and work backward. Build up to your target depth evenly between joists. Use depth markers every few feet to keep coverage consistent. Don't pack it down — blown-in works by trapping air, and compressing it kills the R-value.

Work in shifts if the attic is hot. Twenty minutes in a 130°F attic is the safe limit. Take breaks, hydrate, and cool down before going back up.

### The Three Mistakes That Ruin DIY Jobs

**Blocking soffit vents.** Install baffles (cardboard or foam channels) at every soffit vent before blowing anything. Soffits provide ventilation from eave to ridge. Bury them in insulation and you get moisture buildup and ice dams.

**Uneven coverage.** Easy to pile insulation thick in accessible spots and skimp near the eaves where headroom disappears. Those low spots along the exterior wall line are exactly where heat loss is worst. Push coverage all the way to the eaves, over the top plate.

**Burying non-IC-rated recessed lights.** These fixtures overheat when covered, creating a fire risk. Identify all recessed lights first — if they're not rated for insulation contact, build insulation dams around them.

## Air Seal First — This Step Matters More Than the Insulation

The single most important prep before adding blown-in: seal air leaks through the attic floor. Gaps around plumbing, wiring, recessed lights, HVAC ducts, and the attic hatch let heated air bypass the insulation entirely.

Air sealing these penetrations before insulating improves energy savings by 25% or more compared to insulation alone. Use caulk for small gaps, expanding foam for medium gaps, and rigid foam or sheet metal (sealed with foam) for large openings.

## Don't Forget the Ductwork

If HVAC ducts run through the attic, inspect for leaks and insulation coverage. Leaky ducts in an unconditioned attic waste 20–30% of heating and cooling output. Seal joints with mastic (not duct tape — it fails within a year) and verify duct insulation is at least R-8.
