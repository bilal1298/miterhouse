Blown-in insulation costs between $1.00 and $3.00 per square foot installed in 2026, with cellulose at the lower end ($1.00 to $2.50) and fiberglass at the higher end ($1.25 to $3.00). For DIY installation — which is genuinely feasible for attic projects — materials alone run $0.50 to $1.25 per square foot. On a 1,200-square-foot attic, that translates to $600 to $1,500 DIY or $1,200 to $3,600 professionally installed.

These numbers put blown-in insulation among the best-return home improvements you can make, with most projects paying for themselves in energy savings within 2 to 5 years.

## Blown-In Cellulose vs. Blown-In Fiberglass: Cost and Performance

Both materials are blown into place using a machine and hose, but they have different characteristics that matter for specific situations.

### Cellulose
Made from recycled newspaper treated with borate fire retardants. Gray or off-white in appearance, denser than fiberglass, and settles roughly 10% to 20% over the first few years after installation (installers compensate by adding extra depth).

**Per-square-foot breakdown:**
- Material only: $0.50 to $1.00
- Professional installation: $1.00 to $2.50 total
- DIY (with rental machine): $0.50 to $1.00 total

**Advantages:** Higher R-value per inch (R-3.2 to R-3.8 vs. R-2.2 to R-2.7 for fiberglass), better sound dampening, denser coverage around obstacles, and better resistance to air infiltration even without separate air sealing.

**Disadvantages:** Absorbs moisture (which reduces R-value temporarily and can promote mold if the moisture source is persistent), heavier on the ceiling structure, and settles over time (requiring installation to above-target depth to account for settling).

### Fiberglass
Made from spun glass fibers. White or light pink, lighter than cellulose, and does not absorb moisture. Minimal settling over time.

**Per-square-foot breakdown:**
- Material only: $0.50 to $1.25
- Professional installation: $1.25 to $3.00 total
- DIY (with rental machine): $0.50 to $1.25 total

**Advantages:** Does not absorb moisture, lighter weight (less load on ceiling joists), does not settle significantly, non-combustible.

**Disadvantages:** Lower R-value per inch (requires 16 to 18 inches for R-49 vs. 13 to 14 inches for cellulose), lighter weight means it can be displaced by air movement in drafty attics, and it does not fill around obstacles as completely as cellulose.

## Depth Requirements by R-Value Target

The target R-value for your climate zone determines how much material you need, which directly drives cost.

| Target R-Value | Cellulose Depth | Fiberglass Depth |
|---|---|---|
| R-30 | 8–9 inches | 10–12 inches |
| R-38 | 10–11 inches | 13–15 inches |
| R-49 | 13–14 inches | 16–18 inches |
| R-60 | 16–17 inches | 20–22 inches |

These depths include a compensation factor for cellulose settling. The installed depth should be about 15% above the settled target depth.

## DIY Installation: A Step-by-Step Realistic Assessment

Blown-in attic insulation is one of the most accessible DIY insulation projects. Here is what it actually involves.

### Equipment
Most home improvement stores (Home Depot, Lowe's) loan a blowing machine free when you buy a minimum quantity of insulation — usually 20 to 25 bags. The machine fits in a pickup truck or SUV with the seats down. You need:
- The blowing machine (loaner)
- Enough insulation bags for your target depth
- A helper to feed bags into the machine while you work the hose
- N95 respirator and safety goggles (mandatory, not optional)
- Headlamp or work light
- Temporary walkway boards for the attic
- Depth markers (rulers or marked sticks) to verify coverage

### The Process
Set up the machine outside or in the garage near a window or opening that the hose can reach into the attic. Your helper feeds bags into the hopper while you direct the hose in the attic, starting at the farthest point from the access hatch and working backward.

Apply insulation evenly between joists, building up to your target depth. Use depth markers (stand a ruler in the insulation every few feet) to verify consistent coverage. Do not pack it down — blown-in insulation works by trapping air, and compressing it reduces its effectiveness.

Work in shifts if the attic is hot. Twenty minutes in a 130-degree attic is about the limit for safe, focused work. Take breaks, hydrate, and let your body temperature drop before going back in.

### Common DIY Mistakes
**Blocking soffit vents.** Soffit vents along the eaves provide critical ventilation to the attic. Before blowing insulation, install baffles (cardboard or foam channels) at every soffit vent to maintain the airflow path from soffit to ridge. Blocking soffits with insulation causes moisture buildup and ice dams.

**Uneven coverage.** It is easy to pile insulation thick in accessible areas and skimp near the eaves where headroom is limited. This creates cold spots along the exterior wall line — exactly where heat loss is worst. Get the insulation all the way to the eaves (over the top plate of the wall) and maintain consistent depth.

**Burying recessed lights.** Non-IC-rated recessed light fixtures overheat when covered with insulation, creating a fire risk. Identify all recessed lights and either confirm they are IC-rated (rated for insulation contact) or install insulation dams around them.

**Covering the attic access hatch.** The hatch itself needs insulation too — a rigid foam board glued to the top of the hatch and weatherstripped around the edges is the standard approach.

## Maximizing Your Investment

### Air Seal First
The single most important step before adding blown-in insulation: seal air leaks through the attic floor. The gaps around plumbing pipes, electrical wiring, recessed lights, HVAC ducts, and the attic hatch allow heated air to escape into the attic, bypassing the insulation entirely.

Air sealing these penetrations before insulating can improve energy savings by 25% or more compared to insulation alone. Use caulk for small gaps, expanding foam for medium gaps, and rigid foam or sheet metal (sealed with foam) for large openings.

### Address Ductwork
If your HVAC ducts run through the attic, inspect them for leaks and insulation coverage. Leaky ducts in an unconditioned attic can waste 20% to 30% of your heating and cooling output. Seal joints with mastic (not tape) and ensure duct insulation is at least R-8.

The [EPA's radon information](https://www.epa.gov/radon) is relevant for any project that changes your home's air sealing — tighter homes may need radon mitigation.
