Attic insulation replacement costs $2 to $6 per square foot when you hire a professional, putting a 1,000-square-foot attic at $2,000 to $6,000 total. That includes removing the old insulation, disposing of it, air sealing the attic floor, and installing new material. The wide range reflects the difference between a straightforward swap (clean old fiberglass out, blow new cellulose in) and a complicated one (contaminated insulation, mold remediation, wiring issues discovered after removal).

Going the DIY route on the installation side alone saves roughly 40 percent, but the removal step is where the real cost and effort sit. Here is a full breakdown of what the project costs, what surprises drive the price up, and how to keep the budget under control.

## Complete Cost Breakdown

### Removal Costs

| Scenario | Pro Cost (per sq ft) | DIY Cost |
|----------|---------------------|----------|
| Clean blown-in (cellulose or fiberglass) | $1.00-$1.50 | $200-$300 vacuum rental + disposal fees |
| Clean batt insulation | $0.75-$1.25 | Trash bags + disposal fees ($50-$150) |
| Contaminated (animal waste, mold) | $1.50-$3.00 | Not recommended for DIY |
| Vermiculite (potential asbestos) | $3.00-$10.00+ | Never DIY — requires certified abatement |

Disposal costs vary by municipality. Some areas allow clean fiberglass and cellulose in regular construction dumpsters ($250 to $400 for a 10-yard). Others require it to go to specific facilities. Contaminated insulation and vermiculite always require specialized disposal.

### Air Sealing Costs

Air sealing the attic floor after old insulation removal is the most cost-effective upgrade you can make during this project. With the floor exposed, every penetration is accessible:

- Professional air sealing: $350 to $1,000
- DIY air sealing: $50 to $150 in materials (expanding foam, caulk, sheet metal, fire-rated sealant)

Skip this step and you lose 15 to 25 percent of the new insulation's potential benefit. Every gap in the attic floor allows warm air to rise into the attic in winter and hot attic air to push down in summer. Sealing those gaps is often more impactful than the insulation itself.

### New Insulation Costs

| Type | Installed Cost/sq ft | DIY Cost/sq ft | R-value per inch |
|------|---------------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Blown cellulose | $0.80-$1.50 | $0.40-$0.70 | 3.2-3.8 |
| Blown fiberglass | $1.00-$1.80 | $0.50-$0.90 | 2.2-2.7 |
| Fiberglass batts | $1.00-$2.20 | $0.50-$1.20 | 3.0-3.7 |
| Open-cell spray foam | $1.50-$3.50 | Not DIY | 3.5-3.8 |
| Closed-cell spray foam | $3.00-$7.00 | Not DIY | 5.8-6.5 |

For most replacement projects, blown cellulose offers the best combination of performance, cost, and coverage. It fills around obstacles better than batts, costs less than spray foam, and provides competitive R-value per inch. Our [blown insulation per square foot cost guide](/blog/blown-insulation-per-square-foot-cost-breakdown-real-numbers/) and [blow-in insulation cost analysis](/blog/how-much-does-blow-in-insulation-per-square-foot-really-cost/) provide deeper material comparisons.

## What Drives Costs Higher Than Expected

Every insulation contractor has stories of jobs that doubled in scope after the old material came out. Here are the most common surprises and their costs:

### Mold Remediation ($500 to $5,000+)

Surface mold on framing can often be treated in place with encapsulating sealants after cleaning ($500 to $1,500). Extensive mold that has penetrated the wood or spread across large areas requires professional remediation with containment, HEPA filtration, and potentially framing replacement. The [EPA's mold guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold) provides actionable information for assessing severity.

The root cause must be fixed first. Common sources: roof leak, bath fan venting into the attic, inadequate soffit/ridge ventilation, or blocked soffit vents (often blocked by the old insulation itself).

### Electrical Issues ($200 to $2,000+)

Old wiring — knob-and-tube in pre-1950 homes, aluminum in 1960s-70s homes, or rodent-chewed wiring in any era — creates both safety hazards and insulation compatibility problems. Knob-and-tube wiring cannot be buried in insulation at all. An electrician must either replace active circuits or install standoff barriers.

### Structural Damage ($500 to $3,000+)

Water-damaged rafters, rotted sheathing, or termite damage to framing members. These need to be repaired before re-insulating, both for structural integrity and because damaged wood holds moisture that will degrade the new insulation.

### Ventilation Corrections ($300 to $1,500)

If the old insulation was blocking soffit vents or the attic lacked proper exhaust ventilation, correcting this is essential before re-insulating. Baffles at every rafter bay along the eaves ($2 to $4 each, 40 to 60 needed for a typical ranch house) maintain airflow. Adding a ridge vent or powered vent exhaust costs $300 to $800.

## The DIY Approach: Realistic Assessment

### What You Can Do Yourself

- Remove clean, uncontaminated batt insulation (roll it up, bag it, haul it)
- Remove clean blown insulation with a rented vacuum ($200-$300/day)
- Air seal all penetrations with expanding foam and caulk
- Install blown cellulose or fiberglass with a rented blowing machine
- Install baffles in rafter bays at the eaves

### What You Should Not Do Yourself

- Remove vermiculite or any insulation potentially containing asbestos
- Remove insulation contaminated with animal waste (requires respiratory protection beyond a standard N95)
- Address mold beyond surface cleaning of small areas
- Install spray foam (requires specialized equipment, training, and certification)

### DIY Budget for a Simple Replacement (1,000 sq ft)

| Item | Cost |
|------|------|
| Insulation vacuum rental (1 day) | $250 |
| Disposal (dumpster or haul) | $150-$300 |
| Air sealing materials | $75-$150 |
| Baffles (50 pieces) | $100-$200 |
| Blown cellulose (30 bags at $12-$18) | $360-$540 |
| Blowing machine (free with purchase) | $0 |
| Personal protective equipment | $40-$80 |
| **Total** | **$975-$1,520** |

That saves $1,400 to $2,500 compared to full professional service for the same scope. The trade-off is two to three full days of physically demanding work in a hot, uncomfortable space.

## Energy Savings and Payback

The [Department of Energy's insulation guidance](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/insulation) provides regional estimates, but here are realistic expectations for replacing degraded insulation:

**From R-11 (degraded) to R-49 (new):**
- Annual energy savings: 20 to 40 percent of heating/cooling costs
- For a home spending $2,400/year on HVAC: $480 to $960 annual savings
- Simple payback on a $3,000 project: 3 to 6 years

**From R-19 (partially degraded) to R-49 (new):**
- Annual energy savings: 10 to 25 percent
- For the same home: $240 to $600 annual savings
- Simple payback on a $3,000 project: 5 to 12 years

Federal tax credits through the Inflation Reduction Act (30 percent of cost, up to $1,200/year for insulation) and state or utility rebates can reduce the effective cost substantially. Check the [Energy Star rebate finder](https://www.energystar.gov/) for current programs.

## Timing and Scheduling

**Best season for the work:** Late fall or early spring. Attic temperatures in summer can exceed 140 degrees F, making the space dangerous for workers and uncomfortable even with cooling breaks. Winter attics in cold climates are cold and may have ice dam issues. The shoulder seasons are the sweet spot.

**Scheduling lead time:** Professional insulation contractors are typically booked 1 to 4 weeks out. During peak demand (post-winter, post-energy-audit season), lead times stretch to 6 weeks. Book early if you want work done during comfortable weather.

**Project duration:** Most professional replacements complete in 1 to 2 days for a standard attic. Add 1 to 3 days if remediation or electrical work is needed. DIY projects spread across 2 to 3 weekends is realistic for a 1,000-square-foot attic.

## Making the Decision

Replace attic insulation when the old material is contaminated, mold-damaged, severely degraded, or when you are converting the space. Add insulation on top of the existing when it is clean, dry, and still has reasonable loft — just not enough depth.

Replacement costs more than adding, but it gives you a clean start: fresh air sealing, a chance to fix hidden problems, and properly installed new material with full rated performance. If you have been living with sky-high energy bills, inconsistent room temperatures, or that musty smell from the attic, replacement is almost certainly the right call.
