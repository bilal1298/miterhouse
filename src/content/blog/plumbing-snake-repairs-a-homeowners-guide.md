A plumbing snake — also called a drain auger — is the tool you reach for when a plunger fails. It physically breaks through or pulls out clogs that liquid drain cleaners and plunging cannot touch. For about $25 to $40, a basic hand snake handles most household drain clogs, and learning to use one properly saves you $150 to $350 per service call.

Here is how to use a plumbing snake correctly, which type to buy, and when the clog is beyond what this tool can fix.

## Types of Plumbing Snakes and Which You Need

Not all snakes are created equal. The right tool depends on the drain size and clog location.

### Hand Crank Drum Auger (The One to Buy First)

This is the standard homeowner snake. A 25-foot cable coiled inside a drum housing with a hand crank. The cable is typically 1/4-inch diameter, which fits through sink, tub, and shower drains. Cost: $25 to $50.

Best for: bathroom sink clogs, tub drain hair clogs, shower drain backups, and kitchen sink clogs that are in the branch line (between the sink and the main stack).

### Toilet Auger (Closet Auger)

A specialized snake with a protective rubber boot that prevents scratching the porcelain. The cable is shorter (usually 3 to 6 feet) and stiffer, designed specifically for the toilet's built-in trap. Cost: $15 to $30.

If you own a home with toilets, you should own one of these. Never use a regular drain snake in a toilet — you will scratch the bowl.

### Power Drain Snake

An electric or battery-powered machine that spins the cable mechanically. Professional-grade units use 1/2-inch to 3/4-inch cables up to 100 feet long. Homeowner-grade power snakes are smaller and more affordable ($100 to $200) but still significantly more capable than hand snakes.

Best for: recurring clogs, main line blockages accessible through a cleanout, and older homes with chronic drain issues.

### Flat Tape Snake

A flat, flexible metal tape (not a round cable) used for longer runs and larger pipes. Common in 1/2-inch width and lengths up to 50 feet. These are less common for DIY use but available at rental shops.

## How to Snake a Drain: Step by Step

### Snaking a Sink Drain

1. **Remove the P-trap if possible.** Place a bucket underneath, unscrew the slip nuts by hand or with channel-lock pliers, and remove the curved trap section. This gives the snake direct access to the wall pipe and avoids pushing the clog further into the trap.

2. **Feed the cable into the wall pipe.** Push the cable in by hand until you feel resistance. Lock the cable in the drum housing so about 6 inches extends from the drum to the pipe entry.

3. **Crank and push.** Turn the handle clockwise while applying forward pressure. The cable tip spins and bores through soft clogs (soap scum, grease) or hooks into solid obstructions (hair mass, foreign objects).

4. **Work through resistance.** When you hit the clog, you will feel the cable slow down or stop. Keep cranking. The tip is designed to either break through the material or wrap around it so you can pull it back out.

5. **Retrieve and test.** Pull the cable back slowly, cleaning it with a rag as it comes out. You may pull out a mass of hair, soap scum, or other debris. Reassemble the P-trap and run water for two minutes to confirm the drain is clear.

### Snaking a Toilet

1. **Insert the toilet auger.** Feed the cable end into the bowl with the rubber boot resting against the porcelain at the drain opening. The bend in the auger housing guides the cable into the toilet's internal trap.

2. **Crank through the trap.** Turn the handle clockwise while pushing down. The cable navigates the S-shaped trap inside the toilet. Most toilet clogs are within this trap — usually 12 to 18 inches from the bowl opening.

3. **Push through or hook the obstruction.** You will feel when the cable contacts the clog. Continue cranking to either push it into the wider drain line or hook it for extraction.

4. **Pull back and flush.** Slowly retract the cable. Flush the toilet to verify the clog is cleared. If the water drains slowly, repeat the process.

### Snaking a Main Line Through a Cleanout

This is where a hand snake reaches its limits and a power snake becomes necessary.

1. **Locate the cleanout.** It is a capped pipe fitting, usually 3 or 4 inches in diameter, found in the basement, crawl space, or outside the house near the foundation. Some homes have multiple cleanouts.

2. **Open carefully.** Unscrew the cleanout cap slowly. If the main line is backed up, standing water will be behind that cap. Have towels and a bucket ready.

3. **Feed the power snake.** Guide the cable into the cleanout in the direction of the blockage (toward the street for sewer clogs, toward the house for branch clogs). Let the machine do the work — do not force it.

4. **Work the cable back and forth.** When you hit resistance, let the spinning cable work through it. Pull back a foot, then push forward again. Repeat until the cable moves freely.

For main line issues that keep coming back, you may want to explore [trenchless repair options](/blog/trenchless-plumbing-repairs-a-homeowners-guide/) or consult a [plumbing company](/blog/plumbing-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/) for a camera inspection.

## Common Mistakes That Damage Pipes

**Forcing the cable around tight bends.** If the cable will not advance, do not muscle it. Excessive force can kink the cable (ruining it) or puncture older cast iron or clay pipes. Pull back and try a different angle.

**Using the wrong size cable.** A 1/2-inch cable in a 1-1/4-inch sink drain pipe can tangle and get stuck. Match the cable diameter to the pipe: 1/4-inch for 1-1/4 to 2-inch drains, 3/8-inch for 2 to 3-inch drains, 1/2-inch for 3-inch and larger.

**Running a power snake without gloves.** The spinning cable will remove skin instantly if it catches your hand. Wear heavy work gloves every time. Follow [OSHA safety guidelines](https://www.ul.com/) — this is not a theoretical risk.

**Skipping the P-trap removal on sinks.** Feeding a snake through the P-trap works but risks pushing the clog deeper or snagging the cable in the trap. The extra two minutes to remove the trap makes the whole job easier.

**Relying on chemical drain cleaners first.** If you have poured drain cleaner down the pipe and it did not work, now you are snaking a drain full of caustic chemicals. Wear eye protection and gloves. Better yet, skip the chemicals entirely and go straight to the snake.

## When the Snake Is Not Enough

A plumbing snake will not solve every drain problem. Recognize these situations:

**Tree root intrusion.** If your snake hits roots in the main sewer line, a hand snake will not cut through them. You need a power snake with a cutting head, or hydro-jetting. Signs: recurring main line backups, gurgling sounds from multiple drains, green patches in the yard over the sewer line.

**Collapsed pipe.** If the cable will not advance past a certain point no matter what you do, the pipe may be collapsed or crushed. A camera inspection ($200 to $400) is the only way to know for sure.

**Grease buildup in kitchen drains.** A snake punches a hole through a grease clog but does not remove the buildup from the pipe walls. The clog returns in weeks. Hydro-jetting ($350 to $600) is the proper fix for grease-clogged kitchen drain lines.

**Bellied pipe.** A low spot in the drain line collects debris and water, creating chronic clogs. No amount of snaking fixes a grading problem. This requires excavation or [pipe repair](/blog/plumbing-drain-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/).

For guidance on building code compliance for any drain repair or modification, refer to the [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/). Your local jurisdiction may have adopted amendments that differ from the base code.

If you discover water damage during your drain investigation, refer to the [NFPA electrical safety standards](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70) — standing water near electrical components is a safety hazard that takes priority over the drain repair.

## Maintaining Your Snake

A well-maintained snake lasts decades. After each use:

- Wipe the cable clean with a rag as you retract it
- Spray the cable with a light machine oil (WD-40 or similar) to prevent rust
- Let the cable dry fully before coiling it back into the drum
- Replace the head attachment if it becomes bent or worn
- Store in a dry location

## How Often You Should Need to Snake

In a healthy plumbing system, you should rarely need a snake. If you are snaking the same drain more than twice a year, the clog is a symptom of a bigger problem — buildup in the line, root intrusion, or a venting issue that is causing slow drainage.

For kitchen drains, running hot water for 30 seconds after each use and avoiding pouring grease down the drain prevents most clogs. For bathroom drains, a $3 mesh drain screen catches the hair before it enters the pipe. These simple habits make the snake a tool you own for emergencies rather than regular maintenance.
