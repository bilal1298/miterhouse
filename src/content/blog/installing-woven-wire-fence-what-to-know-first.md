Woven wire fencing — sometimes called field fence or farm fence — is one of the most versatile and affordable fencing options for rural and semi-rural properties. It handles livestock containment, garden protection, and property boundary marking at $2 to $5 per linear foot for materials alone, making it roughly half the cost of chain link and a quarter the cost of wood privacy fencing.

Installing it properly, though, is less straightforward than it looks. The wire wants to do its own thing, posts need to be right, and corners are where most DIY installations fall apart. Here is what you actually need to know.

## Understanding Woven Wire Specifications

Woven wire comes in standardized configurations described by a numbering system. A roll labeled "12-1/2-12" means 12 horizontal wires, 1/2-inch spacing between the bottom wires (graduated to wider spacing toward the top), and 12-inch vertical stays.

For residential and small-farm use, common configurations include:

- **47-inch height, 12-1/2 gauge:** General livestock and property boundary. Keeps in goats, sheep, and medium dogs. About $100 to $150 per 330-foot roll.
- **48-inch height, 14-1/2 gauge:** Lighter weight, suitable for garden fencing and small animal exclusion. About $80 to $120 per 330-foot roll.
- **60-inch height, 12-1/2 gauge:** Tall enough for deer pressure areas and larger livestock. About $140 to $200 per roll.

The gauge number refers to the wire thickness — lower numbers mean thicker wire. For any fence expected to contain animals or last more than 10 years, stick with 12-1/2 gauge. The lighter 14-1/2 gauge sags and stretches noticeably within a few years.

## Materials You Will Need

For a 200-linear-foot run of woven wire fence, plan on these materials:

- **Woven wire:** 1 roll (330 feet) — $100 to $200 depending on spec
- **T-posts:** 20 to 25 (one every 8 to 10 feet) — $5 to $8 each
- **Wood corner/end posts:** 4 to 6 (6-inch diameter, 8 feet long, pressure-treated or locust) — $15 to $30 each
- **Corner bracing lumber or pipe:** 4 to 6 pieces — $10 to $20 each
- **Post clips:** 3 per T-post, 1 box — $10 to $15
- **Smooth brace wire:** 1 roll — $20 to $30
- **Fence staples (for wood posts):** 1 box — $8 to $12
- **Concrete for corner posts:** 2 bags per post — $5 to $8 per bag

**Total materials for 200 feet:** $350 to $650.

**Tools:** T-post driver ($30 to $50 if you don't own one), fence stretcher/come-along ($25 to $50), pliers, wire cutters, post hole digger or auger, level, measuring tape, and heavy leather gloves. This wire will cut you if you're not wearing gloves.

## Setting Corner and End Posts

This is the most important part of the entire installation. Woven wire under tension pulls hard on corner and end posts — if they are not properly braced, they will lean inward within the first year.

**Post depth.** Set corner posts at least 3 feet deep, with 6 inches of gravel at the bottom for drainage. Fill with concrete, crown the concrete so water sheds away from the post. Let cure at least 48 hours before stretching wire.

**Bracing.** Each corner needs a horizontal brace running from the corner post to the first line post (or a dedicated brace post set 8 feet away). The brace should be a 4-inch or larger piece of lumber or a galvanized pipe, seated in notches cut into both posts.

Then run a diagonal tension wire from the top of the brace post to the bottom of the corner post, twisted tight with a stick or tension tool. This diagonal wire is what actually holds the corner post upright against fence tension. Skip it and the corner fails — this is the single most common mistake in woven wire installation.

For fence runs over 300 feet, add an in-line braced post assembly at the midpoint to maintain tension.

## Setting Line Posts

T-posts are the standard for line posts in woven wire fencing. They are inexpensive, fast to install, and strong enough for the application.

**Spacing:** 8 feet apart on flat ground, 6 feet apart on slopes or curves. Closer spacing near gates and corners.

**Driving depth:** At least 18 to 24 inches. The anchor plate at the bottom of the T-post should be fully below grade. Use a T-post driver — a heavy steel tube that fits over the top of the post. It takes 15 to 30 good swings per post.

**Orientation:** The studded face of the T-post faces the wire. Clips hook over the studs to hold the wire in place.

A tip that saves time: drive all your T-posts before unrolling any wire. Use a string line between corner posts to keep the line posts straight. Even a few inches of wander shows clearly in the finished fence.

## Stretching and Attaching the Wire

This is the step where woven wire installation differs most from other fencing types, and where having a helper makes a significant difference.

**Unroll the wire along the fence line** on the outside of the posts (the side facing away from what you are containing). Let it relax for an hour or two — wire fresh off the roll has memory and will fight you.

**Attach one end** to the first corner post using fence staples or a tension bar (a steel rod woven through the end of the mesh, then bolted to the post). The tension bar method is stronger and recommended for any fence under significant load.

**Stretch the wire** using a come-along or fence stretcher attached to the far corner post. Pull it tight — tight enough that the horizontal wires are straight and the vertical stays are vertical, but not so tight that the mesh distorts. A properly tensioned woven wire fence has about 1 to 2 inches of deflection when you push on it between posts.

**Attach to line posts** using T-post clips at the top wire, middle, and bottom wire. On wood posts, use fence staples — but do not drive them fully home. Leave about 1/8 inch of play so the wire can expand and contract with temperature changes without the staple cutting through it.

**At corners,** you have two options: cut the wire and re-anchor to both sides of the corner post (stronger) or bend the wire around the post (faster but weaker). For permanent fencing, cut and re-anchor.

## Dealing with Slopes

Woven wire handles gentle slopes well — the rectangular mesh pattern distorts slightly to follow the grade. For slopes steeper than about 15 degrees, you need to adjust.

The standard approach is to keep the bottom wire following the ground contour while the top wire steps. At each T-post on the slope, push the bottom of the wire flush with the ground and clip it. The vertical stays will angle slightly — this is normal and does not affect fence performance.

For steep terrain, consider running the fence in level sections with a gap at grade changes, filled by a short section of wire cut to fit. This is slower but produces a neater result.

## What a Pro Installation Costs

If you would rather hire it out, professional woven wire fence installation runs $5 to $12 per linear foot depending on terrain and post type. For 200 linear feet, expect $1,000 to $2,400 fully installed.

The biggest cost variable is the corner post work — wooden braced corners are more labor-intensive than a stretch of T-posts and wire. A fence with 4 corners costs meaningfully more per foot than a straight 200-foot run with just two end posts.

## Maintenance and Lifespan

Galvanized woven wire lasts 15 to 25 years depending on climate and soil chemistry. In humid areas with acidic soil, the galvanizing corrodes faster — expect the lower end. In dry climates, the upper end or beyond.

Annual maintenance is minimal: walk the fence line once or twice a year, re-tension any sections that have sagged, replace broken clips, and clear vegetation growing into the mesh (it adds wind load and accelerates corrosion at contact points).

The weakest point long-term is always the bottom wire where it contacts soil. If you need maximum longevity, apply a bead of asphalt coating along the bottom 6 inches of the mesh where it meets grade. This adds years to the fence's life in wet climates.

## Where Woven Wire Makes Sense — And Where It Doesn't

Woven wire is the right choice for large-area enclosures, livestock management, garden protection, and rural property boundaries where appearance is secondary to function. It is a poor choice for front-yard residential applications (code may prohibit it, and aesthetics suffer), pool enclosures (most codes require specific pool barrier fencing), or high-security applications.

For those uses, consider [chain link](/blog/chain-link-fence-installation-costs-and-tips/) or other purpose-built options.
