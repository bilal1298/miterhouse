Replacing laminate flooring costs $3 to $8 per square foot for materials and $2 to $5 per square foot for professional installation, putting a typical 300-square-foot room at $1,500 to $3,900 all-in. That range swings based on the quality of laminate you choose, the condition of your subfloor, how much demolition is involved, and whether you tackle the installation yourself or hire it out.

Laminate flooring has a functional lifespan of 10 to 25 years depending on quality, traffic, and maintenance. If your laminate is peeling at the edges, swelling at the seams, showing wear-through on the decorative layer, or simply looks dated after 15 years, replacement is the right call — laminate cannot be refinished like hardwood.

## Material Costs by Quality Tier

Laminate flooring is sold per square foot, and the price reflects the thickness, wear layer quality, locking mechanism, and brand:

**Budget laminate ($0.75 to $2.00/sq ft):** Typically 6-8mm thick with a basic AC3-rated wear layer. Functional for low-traffic areas or rental properties. Thinner laminate feels hollow underfoot, telegraphs subfloor imperfections, and has a less convincing wood-look pattern. Brand examples: TrafficMaster (Home Depot house brand), some Select Surfaces products.

**Mid-range laminate ($2.00 to $4.00/sq ft):** 8-12mm thick with AC4-rated wear layers. This is the sweet spot for most homeowners. Thicker core provides better sound absorption and a more solid feel underfoot. Water-resistant (not waterproof) cores are common in this range. Brand examples: Pergo, Mohawk RevWood, some QuickStep products.

**Premium laminate ($4.00 to $8.00/sq ft):** 12mm+ thick with AC5-rated wear layers. Enhanced water resistance, realistic textures (registered embossing that matches the printed grain pattern), and superior locking mechanisms. At this price point, you are competing with luxury vinyl plank (LVP) — and LVP offers true waterproofing that even premium laminate cannot match. Brand examples: QuickStep Impressive, Pergo TimberCraft, Mohawk RevWood Plus.

**Important note on pricing:** Big-box store promotions frequently put mid-range laminate at budget prices during seasonal sales. If your timeline is flexible, watch for these sales — savings of 30-40% on materials are common during spring and fall flooring events.

## Removal and Disposal Costs

Before new laminate goes down, the old flooring comes up. Laminate removal is relatively straightforward compared to other flooring types:

**Laminate over laminate:** Old click-lock laminate disassembles fairly easily — pry up the first row and the rest typically pulls apart along the locking seams. Budget 1-2 hours per 100 square feet for an experienced DIYer. Professional removal: $1.00 to $2.00 per square foot.

**Laminate that was glued down:** Less common with modern laminate but found in some older installations. Glued laminate is significantly harder to remove — it comes up in chunks and often damages the underlayment beneath. Professional removal: $1.50 to $3.00 per square foot.

**Disposal costs:** Laminate flooring is not recyclable in most areas. Expect $30 to $60 per load at the local dump, or $50 to $150 if the installer includes debris hauling. A 300-square-foot room generates roughly one pickup truck load of old flooring and underlayment.

If you are replacing laminate over a different substrate — say the original laminate was installed over vinyl, tile, or hardwood — removing that lower layer adds both cost and complexity. [Vinyl flooring removal](/blog/vinyl-flooring-removal-pricing-guide-for-homeowners/) in particular can involve adhesive issues and potential asbestos concerns in older homes.

## Subfloor Preparation

The subfloor condition makes or breaks a laminate installation. Laminate is a floating floor — it sits on top of the subfloor without being glued or nailed down — and it telegraphs every imperfection beneath it. High spots create pressure points that wear the laminate prematurely. Low spots create hollow, bouncy areas.

**Concrete subfloor prep ($0.50 to $2.00/sq ft):** Grind down high spots, fill low spots with self-leveling compound, and test for moisture. Concrete moisture testing is critical — laminate manufacturers require the subfloor to test below a specific moisture level (typically 3 lbs per 1,000 sq ft per 24 hours on a calcium chloride test, or below 75% relative humidity on an in-situ probe test). Excessive moisture ruins laminate from below, and the damage is not covered by warranty.

**Wood subfloor prep ($0.50 to $1.50/sq ft):** Sand or plane down high spots, fill gaps, and secure any loose or squeaky boards with screws (not nails — nails work loose). Plywood subfloors are easier to work with than OSB. If the existing subfloor is badly damaged, overlaying a 1/4-inch plywood skin costs $0.75 to $1.50 per square foot but creates a perfectly flat, stable surface.

**Subfloor repair ($2 to $10/sq ft for damaged areas):** [Water damage](/blog/moisture-damage-laminate-flooring-what-homeowners-should-know/) to subfloors is the most common surprise in laminate replacement projects, especially around exterior doors, kitchens, and bathrooms. Soft, spongy, or discolored subfloor sections need to be cut out and replaced before new flooring goes down.

## Underlayment: The Hidden Cost That Matters

Underlayment sits between the subfloor and the laminate, providing sound absorption, slight cushioning, and moisture protection. Every laminate installation needs it, but the type and cost vary:

**Basic foam underlayment ($0.15 to $0.30/sq ft):** Thin polyethylene foam that provides minimal sound dampening and a small amount of cushion. Adequate for upper-floor installations over plywood where sound transmission to rooms below is not a concern.

**Premium foam with moisture barrier ($0.30 to $0.75/sq ft):** Includes a built-in polyethylene moisture barrier. Required for installations over concrete and recommended for any grade-level installation. The moisture barrier prevents ground moisture from reaching the laminate core.

**Cork underlayment ($0.50 to $1.00/sq ft):** Superior sound absorption compared to foam. Feels more solid underfoot. Popular for upper-floor installations in multi-story homes where footstep noise matters.

**Attached underlayment (built into the laminate, no separate cost):** Some mid-range and premium laminate planks come with underlayment pre-attached to the bottom. This saves installation time and eliminates the separate underlayment cost, but limits your ability to choose a premium underlayment product.

Do not double-stack underlayment — using a separate underlayment over laminate that already has attached padding creates too much cushion, stressing the locking joints and causing premature failure.

## Installation Labor Costs

Professional laminate installation runs $2 to $5 per square foot in most markets:

| Room Size | Material Cost (mid-range) | Labor Cost | Total Installed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small room (100 sq ft) | $200-$400 | $300-$600 | $500-$1,000 |
| Medium room (200 sq ft) | $400-$800 | $500-$900 | $900-$1,700 |
| Large room (300 sq ft) | $600-$1,200 | $700-$1,300 | $1,300-$2,500 |
| Whole floor (800 sq ft) | $1,600-$3,200 | $1,600-$3,500 | $3,200-$6,700 |

Factors that push labor costs higher:
- Complex room shapes with many cuts around obstacles
- Stairs (laminate on stairs costs $40 to $100 per step)
- Transitions between rooms or to different flooring types ($50 to $150 per transition)
- Removing and reinstalling baseboards ($1 to $3 per linear foot)
- Moving furniture (some installers include this; many charge extra or require you to move furniture beforehand)

## The DIY Option

Laminate flooring is one of the most DIY-friendly flooring products available. Click-lock laminate requires no specialized tools, no adhesive, and no fasteners. The primary skills needed are accurate measuring, straight cutting, and patience.

**Tools you need ($50 to $150 if you do not own them):**
- Tape measure and pencil
- T-square or straight edge
- Miter saw or circular saw (for crosscuts)
- Jigsaw (for notches around door frames, pipes, and corners)
- Tapping block and pull bar (usually included in a laminate installation kit for $15 to $25)
- Spacers (included in installation kits)
- Utility knife
- Safety glasses and knee pads

**Realistic DIY timeline:** Plan for one full day per 150-200 square feet of straightforward installation. Complex rooms with many cuts, angled walls, or obstacles take longer. A 300-square-foot living room is a weekend project for a first-timer.

**Where DIYers commonly struggle:**
- Getting the first row perfectly straight (this sets the alignment for the entire floor)
- Cutting the last plank in each row to fit (measure twice, cut once — and leave the expansion gap)
- Transitions at doorways and between rooms
- Undercutting door jambs so the laminate slides underneath instead of butting up against the trim
- Maintaining consistent expansion gaps around all edges (1/4 inch minimum, hidden by baseboards)

The DIY savings are substantial — you are eliminating the $2 to $5 per square foot labor cost, which on a 300-square-foot room saves $600 to $1,500. For homeowners comfortable with basic tools and willing to invest a weekend, the return on effort is excellent.

## Laminate vs. Alternatives: When to Switch Materials

If you are replacing laminate, you have a natural decision point — do you install new laminate, or switch to a different material? Consider:

**Luxury Vinyl Plank (LVP), $3 to $7/sq ft:** Waterproof, slightly more durable than laminate, and installs virtually the same way. LVP is the strongest competitor to laminate and has largely overtaken it in market share. The main tradeoff is that LVP can feel softer underfoot and may not look quite as convincing as premium laminate at close range. For kitchens, bathrooms, laundry rooms, and basements, LVP is the better choice because of its waterproof core. See our [vinyl flooring installation cost guide](/blog/install-vinyl-flooring-pricing-guide-for-homeowners/) for details.

**Engineered hardwood, $5 to $15/sq ft:** A real wood veneer over a plywood core. More expensive than laminate but refinishable (once, maybe twice depending on veneer thickness) and indistinguishable from solid hardwood. Not suitable for high-moisture areas.

**Porcelain tile, $5 to $15/sq ft installed:** The most durable option for kitchens and bathrooms. Cold and hard underfoot without radiant heat. Labor-intensive installation pushes the total cost significantly higher than laminate. Standards set by the [Tile Council of North America](https://www.tcnatile.com/) govern proper installation methods.

**Solid hardwood, $8 to $20/sq ft installed:** The premium option. Lasts 50-100 years with periodic refinishing. Not suitable for basements or areas with moisture concerns. Installation requires nailing or stapling — not a floating floor system.

For most rooms in most homes, the laminate-versus-LVP decision is the practical one. If the room stays dry, laminate offers a convincing wood look at a competitive price. If moisture is any concern at all, LVP wins.

## Warranty Considerations

Laminate manufacturers offer warranties ranging from 10 years (budget products) to lifetime (premium products). Understanding what the warranty covers — and what voids it — affects your cost calculation:

**Typically covered:** Wear-through of the decorative layer, structural defects, fading from sunlight, staining from household substances.

**Typically NOT covered:** Damage from water or excessive moisture (the most common laminate failure), damage from improper installation, scratches from furniture or pets, chipping from impacts, damage from failure to acclimate the product before installation.

The moisture exclusion is critical. Laminate's biggest weakness is water, and virtually every manufacturer excludes water damage from their warranty. If your laminate failed due to a dishwasher leak, a pet accident, or high ambient humidity, the warranty will not help. This is another reason to consider waterproof LVP if your replacement location has any moisture exposure.

The [National Wood Flooring Association](https://www.nwfa.org/) provides installation guidelines that align with most manufacturer warranty requirements. Following these guidelines protects both your installation and your warranty coverage.

Budget for 10% extra material beyond your measured square footage to account for cuts, waste, pattern matching, and keeping a few spare planks for future repairs. This is not optional — it is built into every professional flooring estimate, and running short mid-installation costs far more than the extra box of planks.
