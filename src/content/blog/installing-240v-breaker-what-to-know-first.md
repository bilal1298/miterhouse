Installing a 240V breaker is one of those electrical tasks that sits right on the boundary between advanced DIY and call-a-professional territory. If you are adding a circuit for an electric dryer, range, water heater, EV charger, or workshop equipment, you need a 240V double-pole breaker in your main panel. Here is what the job involves, what it costs, and an honest assessment of whether you should tackle it yourself.

## How 240V Circuits Differ from 120V

Standard household outlets run on 120V single-pole circuits: one hot wire, one neutral, and a ground. A 240V circuit uses two hot wires (each carrying 120V on opposite phases), a neutral (for circuits that also need 120V, like ranges with clocks), and a ground. The double-pole breaker connects to both bus bars in your panel, giving you the full 240V between the two hot conductors.

This distinction matters because 240V circuits carry more energy and present greater shock and fire hazards than standard 120V circuits. The wire is larger, the connections must be tighter, and the consequences of mistakes are more severe.

## What a 240V Breaker Installation Costs

If you hire an electrician, expect to pay $200-$500 for a straightforward breaker installation where the panel has available space and the wire run is under 50 feet. More complex installations run higher:

| Scenario | Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Simple breaker add (panel has space, short wire run) | $200-$500 |
| Moderate run (50-100 ft, through finished walls) | $500-$1,000 |
| Panel subpanel addition (panel is full) | $1,200-$2,500 |
| Panel upgrade (100A to 200A) | $2,000-$4,500 |
| Permit and inspection fees | $50-$200 |

The breaker itself costs $10-$40. The wire (6/3 NM-B for a 50-amp circuit, for example) costs $2-$5 per foot. Labor is the primary cost driver.

If you are adding a 240V circuit as part of a larger renovation, coordinating with other electrical work saves on the electrician's trip charge and mobilization. ## Panel Capacity: The First Thing to Check

Before anything else, determine whether your electrical panel can accommodate a new 240V breaker. Open the panel cover (with the main breaker still on — you are just looking, not touching) and count the available spaces.

A double-pole 240V breaker takes two adjacent spaces in the panel. If your panel is full, you have three options:

1. **Tandem breakers:** Some panels accept tandem (piggyback) breakers that fit two 120V circuits into one space, freeing up room for your 240V breaker. Not all panels accept tandems, and there are limits on how many you can install. Check the panel's label for approved tandem positions.

2. **Subpanel:** If the main panel is genuinely full and tandem breakers will not work, adding a subpanel ($800-$2,000 installed) provides additional circuit capacity. The subpanel gets its own breaker in the main panel and can house multiple new circuits.

3. **Panel upgrade:** If your home still has a 100-amp panel and you are adding high-draw 240V loads (EV charger, heat pump, electric range), a panel upgrade to 200 amps may be the right long-term move. This is a significant project ($2,000-$4,500) but solves capacity constraints permanently.

## Wire Sizing: Getting It Right

Wire size must match the breaker amperage. Using undersized wire creates a fire hazard because the wire overheats before the breaker trips. The [National Electrical Code](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70) specifies minimum wire gauges for each circuit amperage:

| Breaker Amperage | Wire Size (copper NM-B) | Common Uses |
|---|---|---|
| 20A | 12/2 | Window AC, small workshop tools |
| 30A | 10/3 | Electric dryer, small water heater |
| 40A | 8/3 | Electric range (smaller), cook top |
| 50A | 6/3 | Electric range, large water heater |
| 60A | 6/3 or 4/3 | EV charger, subpanel feed |

For wire runs over 100 feet, you may need to upsize the wire by one gauge to compensate for voltage drop. A 50A circuit at 150 feet might need 4/3 instead of 6/3. Your electrician (or the NEC voltage drop tables) will specify the correct sizing.

All products should carry appropriate safety certifications. [UL (Underwriters Laboratories)](https://www.ul.com/) listing on breakers and wire confirms they meet safety standards. Never install unlisted electrical components.

## The Actual Installation Process

This section is informational to help you understand what the work involves and communicate with your electrician. If you choose to do this work yourself, verify local regulations first — many jurisdictions require a licensed electrician for panel work.

**Step 1: Turn off the main breaker.** Even with the main breaker off, the service entrance wires above the main breaker remain energized. Do not touch them. Period.

**Step 2: Remove the panel cover.** With the main breaker off, remove the panel cover to access the bus bars and breaker mounting positions.

**Step 3: Route the cable.** Run the appropriately sized NM-B cable from the new outlet location to the panel. Secure the cable with staples every 4.5 feet and within 12 inches of boxes and the panel. Use a cable connector (Romex connector) where the cable enters the panel.

**Step 4: Connect the wires in the panel.** Strip the cable sheath, then connect: the two hot wires (typically black and red) to the double-pole breaker terminals, the neutral (white) to the neutral bus bar, and the ground (bare copper) to the ground bus bar.

**Step 5: Snap in the breaker.** With the breaker in the OFF position, clip it onto the bus bar at the designated position. Ensure it seats firmly and does not wobble.

**Step 6: Install the outlet.** At the other end of the cable, connect the wires to the appropriate NEMA outlet (14-30 for dryers, 14-50 for ranges, 6-50 for EV chargers, etc.). Mount the outlet in a proper electrical box.

**Step 7: Test.** Restore power at the main breaker, then switch on the new 240V breaker. Use a plug-in circuit tester to verify correct wiring at the outlet. Test the breaker by pressing the test button (if it is a GFCI or AFCI type).

## Should You DIY This?

Here is an honest assessment.

**Arguments for DIY:**
- The electrical work itself is straightforward if you follow NEC guidelines precisely
- You save $150-$400 in labor on a simple installation
- Some jurisdictions allow homeowners to pull their own electrical permits

**Arguments against DIY:**
- Working inside an electrical panel exposes you to lethal voltages, even with the main breaker off (service entrance wires remain live)
- Incorrect connections can cause arcing, which may not trip a breaker but can start a wall fire
- Many homeowner's insurance policies do not cover damage from DIY electrical work
- Permit inspections may require a licensed electrician's sign-off

**The practical middle ground:** If you have experience with electrical work, own a proper voltage tester, and understand the NEC requirements for your specific circuit, the work is within reach. If this is your first electrical project beyond replacing a light switch, hire a licensed electrician. The cost savings of DIY do not justify the risk on a project where a mistake can burn down your house.

The [ANSI (American National Standards Institute)](https://www.ansi.org/) develops the standards that electrical products and installations must meet. Familiarizing yourself with applicable standards helps you evaluate whether a contractor's work meets requirements.

## Permits and Inspections

Most jurisdictions require an electrical permit for adding a new circuit. The permit process typically involves:

1. Submit an application describing the work (new 240V circuit, amperage, purpose)
2. Pay the permit fee ($50-$150)
3. Perform the work
4. Schedule a rough-in inspection (if the wire is being run through open walls)
5. Schedule a final inspection after the breaker and outlet are installed

Inspectors check wire sizing, breaker rating, connection quality, grounding, and NEC compliance. They also verify that the panel has adequate capacity for the new load. Passing inspection is not just a formality — it confirms your installation is safe.

## Common 240V Circuit Applications

Here are the most common reasons homeowners add 240V circuits, with specific requirements for each:

**Electric dryer:** 30A circuit, NEMA 14-30 outlet, 10/3 NM-B wire. Modern dryers require a 4-wire connection (two hots, neutral, ground). Older 3-wire setups are grandfathered but should be upgraded during renovation.

**Electric range/oven:** 50A circuit, NEMA 14-50 outlet, 6/3 NM-B wire. Ranges draw significant current during oven preheat and when multiple burners are running simultaneously.

**EV charger (Level 2):** 40A-60A circuit, NEMA 14-50 outlet (for portable EVSE) or hardwired connection (for wall-mounted chargers). A 48A charger requires a 60A circuit (NEC requires the circuit to be rated at 125% of continuous load).

**Electric water heater:** 30A circuit, typically hardwired (no outlet). A dedicated circuit for water heaters is required by code.

**Workshop equipment:** 20A-50A depending on the equipment. A table saw, welder, or air compressor may each need a dedicated 240V circuit.

## The Bottom Line

A 240V breaker installation is a well-defined electrical task with clear code requirements. For homeowners with electrical experience, it is manageable. For everyone else, the $200-$500 cost of hiring a licensed electrician buys safety, code compliance, and peace of mind. Either way, always pull a permit, always verify wire sizing against the NEC tables, and never work on energized circuits. The stakes are too high for shortcuts.
