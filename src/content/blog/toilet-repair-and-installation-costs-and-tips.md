Most toilet repairs cost under $200, and a complete toilet replacement (new toilet plus installation) runs $250–$800 for a standard model or $500–$1,500 for a premium unit. The confusion around toilet costs comes from lumping simple repairs — a $10 flapper replacement — with full bathroom plumbing overhauls. Here's what each scenario actually involves and costs.

## Common Toilet Repairs and What They Cost

### Running Toilet ($10–$75 DIY, $75–$200 pro)

A toilet that runs constantly or intermittently is almost always one of three parts: the flapper, the fill valve, or the flush valve. Diagnosing which one takes about 30 seconds.

**The flapper** ($5–$15) — the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank. Lift the tank lid, flush, and watch. If the flapper doesn't seal cleanly against the flush valve seat, that's your problem. Replace it: turn off the water supply, flush to empty the tank, unhook the old flapper, hook on the new one. Five minutes, no tools required.

**The fill valve** ($8–$25) — the tall mechanism in the left side of the tank that refills it after a flush. If the water keeps running after the tank is full, or the tank takes forever to refill, replace the fill valve. Turn off the water, flush and sponge out remaining water, disconnect the supply line, unscrew the old valve, drop in the new one. Fifteen minutes, adjustable wrench needed.

**The flush valve** ($15–$30) — the assembly the flapper seals against. If the valve seat is corroded or cracked, a new flapper won't seal. Replacing the flush valve requires removing the tank from the bowl — more involved but still a DIY project. Budget 45 minutes.

A plumber charges $75–$200 for any of these repairs, with the minimum service call accounting for most of that cost. Unless you're physically unable to do the work, these are among the most beginner-friendly plumbing repairs that exist.

### Weak or Incomplete Flush ($0–$50)

Before replacing anything, check the obvious: is the water level in the tank at the fill line? Adjust the fill valve to raise the water level if it's low. Is the flapper chain too tight (preventing full opening) or too loose (getting caught under the flapper)? Adjust the chain to have about 1/2 inch of slack.

If those adjustments don't help, mineral buildup in the rim jets (the small holes under the toilet rim) may be restricting water flow. A stiff brush and white vinegar ($3) clean these out. For stubborn buildup, a small Allen wrench or wire can clear individual jets.

For persistent weak-flush issues, a toilet auger ($10–$30) clears partial blockages in the trapway that slow the flush without fully clogging.

### Leaking at the Base ($5–$30 DIY, $100–$250 pro)

Water pooling at the base of the toilet usually means the wax ring seal between the toilet and the floor flange has failed. This is a moderately involved repair:

1. Turn off water supply and flush to empty
2. Disconnect supply line
3. Remove the two bolts holding the toilet to the floor
4. Lift the toilet straight up and set it aside on towels
5. Scrape off the old wax ring from both the toilet bottom and the floor flange
6. Inspect the floor flange for cracks or corrosion
7. Press a new wax ring onto the toilet outlet
8. Set the toilet back onto the bolts, press down firmly, tighten evenly
9. Reconnect supply, turn on water, check for leaks

A standard wax ring costs $3–$8. A wax-free rubber gasket (like Fluidmaster's Better Than Wax) costs $8–$15 and offers a more forgiving installation — useful if you're not confident about getting the alignment right on the first drop.

**Important:** If the floor flange is cracked, broken, or sits below the finished floor surface, you need a flange repair kit ($10–$25) or a plumber to replace the flange ($150–$350). A bad flange means the wax ring can't seal properly no matter how many times you replace it.

### Cracked Tank or Bowl ($150–$800 for replacement)

A cracked tank or bowl can't be repaired — the toilet needs replacement. Hairline cracks in the porcelain will worsen over time, and the catastrophic failure of a toilet tank (usually at the tank-to-bowl bolts) dumps 1.6 gallons of water per flush cycle plus the entire tank volume onto your bathroom floor.

If you notice a crack, replace the toilet rather than waiting. Water damage to subfloor and the ceiling below (in multi-story homes) costs far more than a new toilet.

## Toilet Replacement: Full Cost Breakdown

### The Toilet Itself

| Category | Price Range | What You Get |
|----------|-----------|--------------|
| Builder-grade (round bowl) | $100–$200 | Basic, functional, smaller footprint |
| Mid-range (elongated bowl) | $200–$450 | Comfortable, WaterSense certified, decent flush |
| Premium (one-piece, comfort height) | $400–$800 | Sleek design, powerful flush, easy to clean |
| High-end (wall-hung, smart features) | $800–$3,000+ | Bidet functions, heated seats, tankless designs |

For most bathrooms, a mid-range elongated toilet in the $250–$400 range is the sweet spot. Look for [WaterSense-certified models](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) that use 1.28 gallons per flush or less — they perform as well as older 1.6-gallon models while saving roughly 13,000 gallons of water annually for a family of four.

Comfort-height toilets (seat at 17–19 inches versus the standard 15 inches) are worth the small premium for anyone with mobility concerns. They're easier to sit down on and stand up from. Most new toilets default to comfort height now.

### Installation Labor

Professional toilet installation runs $100–$300 in labor, depending on complexity:

- **Straightforward replacement (same rough-in, no flange issues):** $100–$175
- **Replacement with flange repair:** $175–$300
- **New installation (different rough-in or floor repair needed):** $250–$500+

The "rough-in" is the distance from the wall to the center of the drain pipe, measured from the finished wall surface. Standard is 12 inches. Older homes sometimes have 10-inch or 14-inch rough-ins. Measure before buying — a 12-inch rough-in toilet won't fit a 10-inch space, and a 10-inch toilet on a 12-inch rough-in leaves a gap at the back.

### DIY Installation

Toilet installation is one of the most accessible plumbing DIY projects. You need:

- Adjustable wrench
- Putty knife (for scraping old wax ring)
- Level
- New wax ring or wax-free gasket
- New supply line (braided stainless steel, $5–$10 — always replace this when installing a new toilet)
- Toilet shims ($3 for a pack)
- Silicone caulk

Total DIY time: 1–2 hours for a straightforward swap. The heaviest part of the job is literally lifting the toilet — a standard two-piece toilet weighs 50–80 pounds. A one-piece unit weighs 80–120 pounds. Have a helper for the one-piece.

The [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) specifies minimum clearances for toilet installation: 15 inches from center to any side wall or fixture, and 21 inches of clear space in front. Verify these clearances before purchasing, especially in small bathrooms.

## When to Repair vs. Replace

**Repair if:**
- The issue is a worn flapper, fill valve, or flush valve
- The toilet is less than 10 years old and functions well otherwise
- The bowl and tank are crack-free
- You're satisfied with the style and comfort

**Replace if:**
- The toilet is 20+ years old (older models use 3.5–5 gallons per flush — modern ones use 1.28)
- The bowl or tank is cracked
- The toilet requires frequent repairs (more than once a year)
- You're already renovating the bathroom
- You want to upgrade from round to elongated bowl or standard to comfort height

The water savings alone can justify replacement. Swapping a 3.5-gallon-per-flush toilet for a 1.28-gallon model saves a family of four approximately 16,000 gallons per year. At typical water and sewer rates, that's $50–$100 annually — meaning a $300 toilet pays for itself in 3–6 years through utility savings alone.

## Toilet Brands Worth Considering

Based on plumber feedback and long-term durability data:

- **TOTO** — the professional's choice. Drake and Ultramax lines are virtually bulletproof. Premium price but outstanding flush performance and longevity.
- **Kohler** — wide range from builder-grade to luxury. The Cimarron and Highline models offer excellent value in the mid-range.
- **American Standard** — the Champion 4 is legendary for its powerful flush. Good mid-range value.
- **Swiss Madison** — newer brand with good designs at lower prices. Less long-term track record.

Avoid the cheapest off-brand toilets at big-box stores. The $79 toilet uses lower-grade glazing (meaning the bowl surface is rougher and harder to clean) and internal components that fail sooner. Spending $200–$350 gets you a significantly better toilet that'll last 20+ years without issue.
