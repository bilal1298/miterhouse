Board and batten siding consists of wide boards installed vertically with narrow strips (battens) covering the seams between them. Installing it correctly costs between $5 and $12 per square foot for materials alone, or $8 to $20 per square foot installed professionally. For a typical 1,500-square-foot exterior, that puts the total project between $12,000 and $30,000 depending on material choice and labor costs.

The pattern itself is simple, but the installation details — moisture management, fastening patterns, and movement accommodation — determine whether your siding lasts 5 years or 50.

## Choosing Your Board and Batten Material

The material choice drives everything: cost, maintenance, lifespan, and installation technique.

### Real Wood (Cedar, Pine, Redwood)
- **Cost:** $4 to $10 per square foot for materials
- **Lifespan:** 20 to 40 years with maintenance
- **Pros:** Authentic look, can be stained or painted, repairable by replacing individual boards
- **Cons:** Requires regular maintenance (stain/paint every 3 to 7 years), susceptible to rot and insects if not properly protected
- **Best board dimensions:** 1x10 or 1x12 for boards, 1x2 or 1x3 for battens

Cedar is the traditional choice because of its natural rot resistance. Pine is cheaper but requires more aggressive treatment (pressure-treated or thoroughly primed and painted on all six sides before installation).

### Engineered Wood (LP SmartSide, similar)
- **Cost:** $3 to $7 per square foot for materials
- **Lifespan:** 25 to 50 years
- **Pros:** More dimensionally stable than real wood, factory-primed, better resistance to moisture and insects, consistent quality
- **Cons:** Cannot be stained (paint only), heavier than natural wood, cut ends must be sealed

### Fiber Cement (James Hardie, similar)
- **Cost:** $5 to $12 per square foot for materials
- **Lifespan:** 30 to 50+ years
- **Pros:** Fireproof, rot-proof, insect-proof, extremely durable, takes paint well
- **Cons:** Heavy (requires more robust fastening), creates silica dust when cut (respirator required), more labor-intensive to install

### Vinyl Board and Batten
- **Cost:** $2 to $5 per square foot for materials
- **Lifespan:** 20 to 30 years
- **Pros:** Low maintenance, never needs painting, affordable, lightweight
- **Cons:** Less authentic appearance, expands and contracts significantly, can crack in extreme cold, limited color options

## Pre-Installation: The Critical Steps Most Guides Skip

The siding itself is the visible part. What is underneath it determines whether it actually protects your house.

### House Wrap or Weather-Resistant Barrier

Before any siding goes on, the sheathing must have a weather-resistant barrier (WRB). Tyvek, felt paper, or a similar product creates the primary water defense layer. The siding is the first line of defense against bulk water, but the WRB handles whatever gets past it.

Install the WRB with proper lapping — upper courses overlap lower courses, just like shingles. Tape all seams. Flash around windows, doors, and penetrations with compatible flashing tape.

### Furring Strips (The Rain Screen)

This is the step that separates professional-grade installation from mediocre work. Applying 3/4-inch furring strips vertically over the WRB creates a rain screen — an air gap between the siding and the house that allows moisture to drain down and air to circulate.

The rain screen dramatically improves the siding's longevity by preventing trapped moisture from rotting the boards from behind. For board and batten, the furring strips run horizontally since the siding boards run vertically. Space them 16 to 24 inches apart, aligning with wall studs for secure fastening.

### Flashing Details

Every window, door, and penetration needs head flashing that directs water over and away from the opening. Z-flashing at horizontal joints (where the siding meets the foundation or a band board) prevents water from wicking behind the lower course.

These details take time and add materials cost, but they are non-negotiable. Every siding failure I have seen traces back to a flashing shortcut.

## Installation: Step by Step

### Layout Planning

Board and batten looks best when the layout is balanced — you do not want a narrow sliver of board at one corner and a full-width board at the other. Measure the wall width, decide on your board width (10 to 12 inches is typical), and adjust the starting position so the pattern is symmetrical.

Plan batten placement to cover every board joint. Standard battens are 1.5 to 3 inches wide. The gap between boards (which the batten covers) should be roughly 1/2 inch for wood siding — this allows the boards to expand and contract without pushing against each other.

### Installing the Boards

Start at one corner and work across. Each board gets fastened with stainless steel or hot-dipped galvanized ring-shank nails or screws — never use plain steel fasteners, which will rust and stain. Nail through the center of each board only, not the edges. This allows the board to expand and contract from the center.

For wood boards, drive fasteners into the furring strips (and through to studs where possible). Each board should have fasteners every 24 inches vertically. Stagger end joints between adjacent boards and seal all cut ends with primer or end-grain sealer before installation.

Leave a 1/2-inch gap between boards. Do not butt them tight — wood moves, and tight joints will buckle.

### Installing the Battens

Battens go over the gaps between boards. The key detail: **nail through the gap, not through the boards underneath.** The battens should be fastened independently so the boards behind them can move. If you nail through the batten and into the board edge, you pin the board and force it to crack when it expands.

Use one nail per furring strip location, centered on the gap. The nail penetrates through the gap and into the furring strip behind.

### Corners, Windows, and Trim

Outside corners can be handled with a corner board (a 1x4 or 1x6 installed vertically at the corner, with siding butting against it from both sides) or by mitering the boards (more difficult and often fails as wood moves).

Window and door trim should stand proud of the siding surface slightly. Install trim first, then run siding boards to within 1/4 inch of the trim and caulk the gap with a quality exterior sealant.

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

**Fastening board edges.** The single most common mistake. Nailing near the edge of a board pins it and prevents expansion. When the wood swells from moisture, the board splits along the nail line. Center-nail only.

**Skipping the rain screen.** Direct-applied board and batten traps moisture behind the siding. In humid climates or on north-facing walls, this leads to rot within 5 to 10 years even with naturally rot-resistant wood.

**Using the wrong fasteners.** Standard bright nails corrode and create ugly rust streaks. Worse, they lose holding power. Stainless steel is ideal. Hot-dipped galvanized is acceptable. Electro-galvanized is not durable enough for exterior use.

**Tight board joints.** Leaving zero gap between boards guarantees buckling. Wood expands across the grain — a 10-inch-wide board can grow 1/8 to 1/4 inch in width between dry winter and humid summer.

**Skipping end-grain sealing.** Cut ends of wood boards absorb moisture aggressively. Every cut end should be sealed before installation to prevent wicking that leads to premature rot.

## Cost Breakdown: Materials and Labor

For a wall section of 500 square feet:

| Component | Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Boards (cedar 1x10) | $1,500–$3,000 |
| Battens (cedar 1x3) | $300–$600 |
| Furring strips | $150–$300 |
| House wrap | $100–$200 |
| Fasteners (stainless) | $150–$300 |
| Flashing and trim | $200–$500 |
| Paint/stain | $200–$400 |
| **Materials total** | **$2,600–$5,300** |
| Labor (professional) | $2,500–$5,000 |
| **Installed total** | **$5,100–$10,300** |

The [American Wood Council](https://www.awc.org/) publishes standards for wood construction that inform best practices for wood siding installation. Their guidelines on fastening schedules and moisture management apply directly.

For aging-in-place considerations with exterior projects, the [AARP's housing resources](https://www.aarp.org/livable-communities/housing/) discuss how exterior maintenance factors into long-term home ownership planning.

## Timeline and Practical Expectations

A professional crew can side a typical house (1,500 square feet of siding area) in 3 to 5 days. A DIYer working weekends should plan for 4 to 8 weekends depending on skill level and house complexity.

Weather matters. Do not install wood siding in rain or when rain is expected within 24 hours. The wood should acclimate to your local humidity for at least a week before installation — stack it in a covered, ventilated area near the house.
