Installing laminate flooring runs between $2.50 and $8 per square foot when you add up materials, underlayment, and labor. A 300-square-foot living room project costs $750 to $2,400 depending on the laminate grade and whether you DIY or hire out. That range is wide, so here's exactly where every dollar goes and how to land on the lower end without sacrificing the finished result.

I've tracked costs across five laminate installations — three that I did myself and two where I hired professionals. The biggest takeaway: the material costs are predictable, but the prep and extras are where budgets blow up.

## Material Cost Reality Check

Laminate pricing per square foot tells you a lot about what you're getting, but not everything.

**$0.70 to $1.25 per square foot** gets you a thin plank (6-7mm) with basic imagery and an AC2 or AC3 wear rating. These floors look passable in photos but feel cheap underfoot. The click mechanisms are less forgiving, which means more frustration during installation and more risk of gaps forming over time. Use these only in spaces where longevity isn't the priority.

**$1.50 to $3.00 per square foot** is where value lives. At this price, you get 8mm to 10mm thickness, realistic textures that you can feel with your hand (not just see), decent water resistance at the joints, and click systems that actually work smoothly. I've had good results from Pergo TimberCraft, Shaw Repel, and select Home Decorators Collection lines in this range.

**$3.50 to $6.00 per square foot** buys premium performance. Twelve-millimeter thickness, commercial AC5 ratings, waterproof core technology, and wider planks that create a convincing hardwood look. At the top of this range, seriously consider whether engineered hardwood makes more sense for your situation.

Beyond the planks themselves, you need:
- **Underlayment** ($0.25–$0.75/sq ft) unless your laminate has it pre-attached
- **Transition strips** ($8–$20 each) at every doorway
- **Quarter-round or shoe molding** ($0.50–$1.50/linear foot) if baseboards don't sit flush

## Labor Costs: The Real Range

Professional laminate installation labor runs $1.50 to $3.00 per square foot in most markets. Here's what influences where you land in that range:

**Lower end ($1.50–$2.00/sq ft):**
- Rectangular rooms with minimal cuts
- Clean, flat subfloor needing no prep
- No old flooring to remove
- Standard straight-lay pattern
- Easy access (ground floor, wide doorways)

**Higher end ($2.50–$3.00/sq ft):**
- Rooms with multiple closets, angles, or built-ins
- Subfloor needing leveling or repair
- Old flooring removal included
- Upper floors requiring material hauling
- Complex areas around fireplaces or kitchen islands

**Stair installation** adds $30 to $60 per step regardless of the per-square-foot rate. A standard 13-step staircase with laminate treads runs $390 to $780 on its own. Stairs are genuinely difficult to do well with laminate — the nosing installation and tight cuts around balusters require precision.

## The Prep Work That Decides Everything

I can't emphasize this enough: subfloor preparation is the difference between a floor that looks and performs great for 15 years and one that develops problems within months.

### Concrete Subfloor Prep

**Moisture testing** is mandatory before laminate goes over concrete. The standard test involves taping a 2x2-foot piece of plastic to the slab, sealing all edges with tape, and checking after 48 hours. Any condensation on the underside indicates excessive moisture that needs addressing.

For a more precise measurement, calcium chloride test kits ($25–$35 each) quantify the moisture emission rate. Most laminate manufacturers require results below 3 lbs/1,000 sq ft/24 hours.

**Leveling** concrete requires self-leveling compound for low spots and grinding for high spots. A concrete grinder rents for $50 to $75/day.

### Plywood Subfloor Prep

Walk the entire room slowly and mark every squeak with blue tape. Drive 2-1/2-inch screws through the plywood into joists at each marked spot. This takes $10 in materials and an hour of time, and it eliminates the most common laminate floor complaint.

Check for soft or spongy areas that indicate water damage or delaminated plywood. These sections need cutting out and replacing — not covering up. Laminate over damaged plywood telegraphs every defect.

The [National Wood Flooring Association](https://www.nwfa.org/) provides detailed subfloor assessment guidelines that apply to laminate as well as hardwood installations.

## Installation Tips That Save Time and Money

**Acclimate properly.** Stack unopened boxes in the installation room for 48 hours. Don't stand them on end or pile them in a different room. The material needs to reach the temperature and humidity of the room where it's going.

**Plan your cuts for the morning.** If you're cutting outside or in a garage, do all the measuring in the room, batch the cuts, go cut, and come back. This avoids constant trips back and forth with the saw.

**Use a tapping block religiously.** Never hit the laminate plank directly with a mallet. The tapping block distributes force across the click profile. Direct hits damage the edge and prevent proper engagement.

**Start the second and third rows with different lengths.** Cut the first plank of row 2 to roughly 2/3 length. Cut the first plank of row 3 to roughly 1/3 length. Then use full planks until you reach the end of each row. This creates a natural stagger without constant measuring.

**Leave the last box unopened until you're sure you need it.** Most retailers accept returns of unopened boxes within 30 to 90 days. Ordering 10% extra material is smart, but you might not use it all.

## When Laminate Is the Wrong Choice

Despite its versatility, laminate fails in specific situations:

**Full bathrooms with shower or tub use.** Even waterproof laminate struggles with the sustained moisture levels in a bathroom. Water finds joints and edges, and once it gets underneath, the core swells. [LVP or tile is better for bathrooms](/blog/ceramic-flooring-installation-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/).

**Basements with any history of water intrusion.** One flood event can destroy an entire laminate floor beyond repair. If your basement has ever had water issues, choose LVP or tile that can survive being submerged.

**Rooms with significant direct sunlight.** Some laminate products fade noticeably in areas with heavy UV exposure. Check the product specifications for UV resistance if you have large south-facing windows.

**Over radiant heating systems.** Not all laminate is compatible with radiant floor heat. The adhesive and core materials can break down at elevated temperatures. Check the manufacturer's specification for maximum surface temperature — typically 80-85 degrees Fahrenheit.

## Getting the Best Price

Three strategies that consistently deliver savings:

**Buy during slow season.** January and February pricing on laminate is typically 10-20% lower than spring and summer. Installers are also more available and sometimes negotiate lower rates.

**Price-match aggressively.** Home Depot, Lowe's, and Floor & Decor all have price-match policies. Find the best price online or at a competitor and bring it to your preferred retailer. A $0.50/sq ft reduction on 300 square feet saves $150.

**Consider the hybrid approach.** DIY the easy rooms (rectangular bedrooms with clear subfloors) and hire a pro for complex spaces (kitchens, stairs, large open plans). This lets you save labor on the straightforward work while getting professional quality where it matters most.

Laminate flooring installation is one of the most accessible and budget-friendly home upgrades available. Whether you spend $300 on a bedroom or $6,000 on a whole house, the return in comfort and appearance is immediate and significant.
