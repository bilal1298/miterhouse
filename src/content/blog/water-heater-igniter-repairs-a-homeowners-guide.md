When your gas water heater will not light, the igniter is the first suspect — and in about 60% of cases, it is the actual culprit. A failed igniter means no hot water, and while the repair is often straightforward enough for a handy homeowner, understanding what is actually broken and what it takes to fix it saves you from unnecessary part purchases and potential safety mistakes.

Replacing a water heater igniter yourself costs $15 to $50 for the part and takes 30 to 60 minutes. A professional service call runs $150 to $300 including the part. Either way, this is one of the cheaper water heater repairs compared to a full unit replacement at $800 to $2,000.

## Understanding Your Ignition System

Modern residential gas water heaters use one of three ignition types, and knowing which one you have determines your diagnostic and repair approach.

### Piezo Igniter (Manual Spark)

Found on many standard models, this system uses a button you press to generate a spark that lights the pilot. The piezo element is a crystal that produces voltage when compressed. When it fails, pressing the button produces no click and no spark.

These are the simplest to diagnose and cheapest to replace — the piezo element costs $8 to $20 and snaps into place.

### Hot Surface Igniter (HSI)

Used on newer power-vent and direct-vent models, the HSI works like a glow plug. An electrical current heats a silicon carbide or silicon nitride element to roughly 1,800 degrees, igniting the gas directly without a standing pilot. When it fails, you get nothing — no glow, no ignition, no hot water.

HSI elements cost $20 to $50 and are specific to your water heater model. They are fragile — touching the element with bare hands deposits oils that create hot spots, causing premature failure.

### Electronic Spark Ignition

These systems use an electronic control module to generate a spark, light the pilot (or main burner directly), and verify the flame through a flame sensor. When the igniter portion fails, you may hear clicking but see no spark, or the spark may be too weak to ignite the gas.

The spark electrode costs $15 to $35. However, if the control module itself has failed, the replacement cost jumps to $80 to $200 for the module, and this is where professional diagnosis pays for itself — you do not want to replace a $50 electrode when the $150 module is the actual problem.

## Diagnosing the Problem

Before ordering parts, confirm that the igniter is actually the issue. Work through these checks in order:

**1. Verify gas supply.** Check that the gas valve on the supply line is open (handle parallel to the pipe). Check that other gas appliances in your home are working. If nothing gas-powered is working, the issue is your gas supply, not the igniter.

**2. Check the gas control valve.** The control knob on the heater should be set to "pilot" for lighting. If it is set to "off," turn it to "pilot." If it is already on "pilot" and you are trying to light the pilot, hold the knob down while pressing the igniter — holding the knob opens the pilot gas valve.

**3. Listen and look.** When you press the igniter button or the electronic system cycles, what happens?
- **No click, no spark:** The piezo element has failed, or the electronic ignition module has lost power. Check the wiring connections and the power source (some systems use batteries or a 120V outlet).
- **Click but no spark visible:** The spark electrode may be cracked, corroded, or positioned too far from the pilot orifice. Inspect it visually.
- **Spark but no ignition:** The gas is not reaching the pilot orifice. The pilot tube may be clogged (common in dusty environments), or the gas control valve may be faulty.
- **Ignition followed by immediate flame-out:** This is typically a thermocouple or thermopile issue, not an igniter problem. The flame sensor is not detecting the pilot flame, so the gas valve closes as a safety measure.

**4. Test the thermocouple/thermopile.** If the pilot lights but goes out when you release the control knob, the thermocouple (on older units) or thermopile (on newer units) needs replacement. This is the most commonly misdiagnosed "igniter" problem. A thermocouple costs $10 to $25 and takes 15 minutes to replace.

## Replacing a Piezo Igniter

This is the most common DIY igniter repair.

**Tools needed:** Adjustable wrench, needle-nose pliers, lighter or long match (for testing).

1. Turn the gas control to "off" and close the gas supply valve. Wait 10 minutes for any residual gas to dissipate.
2. Remove the burner access panel at the bottom of the heater.
3. Locate the piezo igniter — it is a small wire running from the igniter button to a spark electrode near the pilot assembly.
4. Disconnect the wire from the old electrode. On most models, it pulls straight off or has a small clip.
5. Remove the old electrode — it is typically held by a single screw or a friction fit.
6. Install the new electrode in the same position, ensuring the spark gap matches the original (usually 3/16 to 1/4 inch from the pilot orifice).
7. Reconnect the wire. Replace the access panel.
8. Open the gas supply, set the control to "pilot," hold the knob down, and press the igniter. You should see a spark and the pilot should light within 2 to 3 clicks.

## Replacing a Hot Surface Igniter

HSI replacement requires slightly more care due to the fragility of the element.

1. [Shut off the water heater](/blog/how-to-shut-off-water-heater-without-wrecking-anything/) — turn the control to "off" and close the gas supply.
2. Disconnect the electrical supply (unplug the unit or turn off the breaker for power-vent models).
3. Remove the burner assembly access panel.
4. Locate the HSI element — it is a small ceramic piece with a dark element extending from it, mounted near the burner.
5. Disconnect the wiring connector. Note the routing of the wires.
6. Remove the mounting screw(s) holding the HSI bracket.
7. Install the new HSI. Handle it by the ceramic base only — do not touch the heating element. Skin oils cause hot spots that lead to early failure.
8. Reconnect the wiring and reassemble.
9. Restore gas and electrical supply. The system should cycle on, glow the HSI for 15 to 30 seconds, then open the gas valve for ignition.

## When to Call a Professional

Igniter replacement is DIY-appropriate in many cases, but call a [licensed plumber or HVAC tech](/blog/plumbing-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/) when:

- You smell gas at any point during diagnosis or repair. Leave the area, do not flip any switches, and call your gas utility's emergency line.
- The problem persists after replacing the igniter. The gas control valve itself may be faulty, and this is not a DIY-safe replacement on most models.
- Your water heater uses a sealed combustion chamber that requires special tools to access the burner assembly.
- You are not confident working around gas connections. This is not a judgment — it is a legitimate safety boundary.

Per [NFPA code requirements](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70), gas appliance repairs should be tested for gas leaks using an approved leak detection solution (or an electronic gas detector) at every connection point after reassembly. Apply the solution to every joint and watch for bubbles. If you see any, tighten the connection and retest. If bubbles persist, shut off the gas and call a professional.

## Preventing Igniter Problems

Most igniter failures are caused by three things:

**Dust and debris.** The burner area collects dust, pet hair, and lint, which can clog the pilot orifice and coat the spark electrode. Vacuum around the base of the water heater monthly and clean the burner area during [annual flushing](/blog/how-often-should-i-flush-my-water-heater-honest-guide/).

**Moisture.** Water heaters in damp basements or utility rooms are prone to corrosion on electrical contacts and spark electrodes. A dehumidifier in the space helps.

**Age.** Thermocouples and igniter elements have a typical lifespan of 5 to 10 years. If your heater is in that range and the igniter is original, proactive replacement during routine maintenance is cheaper than an emergency service call when it fails on a Sunday morning.

A functioning igniter is not optional — it is the gateway to hot water in your house. Keeping it maintained, knowing how to diagnose problems, and understanding when DIY stops and professional help starts will keep your hot water flowing reliably for years.
