Chain wire fence (also called chain link) is the most cost-effective fencing option for residential properties, running $8 to $25 per linear foot installed. A 200-foot perimeter fence typically costs $1,600 to $5,000 depending on height, gauge, coating, and whether you DIY or hire a contractor. It is also one of the more realistic DIY fence projects — the skills required are learnable, and the tools are available at any rental center.

The trade-off is aesthetics. Chain wire does not win beauty contests. But for securing a yard, containing pets, defining property boundaries, or protecting a garden, it delivers years of reliable service at a fraction of what wood or vinyl fencing costs.

## Material Costs Breakdown

Chain wire fencing has fewer components than most fence types, which keeps the cost straightforward:

### The Mesh (Chain Wire Fabric)

| Specification | Cost Per Linear Foot (4 ft tall) | Cost Per Linear Foot (6 ft tall) |
|---|---|---|
| 11-gauge galvanized | $3-$5 | $5-$8 |
| 11-gauge vinyl-coated (green/black) | $5-$8 | $7-$12 |
| 9-gauge galvanized (heavier duty) | $5-$7 | $7-$10 |
| 9-gauge vinyl-coated | $7-$10 | $9-$14 |

**Gauge explained:** Lower gauge numbers mean thicker wire. Standard residential chain wire is 11 or 11.5 gauge. If you are building to contain large dogs, secure a commercial property, or want maximum durability, step up to 9 gauge. The price difference is modest and the strength improvement is substantial.

**Mesh size:** Standard residential chain wire has 2-inch diamond openings. Smaller openings (1 inch or smaller) are available for pet containment (keeping small dogs and cats from squeezing through) but cost significantly more.

### Posts

Chain wire fence uses three types of posts:

**Line posts (intermediate):** Placed every 10 feet along the fence run. Galvanized steel pipe, typically 1-5/8" diameter for 4-foot fences, 2" for 6-foot fences.
- Cost: $8-$15 each

**Terminal posts (end, corner, and gate posts):** Heavier gauge pipe (2-3/8" to 2-7/8" diameter) at every end, corner, and gate location. These posts handle the tension from the stretched fence fabric.
- Cost: $15-$30 each

**Gate posts:** Same as terminal posts but with hinge and latch hardware mounted.
- Cost: $20-$35 each (before hardware)

### Hardware and Fittings

The hardware for chain wire fences is specialized but inexpensive:

- **Top rail (1-3/8" pipe):** $2-$4/linear foot. Runs along the top of the fence between terminal posts. Keeps the fence line straight and adds rigidity.
- **Tension wire:** $0.15-$0.30/linear foot. Runs along the bottom and sometimes the middle of the fence for additional rigidity.
- **Tension bars and bands:** $2-$5 per terminal post. Used to secure the fabric ends to terminal posts.
- **Post caps:** $1-$3 each. Prevent water from entering the open top of line posts.
- **Tie wires:** $5-$10 per bag. Secure the fabric to the top rail and line posts.
- **Gate hardware (hinges, latches, drop rods):** $20-$60 per gate depending on size and style.

### Total Material Cost Examples

| Project | Material Cost Range |
|---|---|
| 100 ft, 4 ft tall, galvanized | $600-$1,000 |
| 150 ft, 4 ft tall, vinyl-coated | $1,100-$1,800 |
| 200 ft, 6 ft tall, galvanized | $1,500-$2,500 |
| 200 ft, 6 ft tall, vinyl-coated | $2,000-$3,500 |

## Professional Installation Costs

Hiring a fence contractor adds $5-$15 per linear foot for labor:

| Project Size | Total Installed Cost (4 ft galvanized) | Total Installed Cost (6 ft vinyl-coated) |
|---|---|---|
| 100 linear feet | $1,000-$2,000 | $1,800-$3,500 |
| 150 linear feet | $1,500-$3,000 | $2,700-$5,250 |
| 200 linear feet | $2,000-$4,000 | $3,600-$7,000 |

Professional installation typically takes 1-2 days for a standard residential perimeter. The crew brings their own tools, including a power auger for post holes, and most companies include old fence removal in the quote if needed.

## DIY Installation: Step by Step

Chain wire fence installation is more physically demanding than technically difficult. If you are comfortable using basic tools and can commit a full weekend, this is a viable DIY project.

### Tools You Need

**Own or buy:**
- Tape measure
- String line and stakes
- Level
- Pliers (fence pliers are ideal)
- Wrenches for bolt assemblies
- Wire cutters
- Come-along or fence stretcher (rental)
- Hacksaw or angle grinder for cutting pipe

**Rent:**
- Post hole digger or power auger ($50-$100/day)
- Fence stretcher / come-along ($30-$50/day)

### Step 1: Layout and Planning

Mark all post locations with stakes. Terminal posts go at every corner, end, and gate opening. Line posts go every 10 feet between terminal posts. Pull a string line at the intended fence height to visualize the finished fence and check for clearance issues.

Call 811 for underground utility locating before you dig anything. This is free, required by law, and prevents potentially fatal encounters with buried electrical or gas lines.

### Step 2: Dig Post Holes and Set Posts

**Hole specifications:** 8-10 inches in diameter, 24-30 inches deep for standard installations. In frost zones, go below the frost line (see our [frost fence installation guide](/blog/frost-fence-installation-costs-and-tips/) for depth requirements by region).

**Terminal posts first.** Set the end, corner, and gate posts first in concrete. These establish the fence line. Use a level to make sure they are plumb, and brace them until the concrete sets.

**Line posts next.** After terminal posts are set and cured (minimum 24 hours), run a string line between them at the correct height. Set line posts to this line, checking plumb as you go.

**Post height above ground:**
- For a 4-foot fence: terminal posts 50 inches above ground, line posts 46 inches (2 inches below the top rail)
- For a 6-foot fence: terminal posts 74 inches above ground, line posts 70 inches

### Step 3: Install Top Rail

Slide the top rail through the loop caps on line posts and into the rail end cups at terminal posts. Top rail sections are typically 10.5 feet long (to span the 10-foot post spacing with overlap). Connect sections with internal sleeves.

[Simpson Strong-Tie](https://www.strongtie.com/) makes connectors and brackets that can be useful for non-standard fence configurations, though chain wire fencing primarily uses its own proprietary fitting system.

### Step 4: Hang and Stretch the Fence Fabric

This is the step where technique matters most:

1. **Unroll the fabric** along the outside of the fence line (the side facing away from your property).
2. **Slide a tension bar** through the end links of the fabric. Attach the tension bar to the first terminal post using tension bands and bolts.
3. **Unroll the fabric** along the fence line to the next terminal post, keeping it loosely against the posts.
4. **Stretch the fabric** using a fence stretcher (come-along) attached to the far terminal post via another tension bar. Pull the fabric tight enough that the diamonds are slightly elongated but not visibly distorted. Over-stretching deforms the mesh permanently.
5. **Cut the excess fabric** by unweaving a single wire at the right length.
6. **Tie the fabric** to the top rail and line posts using tie wires every 24 inches along the top rail and every 12 inches on line posts.

**The stretching step is where DIY installations most often go wrong.** Under-stretched fabric sags and looks amateur. Over-stretched fabric pulls posts inward and distorts the diamond pattern. Rent a proper fence stretcher — do not try to tension the fabric with just a come-along and a 2x4. Watch a few tutorial videos specifically on this step before starting.

### Step 5: Install Bottom Tension Wire and Gates

Run tension wire along the bottom of the fence, threading it through the bottom diamonds and securing it to terminal posts. This prevents the fabric from being lifted from below (dogs, wind, intruders) and gives the fence a finished look.

Hang gates last. Gate frames are pre-assembled with fabric already attached. Mount hinges to the gate post, hang the gate, and install the latch on the opposite post. Adjust until the gate swings freely and latches securely.

## Improving Chain Wire Fence Appearance

Raw galvanized chain wire is utilitarian. Here are practical upgrades that improve aesthetics:

**Vinyl-coated fabric (black or green):** The simplest upgrade. Black coated chain wire is significantly less visually intrusive than silver galvanized. It blends into landscaping and becomes nearly invisible at a distance.

**Privacy slats:** Woven through the diamond mesh, these come in multiple styles:
- Vertical slats: $3-$6/linear foot. 85-90% privacy.
- Hedge-style slats: $5-$10/linear foot. Looks like a green hedge from a distance.
- Winged slats: $4-$7/linear foot. Good balance of privacy and wind resistance.

**Planting along the fence:** A row of arborvitae, privet, or climbing vines (jasmine, clematis, ivy) transforms chain wire into a living fence over 2-3 growing seasons. The fence provides the structure; the plants provide the beauty.

**Aluminum or composite topper:** Some homeowners install a decorative rail or lattice topper above the chain wire to add visual interest. This hybrid approach costs less than a full decorative fence.

## Chain Wire vs. Other Fence Types

| Factor | Chain Wire | Wood Privacy | Vinyl |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost per linear foot (installed) | $8-$25 | $20-$45 | $25-$50 |
| Lifespan | 15-25 years | 15-20 years | 25-30 years |
| Maintenance | Minimal | Stain/seal every 2-3 years | Occasional cleaning |
| Privacy | Low (without slats) | High | High |
| DIY difficulty | Moderate | Moderate-High | Moderate |
| Wind resistance | Excellent | Poor (solid panels) | Moderate |
| Pet containment | Excellent | Good | Good |

Chain wire wins on cost, durability, and wind resistance. It loses on privacy and aesthetics. For pure boundary fencing, pet containment, or garden protection, chain wire is hard to beat economically.

The [EPA WaterSense program](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) promotes water-efficient landscaping, which is relevant if you are planning to add vegetation along your new fence line.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Not checking property lines.** Install the fence even 6 inches over your property line and you may be forced to move it. Property surveys cost $300-$600 and eliminate this risk entirely.

**Skipping the bottom tension wire.** Without it, the mesh curls up over time, creating gaps at the bottom that pets can escape through and that look sloppy.

**Under-stretching the fabric.** A saggy chain wire fence looks neglected from day one. Rent the proper stretcher tool and take the time to tension each section correctly.

**Setting gate openings too narrow.** Plan your gate opening to accommodate whatever needs to pass through it. A 4-foot gate works for people and push mowers. You need a 10-12 foot double gate for vehicle access, riding mowers, or moving large items.

**Using the wrong gauge for the purpose.** If the fence needs to contain 80-pound dogs, standard 11.5 gauge residential mesh may not hold up to repeated impacts. Step up to 9 gauge.

## Bottom Line

Chain wire fencing costs $8-$25 per linear foot installed and provides reliable service for 15-25 years with minimal maintenance. DIY installation saves 40-50% of the installed cost and is achievable for homeowners with moderate physical fitness and basic tool skills. The critical steps are setting posts plumb and deep enough, and properly stretching the fabric — get those right and the fence will serve you well for decades.

Budget $1,500-$3,000 for a typical 150-foot residential perimeter in 4-foot galvanized chain wire, or $2,500-$5,000 for 6-foot vinyl-coated. Add $200-$600 for each gate depending on size. If aesthetics matter, vinyl-coated mesh in black combined with some strategic landscaping turns functional chain wire into something that genuinely looks good in a residential setting.
