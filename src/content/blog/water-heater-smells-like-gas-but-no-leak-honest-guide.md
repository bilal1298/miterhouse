If your water heater smells like gas but you cannot find a visible leak with soapy water on the connections, you are dealing with one of several specific situations — and some are more urgent than others. The smell might be a pilot light issue, a burner problem, a vent malfunction, or even a false alarm from a different source entirely. But you need to rule out the dangerous possibilities first before diagnosing the nuisance ones.

Here is a systematic approach to finding the source and deciding what action to take.

## First: The Safety Checklist

Before doing anything else, run through these steps in order:

1. **Do NOT flip any light switches, use lighters, or create any spark.** If there is an actual gas accumulation, a spark can ignite it.
2. **Open windows and exterior doors** near the water heater to ventilate the area.
3. **Check whether you smell gas only near the water heater or throughout the house.** If the smell is throughout the house or very strong, leave immediately and call your gas utility's emergency line from outside. Do not re-enter until they clear the home.
4. **If the smell is localized and mild,** you can investigate further using the diagnostic steps below.

Per [OSHA safety standards](https://www.osha.gov/), any environment with a suspected gas leak should be ventilated before investigation. Your gas utility will send someone out for free — they have combustible gas detectors that read parts per million, far more sensitive than your nose.

## The Most Common Causes (When There Is No Visible Leak)

### Pilot Light Is Out or Malfunctioning

On older water heaters with standing pilot lights, the most common cause of a gas smell is a pilot that has gone out. When the pilot extinguishes, a small amount of unburned gas escapes before the thermocouple safety shuts off the gas valve. That residual gas is what you smell.

**How to check:** Look through the viewing window at the bottom of the water heater. You should see a small blue flame. If there is no flame, the pilot is out.

**The fix:** Follow the relighting instructions on the label of your water heater — they vary slightly by manufacturer, but the general process is:
1. Turn the gas valve to "off" and wait 5 minutes for residual gas to dissipate
2. Turn the valve to "pilot"
3. Press and hold the pilot button while using the igniter (or a long-reach lighter on older units)
4. Hold the pilot button for 30–60 seconds after the flame lights to heat the thermocouple
5. Release and turn the valve to "on"

If the pilot will not stay lit after multiple attempts, the thermocouple is likely failed — a $10–$20 part that you can replace yourself or have a plumber do for $100–$200. ### Dirty or Clogged Burner

A burner caked with dust, lint, or debris does not combust gas completely. Incomplete combustion produces a sulfur-like smell that mimics a gas leak but is actually unburned gas byproducts.

**Signs it is the burner:** The flame is yellow or orange instead of blue, the smell is strongest when the burner is actively firing, and you may notice soot deposits around the burner chamber.

**The fix:** Turn off the gas valve. Remove the burner assembly access panel (usually two screws or clips at the bottom of the water heater). Use a vacuum with a narrow attachment to clean the burner ports and the combustion chamber. A wire brush or compressed air helps clear individual burner ports. Reassemble and check the flame — it should be mostly blue with small yellow tips.

If cleaning does not restore proper combustion, the burner assembly may need replacement ($40–$80 for the part, $150–$300 installed).

### Exhaust Vent Backdrafting

Atmospheric vent water heaters (the most common type with a metal vent pipe going up through the roof) rely on natural draft to pull exhaust gases up and out. If the draft reverses — due to negative air pressure in the house, a blocked vent, or a disconnected vent pipe — exhaust gases containing small amounts of unburned gas come back into the room.

**Signs of backdrafting:**
- Gas smell strongest near the top of the water heater where the vent connects
- Moisture or condensation on the vent pipe or nearby surfaces
- The water heater room feels overly warm when the burner is running
- A match or lighter held near the vent draft hood gets pulled away from the vent (should be pulled toward it)

**Common causes:**
- Bathroom exhaust fans, range hoods, or clothes dryers creating negative pressure that overpowers the water heater's draft
- Vent pipe disconnected at a joint (check every connection point from the heater to the roof)
- Bird nest or debris blocking the vent cap on the roof
- Vent pipe undersized for the BTU rating of the water heater

This is a carbon monoxide risk, not just a nuisance smell. If you suspect backdrafting, stop using the water heater until the vent system is inspected and repaired. A CO detector near the water heater is essential — if you do not have one, install one today (about $25–$40). Per the [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/), gas water heaters must have properly functioning exhaust venting.

### Gas Valve Seepage

Gas control valves develop tiny seepage over time — not enough to produce bubbles in a soapy water test, but enough for your nose to detect. Mercaptan (the odorant added to natural gas) is detectable at concentrations as low as 1% of the lower explosive limit, so even microscopic seepage registers as a smell.

**How to confirm:** Your gas utility's technician has a combustible gas detector that can pinpoint low-level seepage at valve connections and the gas control assembly. Request this specific test when they come out.

**The fix:** If the leak is at a threaded connection, the plumber removes the fitting, applies new gas-rated Teflon tape or pipe dope, and reconnects. If the gas control valve itself is leaking, it needs replacement — $150–$300 for the part, $200–$400 installed. On older water heaters (10+ years), a failed gas valve often makes replacement of the entire unit more economical.

### Sulfur in the Water (The False Alarm)

Hot water that smells like rotten eggs is often mistaken for a gas leak. The smell comes from hydrogen sulfide gas produced by bacteria reacting with the anode rod inside the tank — not from a gas leak at all.

**How to tell the difference:** Run the hot water at a faucet far from the water heater. If you smell sulfur at the faucet but not in the air around the water heater, it is the water, not the gas.

**The fix:** Replace the magnesium anode rod with an aluminum/zinc anode rod ($25–$50). Flush the tank with hydrogen peroxide (a few quarts of 3% peroxide into the tank) to kill the bacteria. ## What a Professional Gas Leak Inspection Involves

When a plumber or gas technician comes to investigate, here is what a thorough inspection looks like:

1. **Combustible gas detector sweep** around every gas connection, the gas valve, the burner area, and along the gas supply line
2. **Gas pressure test** — they connect a manometer to the gas line and check that the pressure holds steady with the burner off. A drop indicates a leak somewhere in the system
3. **Combustion analysis** — measuring CO levels in the exhaust to verify complete combustion
4. **Draft test** — verifying the vent is drawing properly under operating conditions
5. **Visual inspection** of all connections, the flex connector (they have a limited life and should be replaced if kinked, corroded, or over 10 years old), and the vent system

According to the [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/water-heating), gas water heaters should be inspected annually as part of routine maintenance — a practice that catches most of these issues before they become concerning.

## When the Answer Is Replacement

If your water heater is over 10 years old and you are dealing with gas smell issues, run the math on repair versus replacement. A new gas water heater installed costs $1,200–$2,500. If the gas valve is failing, the burner is corroded, and the vent system needs updates, repair costs can approach 50–75% of a new unit — and the old tank is still aging.

## The Key Takeaway

A gas smell near your water heater without a visible leak is almost always one of five things: a pilot light out, a dirty burner, a backdrafting vent, a microscopic valve seepage, or sulfur in the water masquerading as gas. The first four warrant immediate attention and proper diagnosis. Call your gas utility first — the inspection is free and they have better detection equipment than any DIY method. Once you know the cause, most fixes are straightforward and cost under $300.

Never ignore a gas smell and never assume it will go away on its own. It is one of those situations where being cautious costs nothing, but being wrong costs everything.
