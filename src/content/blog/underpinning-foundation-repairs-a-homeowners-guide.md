Foundation underpinning costs $1,000 to $3,000 per pier or section for standard residential work, with most whole-house underpinning projects running $10,000 to $50,000 depending on the number of support points needed, the method used, and the soil conditions on your property. This is one of the most expensive structural repairs a homeowner can face, and it is also one of the most important — a failing foundation threatens the entire structure above it.

Underpinning extends your existing foundation deeper into the ground or transfers its load to a more stable soil layer. It is not the same as crack repair, waterproofing, or cosmetic foundation patching. Those address symptoms. Underpinning addresses the structural cause: a foundation that is settling, shifting, or no longer bearing on competent soil.

## When Underpinning Is Actually Needed

Not every foundation crack means you need underpinning. Foundations crack for many reasons, and most are not structural emergencies. You need underpinning when the foundation is actively moving — settling unevenly, shifting laterally, or sinking — because the soil beneath it can no longer support the load.

**Signs that suggest underpinning may be needed:**
- Cracks wider than 1/4 inch that are growing over time (measure and photograph them monthly)
- Stair-step cracks in block or brick foundations following the mortar joints
- Doors and windows that no longer close properly or have visible gaps
- Visible slope in floors (use a level — 1/2 inch over 15 feet is within normal tolerance; anything more warrants investigation)
- Separation between the foundation wall and the sill plate or framing above
- Water infiltration at new locations that were previously dry

**Signs that probably do not require underpinning:**
- Hairline cracks less than 1/16 inch wide that have not changed in years
- Cracks that formed during the first few years after construction (concrete shrinkage)
- Efflorescence (white mineral deposits) on basement walls
- Minor moisture seepage through existing cracks (waterproofing, not underpinning)

The critical first step is hiring a structural engineer — not a foundation repair contractor — to evaluate your foundation. A structural engineer works for you and has no financial interest in selling you repairs. Their inspection ($300 to $700) provides an objective assessment of whether underpinning is needed, what method is appropriate, and how extensive the work must be. This evaluation pays for itself many times over by preventing unnecessary work or ensuring the right solution is chosen.

## Understanding Underpinning Methods

There are four main approaches to residential underpinning, each suited to different soil conditions and structural situations:

### Push Piers (Steel Resistance Piers)

**Cost:** $1,000 to $2,000 per pier, typically 6-12 piers per home

Push piers are steel tubes driven hydraulically through the existing foundation footing down to competent load-bearing soil or bedrock. The weight of the house is then transferred from the failing shallow soil to the deep stable layer through these steel shafts.

**Best for:** Settlement caused by soil compaction, shrinkage, or organic decomposition beneath the footing. Works in most soil types. Can often lift the foundation back toward its original position.

**Limitations:** Requires access to the foundation footing (exterior excavation or interior access). The home must be heavy enough to provide reaction force for driving the piers. Light structures like garages or additions may not provide enough weight.

### Helical Piers

**Cost:** $1,500 to $3,000 per pier

Helical piers are steel shafts with helical (screw-shaped) plates welded at intervals. They are mechanically screwed into the ground to the target depth. Unlike push piers, they do not rely on the building's weight for installation — the helical plates pull themselves into the soil.

**Best for:** New construction foundations, light structures, and situations where the building weight is insufficient for push piers. Also effective for resisting uplift forces in areas prone to expansive soils.

**Limitations:** More expensive per pier than push piers. Installation generates more vibration and noise. Helical plates can encounter refusal in rocky soil before reaching adequate depth.

### Mass Concrete Underpinning (Traditional Method)

**Cost:** $500 to $1,500 per section

This is the oldest underpinning method: excavating beneath the existing foundation in controlled sections and pouring new concrete to extend the footing depth. Work progresses in small segments (typically 3-4 feet wide) to avoid undermining the existing foundation.

**Best for:** Deepening a foundation for basement conversion or when the existing footing is too shallow. Common in older homes where the original foundation was set on an inadequate footing.

**Limitations:** Extremely labor-intensive. Requires careful sequencing to avoid destabilizing the existing foundation during excavation. Slow — each section must cure before the adjacent section is excavated. Not practical for homes on very deep problem soils.

### Slab Piers (Interior Piers)

**Cost:** $1,200 to $2,500 per pier

For homes on slab-on-grade foundations, slab piers are installed through the interior by coring holes through the slab, driving piers through the holes, and patching the slab after. This is the primary method for stabilizing settling slabs.

**Best for:** Slab foundations that are sinking or cracking due to soil movement. Particularly common in areas with expansive clay soils (Texas, Colorado, parts of the Southeast).

**Limitations:** Requires temporary relocation of furniture and floor coverings. The patched core holes remain visible in exposed concrete floors. Interior access can be complicated by finished flooring and low clearances.

## The Underpinning Process: What to Expect

A typical residential underpinning project follows this sequence:

**Week 1-2: Engineering and permitting.** The structural engineer's report specifies pier locations, types, and target depths. The foundation repair contractor pulls permits from your local building department per [IRC requirements](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/). Some jurisdictions require the engineer's plans to be submitted with the permit application.

**Week 2-3: Site preparation.** For exterior piers, the contractor excavates along the foundation wall to expose the footing. Each excavation is typically 3-4 feet wide and 6-8 feet deep, depending on the footing depth. Landscaping, walkways, and other hardscape in the work zone need to be removed or protected.

**Week 3-4: Pier installation.** Push piers are driven hydraulically — each pier takes 1-3 hours to install depending on depth. Helical piers are screwed in mechanically — similar timeframe. The contractor monitors depth, torque (for helical piers), or resistance (for push piers) to confirm each pier has reached competent soil.

**Week 4: Load transfer and lift.** Hydraulic jacks at each pier point simultaneously lift the foundation, transferring the building load from the failing soil to the piers. The amount of lift is controlled carefully — typically fractions of an inch at a time — to avoid cracking the structure. Not every foundation can be lifted; sometimes stabilization (stopping further settlement) is the realistic goal.

**Week 4-5: Backfill and restoration.** Excavations are backfilled, brackets are permanently secured, and the site is restored. Interior cracks may need cosmetic repair after the foundation has settled into its new position (give it 30-60 days before patching drywall cracks).

## Soil: The Root Cause

Understanding why your foundation is failing helps you ensure the repair addresses the actual problem:

**Expansive clay soils** swell when wet and shrink when dry, creating a cycle of heaving and settling that damages foundations over decades. Common in much of the central and southern US. Underpinning transfers the load past the "active zone" where moisture changes affect the soil.

**Compacted fill.** Homes built on improperly compacted fill settle as the fill consolidates over time. The settlement can be dramatic — several inches over a few years — and typically affects parts of the foundation unevenly.

**Organic soil decomposition.** Foundations built over buried organic material (tree stumps, construction debris, topsoil that was not removed) settle as that material decomposes. This is a construction defect, but it often does not manifest for 5-15 years after building.

**Water-related erosion.** Plumbing leaks, improper drainage, and poor grading wash soil out from under footings. The [EPA's WaterSense program](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/insulation) promotes water efficiency, but from a foundation perspective, the bigger concern is where your water goes — downspouts, grading, and subsurface drainage all affect foundation soil stability.

**Drought shrinkage.** Extended dry periods cause clay soils to shrink away from foundations, removing support. This is why foundations in drought-prone areas often fail during or just after prolonged dry spells.

## Costs That Pile Up Beyond the Piers

The per-pier cost is only part of the total project expense. Budget for these additional items:

**Structural engineer fees:** $300 to $700 for the initial evaluation, plus $200 to $500 for review of the contractor's installation plan and a final inspection after completion.

**Permits:** $200 to $800 depending on jurisdiction.

**Excavation and backfill:** Usually included in the pier contractor's quote, but verify. Excavation in tight spaces or through hardscaped areas costs more.

**Landscaping restoration:** $500 to $3,000 to repair lawns, replant shrubs, and restore hardscape removed during excavation.

**Interior repairs:** $1,000 to $5,000 for drywall crack repair, repainting, and door/window adjustment after the foundation is stabilized or lifted.

**Waterproofing:** $2,000 to $8,000 if the foundation work reveals or creates water infiltration paths. Underpinning and waterproofing are separate scopes, but they often need to happen together.

**Drainage improvements:** $1,500 to $5,000 for French drains, grading corrections, or downspout extensions to address the water management issues that may have contributed to the foundation failure.

## DIY Underpinning: Can You?

No. Foundation underpinning is not a DIY project under any circumstances. The reasons are practical, legal, and safety-related:

**Structural risk.** Incorrectly underpinning a foundation can cause additional settlement, wall collapse, or structural failure. The consequences of a mistake are catastrophic and irreversible.

**Permits require licensed contractors.** Most jurisdictions require foundation repair to be performed by licensed contractors working from engineered plans. DIY permits for structural work are rare to nonexistent.

**Equipment requirements.** Push pier and helical pier installation requires hydraulic equipment, torque monitoring systems, and lifting jacks that are not available for homeowner rental.

**Liability.** If you sell the home, foundation repairs performed without permits, engineering, and licensed contractors are a disclosure nightmare. A buyer's inspector will identify non-permitted structural work, and their lender may refuse to finance the purchase.

What you can do yourself is the detective work: monitoring cracks, photographing changes, checking drainage and grading, and cleaning gutters and maintaining downspout extensions. These [water management tasks](/blog/blown-in-insulation-per-square-foot-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/) — while not structural repairs themselves — directly protect your foundation from the water-related causes of failure.

## Finding the Right Foundation Contractor

Foundation repair is a specialized trade. Not every general contractor or concrete company has the equipment, training, and engineering relationships to do this work properly.

**Look for:**
- Companies that specialize in foundation repair (not general contractors who "also do foundations")
- Membership in the Foundation Repair Association (FRA) or similar professional organizations
- Willingness to work from a structural engineer's plan (not just their own in-house assessment)
- Manufacturers' certification for the pier systems they install
- Written transferable warranty (20-25 year warranties are standard for pier systems)
- References from homeowners and from structural engineers who have inspected their work

**Avoid:**
- Companies that diagnose and sell in the same visit ("We found the problem, sign here today")
- Contractors who refuse to work from an independent engineer's evaluation
- Extremely low bids that omit engineering, permitting, or post-installation monitoring
- Anyone who promises to "fix" your foundation without first understanding why it is failing

[FEMA](https://www.fema.gov/) recommends that homeowners in disaster-affected areas work only with licensed, insured contractors for structural repairs. That guidance applies equally to foundation work unrelated to disasters — the structural stakes are simply too high for unlicensed or uninsured work.

Foundation underpinning is expensive, disruptive, and stressful. It is also one of the few home repairs where doing it right the first time is the only financially rational choice. The money you spend on a structural engineer's evaluation, a properly permitted and engineered repair, and a qualified contractor is money that protects the entire value of your home for decades. Cut corners here, and you pay for it — eventually, and usually at a much higher price.
