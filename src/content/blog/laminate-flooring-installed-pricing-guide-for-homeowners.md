Laminate flooring costs between $3 and $8 per square foot fully installed, with the national average landing around $5 per square foot for a mid-range product with professional installation. That puts a standard 300-square-foot living room at $900 to $2,400 all-in. Laminate remains one of the most cost-effective ways to get the look of hardwood without the hardwood price tag.

The installed price depends on three things: the laminate quality tier you choose, your subfloor condition, and whether you hire a pro or handle the click-lock installation yourself.

## Laminate Material Costs by Quality Tier

Not all laminate is created equal, and the price differences reflect meaningful quality gaps.

### Budget Tier: $0.70–$1.50/sq ft
Basic 6mm to 7mm laminate with a thin wear layer and no attached underlayment. These products look decent from a distance but feel hollow underfoot, show wear in high-traffic areas within 3 to 5 years, and have minimal water resistance. Fine for rental properties or rooms you plan to re-floor within 5 years.

### Mid-Range: $1.50–$3.50/sq ft
This is where most homeowners should shop. Products in this range typically offer:
- 8mm to 10mm thickness for a more solid feel
- Embossed-in-register (EIR) textures that align the surface texture with the printed grain
- Attached underlayment on better models
- AC3 or AC4 wear ratings for residential durability
- Some degree of water resistance (sealed edges, water-resistant core)

Brands at this tier include Pergo, Shaw, and select Mohawk lines. The quality difference between a $1.50 and $3.00 product is noticeable both in appearance and longevity.

### Premium: $3.50–$6.00/sq ft
High-end laminate approaches engineered hardwood in both looks and performance. Features include:
- 12mm thickness with dense core
- AC5 commercial-grade wear rating
- Enhanced water resistance or waterproof core technology
- Longer, wider planks (up to 7 inches wide, 54+ inches long)
- More realistic wood grain patterns and textures

At the top of this range, you start competing with budget engineered hardwood. Consider whether real wood makes more sense at this price point.

## Installation Labor Costs

Professional installation adds $1.50 to $3.00 per square foot for straightforward rooms. This covers:

- Subfloor inspection and minor leveling
- Underlayment installation (if not pre-attached)
- Plank laying, cutting, and clicking
- Transition strips at doorways
- Baseboard reinstallation or installation of quarter-round trim

**Factors that increase labor costs:**
- Stairs: Add $30 to $60 per step (laminate on stairs is tricky and time-consuming)
- Complex room shapes with many corners and angles
- Pattern layouts (herringbone or diagonal) rather than standard straight-lay
- Old flooring removal: $0.50 to $2.00/sq ft depending on what's coming up
- Significant subfloor leveling work

## Why DIY Installation Saves the Most on Laminate

Laminate is the flooring where DIY savings are most impactful relative to difficulty. The click-lock system was literally engineered for homeowners, and the tools required are minimal.

**What you need:**
- Miter saw or circular saw ($50–$150, or $30–$50/day rental)
- Tapping block ($5–$10)
- Pull bar ($8–$15)
- Spacers ($5–$10 for a bag)
- Utility knife for underlayment
- Tape measure, pencil, speed square

**Realistic DIY timeline per room:**
- First room (learning curve): 6–10 hours for 150–200 sq ft
- Subsequent rooms: 4–6 hours each
- A full house: 2–3 weekends for a focused DIYer

The labor savings on a 300-square-foot room are $450 to $750 — substantial enough to upgrade your material choice by one full tier. That's the strategic play: DIY the installation and use the savings to buy better laminate.

## Subfloor Requirements for Laminate

Laminate flooring needs a flat, dry, clean subfloor. Here's what to check and fix before any planks get laid.

### Over Plywood Subfloors
- Check flatness with a 6-foot straightedge. Dips or humps greater than 3/16 inch need fixing.
- Drive screws through plywood into joists at any squeaky spots.
- Fill low spots with floor-leveling compound ($25–$40 per bag).
- Sand or plane high spots.
- Ensure plywood is at least 3/4-inch thick for residential laminate installations per [building code standards](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/).

### Over Concrete
- Test for moisture: tape a 2x2-foot piece of plastic to the slab, seal the edges, wait 48 hours. Condensation underneath means moisture treatment is needed.
- Level low spots with self-leveling compound.
- Grind down high spots with a concrete grinder.
- Use a 6-mil poly vapor barrier or an underlayment with built-in moisture protection.

### Over Existing Flooring
Laminate can float over existing vinyl, tile, or hardwood as long as:
- The existing floor is firmly attached (no loose tiles, no peeling vinyl)
- The surface is flat within specification
- Adding the new laminate thickness (typically 3/8 to 1/2 inch with underlayment) doesn't create clearance problems at doors

Going over existing flooring saves $0.50 to $2.00/sq ft in demolition costs. That's $150 to $600 on a 300-square-foot room — a real savings.

## Laminate vs. LVP: Which to Install?

This is the comparison most homeowners wrestle with, since both are click-lock floating floors at similar price points.

**Choose laminate when:**
- You want the most realistic wood look at a given price (laminate's photographic layer produces sharper images)
- The room is dry and climate-controlled (bedrooms, living rooms, hallways)
- Scratch resistance matters (laminate's hard melamine surface resists scratches better than vinyl)
- You prefer a harder, more rigid feel underfoot

**Choose LVP when:**
- Water exposure is likely (bathrooms, kitchens, basements, laundry rooms)
- Noise reduction matters (LVP is quieter underfoot)
- You want a warmer, softer feel
- Subfloor imperfections are minor (LVP's flexibility masks small irregularities better)

## Common Laminate Installation Mistakes

After doing several rooms, these are the errors that cause the most problems:

**Not staggering end joints properly.** Maintain at least 8 to 12 inches between end joints in adjacent rows. Short offsets create a repetitive "H-pattern" that looks unprofessional and creates structural weak points.

**Using a knee kicker instead of proper clicking.** Laminate planks engage differently than carpet. They need to angle in at the long edge, then click down at the end. Forcing them horizontally damages the click profiles.

**Installing before acclimation.** Let laminate sit in the room for 48 hours before installation, especially if it was stored in a cold warehouse or garage. The material needs to reach room temperature and humidity equilibrium.

**Forgetting to undercut door casings.** Use an oscillating tool to cut the bottom of door trim so laminate slides underneath. This creates a clean, professional appearance at every doorway.

**Using too much force on stubborn planks.** If a plank won't click in easily, the issue is usually debris in the joint, a slightly warped plank, or an unlevel subfloor section. Hammering harder damages the click system. Find and fix the actual problem instead.

## Getting the Best Deal

**Shop remnants.** Flooring stores sell leftover inventory from large orders at 30–50% off. If you need 300 square feet, a 350-square-foot remnant lot gives you materials plus spares at a fraction of retail.

**Buy during sales cycles.** The [National Wood Flooring Association](https://www.nwfa.org/) notes that January/February and holiday weekends offer the most aggressive flooring promotions.

**Compare total installed price, not just material price.** Store A's laminate at $2.50/sq ft with $2.50 installation may actually cost more than Store B's $3.00/sq ft laminate with free installation promotion.

**Don't forget the extras in your budget.** Underlayment, transitions, baseboards, quarter-round trim, and tool rental add $0.50 to $1.50/sq ft beyond the advertised laminate and labor prices.
