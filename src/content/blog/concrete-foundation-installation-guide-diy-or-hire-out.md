Concrete foundation installation for residential projects ranges from $5,000 for a simple shed slab to $30,000 or more for a full house foundation. Whether you are pouring a monolithic slab for a garage, building stem walls for an addition, or installing a frost-protected shallow foundation, the process demands careful planning, proper soil preparation, and respect for local building codes.

This is not a project where you wing it. Foundations are permanent, they bear the entire weight of whatever sits on top, and mistakes are extraordinarily expensive to fix after the concrete cures.

## Foundation Types and Where They Make Sense

### Monolithic Slab

A single pour that combines the footing and slab into one unit. The edges are thicker (typically 12 to 18 inches) to serve as the footing, while the interior is a standard 4-inch slab.

**Best for:** Garages, workshops, simple additions in areas without frost concerns or where the frost line is shallow.

**Cost:** $5 to $9 per square foot for a basic 4-inch slab with thickened edges. A 24x24-foot garage slab runs $2,900 to $5,200.

### Stem Wall Foundation

Separate poured footings with concrete or block walls rising to above grade, then a slab poured inside. This is the standard for most residential construction.

**Best for:** Home additions, areas with frost requirements, sloped lots, and any structure requiring a crawl space below.

**Cost:** $12 to $25 per square foot including footings, walls, and slab. A 500-square-foot addition foundation runs $6,000 to $12,500.

### Pier and Grade Beam

Drilled piers (sometimes called caissons) extend down to stable soil or bedrock, connected by grade beams at the surface. This is the go-to for challenging soil conditions.

**Best for:** Expansive clay soils, steep slopes, areas with a high water table, or where you need to reach stable bearing soil well below the surface.

**Cost:** $15 to $40 per square foot. Pier drilling alone runs $200 to $600 per pier depending on depth and diameter.

## The Real DIY Question

Let me be direct: pouring a full house foundation is not a DIY project. The engineering requirements, code compliance, inspection sequences, and sheer physical scale put it firmly in professional territory.

That said, smaller foundation projects can be within reach for experienced DIYers:

**Realistic DIY candidates:**
- Shed slabs (under 200 square feet)
- Small patio pads
- Concrete pier footings for deck posts
- Simple monolithic slabs for workshops (with an engineer's design)

**Hire a professional for:**
- Any foundation supporting a living structure
- Stem wall foundations
- Anything requiring engineered soil reports
- Projects on slopes or in flood zones
- Foundations deeper than 18 inches

The cost savings from DIY on a small slab project are meaningful — roughly 40% to 50% — because labor is the majority of the cost. On a $5,000 garage slab, you could save $2,000 to $2,500. But if you mess up the grade, the reinforcement, or the mix consistency, tearing it out and starting over costs more than hiring a pro from day one.

## Soil Preparation: Where Most Failures Start

Concrete does not fail because the concrete is bad. It fails because what is underneath it moves, settles, or washes away.

**Step 1: Soil testing.** For any structural foundation, get a geotechnical report. This tells you the soil bearing capacity (how much weight per square foot it can support), the soil type, the water table level, and whether expansive clays are present. Cost: $1,500 to $3,000.

**Step 2: Excavation and grading.** Remove topsoil (it is organic and will decompose, causing settlement). Excavate to the required depth — the bottom of the footing must sit below the frost line in your area. The [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) specifies minimum footing depths, but local codes may be stricter.

**Step 3: Compaction.** Backfill with granular material (crushed stone or gravel) in 6- to 8-inch lifts, compacting each lift with a plate compactor or jumping jack. This is the step most DIYers rush, and it is the step that causes the most cracking and settlement later.

**Step 4: Sub-base.** A 4- to 6-inch layer of compacted gravel provides drainage under the slab and a stable, level surface to pour on.

## Formwork and Reinforcement

Forms define the shape of your pour and hold the concrete in place while it cures. For slabs, 2x4 or 2x6 lumber staked every 2 to 3 feet works. For walls, you need engineered form systems or insulating concrete forms (ICFs).

Reinforcement options:
- **Rebar:** #4 (1/2-inch) rebar in a grid pattern, typically 12 or 16 inches on center, is standard for residential footings and slabs. Rebar must sit in the lower third of the slab, supported on chairs or dobies — not laid on the ground and "pulled up" during the pour.
- **Welded wire mesh:** 6x6-W1.4xW1.4 mesh works for lightly loaded slabs but is not a substitute for rebar in structural applications.
- **Fiber reinforcement:** Synthetic or steel fibers mixed into the concrete reduce surface cracking but do not replace structural reinforcement.

For any load-bearing foundation, an engineer should specify the reinforcement schedule. This is not a place for guesswork.

## Concrete Ordering and Pouring

Residential foundation concrete is typically 3,000 to 4,000 PSI with a 4- to 5-inch slump (how fluid the mix is). For footings, 3,000 PSI is standard. For exposed slabs or garages, 4,000 PSI provides better durability.

**Quantity estimation:** Length x width x depth (in feet), divided by 27, gives you cubic yards. Always order 5% to 10% extra — you will need it for uneven subgrade, slight overexcavation, and waste.

A standard concrete truck delivers 8 to 10 cubic yards. Delivery charges typically include the first load and add $50 to $100 per additional truck. Short-load fees apply for orders under 3 or 4 yards.

**Timing matters.** Once the truck arrives, you have roughly 90 minutes before the concrete becomes unworkable. Have your crew, tools, and plan ready before calling for delivery. For a garage slab, plan on 4 to 6 people for the pour.

## Curing: The Patience Phase

Concrete reaches about 70% of its design strength in 7 days and full strength at 28 days. During this time:

- Keep the surface moist for at least 7 days. Use curing compound, wet burlap, or plastic sheeting.
- Do not allow the surface to freeze for the first 48 hours minimum. In cold weather, use insulating blankets.
- Do not load the foundation with structure weight for at least 7 days, and ideally 14.
- Do not drive on a garage slab for at least 28 days.

Rushing the cure is one of the most common mistakes. Concrete that dries too fast develops surface cracks and reduced strength. If you are pouring in summer heat, consider scheduling the pour for early morning.

## Waterproofing Below Grade

Any foundation wall or slab that contacts soil needs waterproofing. For basements and crawl spaces, this means:

- **Dampproofing** (asphalt emulsion coating on the exterior of foundation walls) — minimum code requirement but not true waterproofing
- **Waterproof membrane** (sheet membrane or liquid-applied) — significantly better protection, adds $2 to $4 per square foot
- **Drainage board** over the membrane to channel water down to the footing drain
- **Perimeter drain** (perforated pipe in gravel) at the footing level, draining to daylight or a sump pit

The [EPA recommends addressing moisture](https://www.epa.gov/mold) proactively in below-grade construction to prevent mold issues. This is far cheaper to do during construction than to retrofit later.

## Radon Mitigation

If you are building in an [EPA-designated radon zone](https://www.epa.gov/radon), install a passive radon mitigation system during construction. This means a gravel layer under the slab, a vapor barrier, and a 4-inch PVC pipe running from beneath the slab to above the roofline. Cost during new construction: $500 to $1,000. Retrofitting later: $800 to $2,500.

## When to Call a Structural Engineer

Always, for anything load-bearing. But specifically:

- Expansive or problematic soils
- Sloped sites (more than 10% grade)
- Additions tying into existing foundations
- Any two-story structure
- Flood zones or high water table areas

An engineer's fee of $1,500 to $4,000 is trivial compared to the cost of a foundation failure. It also satisfies the building department's requirements for a stamped plan, which you will need for your permit.

The foundation is the one part of your home you cannot easily change later. Invest in getting it right the first time — whether that means hiring the right contractor, getting the proper engineering, or simply being honest about whether your skills match the project's demands.
