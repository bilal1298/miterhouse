Installing insulation costs between $0.75 and $7.00 per square foot depending on the insulation type, where in your home it goes, and whether you hire a professional or do the work yourself. For the most common project — adding blown-in insulation to an attic — expect to pay $1,000 to $3,000 for a 1,000-square-foot space. Wall insulation during a renovation runs $1.50 to $4.00 per square foot, and basement or crawl space insulation is typically $1.50 to $5.00 per square foot.

Here are the real numbers by location, material, and installation method so you can budget accurately.

## Cost by Location in Your Home

Where you are insulating matters as much as what material you choose, because accessibility and installation complexity vary dramatically.

### Attic Floor (Most Common)
This is the most cost-effective insulation project for most homes. You are insulating the attic floor (between or over the ceiling joists) to keep conditioned air in the living space below.

| Method | DIY Cost/sq ft | Installed Cost/sq ft |
|---|---|---|
| Blown cellulose | $0.50–$1.00 | $1.00–$2.50 |
| Blown fiberglass | $0.50–$1.25 | $1.25–$3.00 |
| Fiberglass batts | $0.30–$0.80 | $0.75–$2.00 |

DIY blown insulation is realistic here because you can rent a blowing machine from the store where you buy the material, usually at no charge with a minimum purchase. The work is straightforward — point the hose between the joists and build up to the target depth.

### Exterior Walls
Wall insulation is almost always a professional job because it requires either opening the wall from inside (during a renovation), drilling access holes from outside and dense-packing insulation, or removing siding to access wall cavities.

| Method | Installed Cost/sq ft |
|---|---|
| Dense-pack cellulose (injection) | $1.50–$3.50 |
| Spray foam (open-cell) | $2.50–$4.50 |
| Spray foam (closed-cell) | $4.00–$7.00 |
| Fiberglass batts (open wall) | $1.00–$2.00 |

Injection (dense-pack) insulation is the standard approach for existing walls that are not being opened up. Holes are drilled in each stud bay, insulation is blown in under pressure, and the holes are patched. For a whole-house wall insulation project, expect $3,000 to $10,000 depending on wall area and accessibility.

### Basement Walls
Basement insulation serves double duty: thermal insulation and moisture management. The approach depends on whether the walls are finished or unfinished.

| Method | Installed Cost/sq ft |
|---|---|
| Rigid foam board (XPS or EPS) | $1.50–$3.50 |
| Spray foam (closed-cell) | $4.00–$7.00 |
| Fiberglass batts in stud wall | $1.00–$2.50 |

For unfinished basement walls, rigid foam board (extruded or expanded polystyrene) adhered directly to the concrete is the standard approach. It provides insulation and a vapor barrier in one. Never install fiberglass batts directly against concrete basement walls — they absorb moisture and become a mold factory.

### Crawl Space
Crawl space insulation options depend on whether you have a vented or unvented (encapsulated) crawl space.

| Method | Installed Cost/sq ft |
|---|---|
| Floor joists with batts | $1.00–$2.50 |
| Crawl space wall spray foam | $3.00–$5.00 |
| Encapsulation + wall insulation | $5.00–$10.00 |

The modern best practice for crawl spaces in most climates is encapsulation: sealing the crawl space with a vapor barrier on the floor and walls, insulating the walls rather than the floor above, and conditioning the space. This is more expensive upfront but eliminates the moisture problems that plague traditional vented crawl spaces.

## Factors That Affect Your Total Cost

### Amount of Existing Insulation
Adding insulation over existing material is cheaper than starting from scratch. If your attic has 6 inches of old fiberglass (roughly R-19), adding blown cellulose to reach R-49 requires about 8 to 10 additional inches. If the existing insulation is damaged or contaminated, removal adds $1 to $2 per square foot.

### Air Sealing Scope
Air sealing before insulating is critical for maximum energy savings, but it adds cost. A thorough air sealing of a typical attic — around penetrations, ductwork, the attic hatch, and top plates of walls — costs $300 to $1,000 in labor and materials when done by a professional. Some insulation contractors include basic air sealing; others charge separately.

### Accessibility and Working Conditions
Low-clearance attics, tight crawl spaces, and walls with irregular framing all increase labor time. Attic work in summer heat (attics routinely exceed 130 degrees) limits work to short shifts, extending the project timeline.

### Regional Pricing
Insulation installation costs vary by roughly 30% across the country. Major metro areas in the Northeast and West Coast are at the high end. Southeast and Midwest markets tend to be at the lower end.

### Permits
Most insulation-only projects do not require permits, but if your project involves modifying the structure (adding framing for stud walls in a basement, for example) or changing the HVAC system, permits may be required. Check with your local building department.

## DIY vs. Professional: Honest Assessment

**Realistic DIY projects:**
- Blown-in attic insulation (with rental machine)
- Fiberglass batt installation in open attic floor joists
- Rigid foam board on unfinished basement walls
- Fiberglass batts in open stud walls (during a renovation)

**Better left to professionals:**
- Dense-pack wall injection (requires specialized equipment and experience)
- Spray foam (requires specialized equipment, training, and insurance)
- Crawl space encapsulation (moisture management expertise needed)
- Any project involving old insulation removal (potential asbestos, vermiculite with asbestos)

The cost savings from DIY attic insulation are meaningful. A 1,000-square-foot attic insulated with blown cellulose might cost $500 to $800 in materials (DIY) versus $1,500 to $2,500 installed professionally. The labor savings justify a weekend of sweaty, itchy work for many homeowners.

## Energy Savings: What to Actually Expect

Insulation companies love to throw around dramatic savings percentages. Here is a more realistic picture.

Going from no insulation to recommended levels saves 20% to 40% on heating and cooling costs. Going from some insulation to recommended levels saves 10% to 20%. Adding insulation beyond recommended levels provides diminishing returns — the first few inches do the most work.

For a home spending $2,400 per year on heating and cooling (the national average), adding attic insulation from R-19 to R-49 might save $240 to $480 per year. At an installed cost of $1,500 to $2,500, the payback period is 3 to 7 years.

The [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) establishes minimum insulation requirements by climate zone for new construction and major renovations. The [Department of Energy's insulation resources](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/insulation) provide detailed guidance on recommended R-values and insulation best practices.

## Tax Credits and Rebates

Federal energy efficiency tax credits can offset insulation costs by up to 30% of the project cost (with annual caps). Many utility companies also offer rebates for insulation upgrades. Check your utility's website and the federal Energy Star database for current incentives before starting — these programs can reduce your effective cost significantly.

## Getting Quotes

When requesting insulation quotes, specify:
- The areas to be insulated (attic floor, walls, basement)
- Whether air sealing is included
- The target R-value
- Whether old insulation removal is needed
- What brand and type of material will be used

Get at least three quotes. The lowest bid is not always the best — ask what is included and what is not. An installer who includes air sealing in a slightly higher bid is offering more value than one who quotes insulation only at a lower price.
