No, it is not normal for a water heater to leak — but not every drip you see means the tank is failing. Some water around the base of a water heater is harmless condensation, while other leaks indicate serious problems that demand immediate attention. The difference between a $0 non-issue and a $1,500 emergency replacement depends entirely on where the water is coming from and why.

Identifying the leak source takes about 10 minutes and requires nothing more than a flashlight and a dry towel. That quick investigation tells you whether you can relax, make a simple repair, or need to start shopping for a new unit.

## Condensation: The Leak That Is Not a Leak

Before you panic, rule out condensation. Water heaters — especially gas models — can produce visible condensation on the outside of the tank under specific conditions:

- The tank is full of cold water after first installation, a heavy draw, or refilling
- The ambient air is warm and humid (summer months, humid climates)
- The heater is recovering after extended use (several showers back to back)

Condensation appears as a uniform film of moisture on the tank surface, often concentrated near the bottom. It drips down and collects in the drain pan or on the floor, looking exactly like a leak.

**How to tell the difference:** Wipe the tank dry with a towel. If moisture reappears uniformly across the surface within 15 to 30 minutes, it is condensation. If moisture reappears at a specific point — a fitting, valve, or seam — it is a leak.

Condensation is harmless and temporary. It stops once the tank water reaches operating temperature. If it bothers you, increasing the thermostat setting by a few degrees reduces it, though 120 degrees is the recommended maximum for safety and energy efficiency.

## Where Leaks Actually Come From

Real leaks originate from one of six locations. Here they are from most to least common, along with what each one means.

### 1. Inlet and Outlet Connections (Top of Tank)

The cold water inlet and hot water outlet at the top of the heater connect via threaded fittings, flex connectors, or soldered unions. Over time, these connections can loosen due to thermal expansion and contraction cycles.

**What you see:** Water dripping from the top of the tank, running down the side, and pooling at the bottom. This can mimic a tank leak if you do not trace the drip upward.

**The fix:** Tighten the connections with a wrench. If the fittings use Teflon tape, remove, re-tape with 3 to 5 wraps, and reassemble. If the flex connector is corroded, replace it ($8 to $20 each). This is a 15-minute DIY repair.

**Cost:** $0 to $20.

### 2. Temperature-Pressure Relief (TPR) Valve

The TPR valve is a safety device on the side or top of the tank that opens automatically if temperature or pressure exceeds safe limits. It has a discharge pipe that routes water to the floor or exterior.

**Normal TPR discharge** happens rarely — only when actual over-temperature or over-pressure conditions occur. If your TPR is dripping constantly, something is wrong:

- The water temperature is too high (thermostat set above 150 degrees or thermostat malfunction)
- The incoming water pressure exceeds 80 psi (check with a $10 pressure gauge on a hose bib)
- The thermal expansion tank has failed or is missing (common in closed plumbing systems)
- The TPR valve itself is faulty (less common but possible)

**The fix:** Address the underlying cause first. If the valve is simply old and weeping, replacement costs $15 to $30 for the valve and 20 minutes of work. Make sure the [discharge pipe is properly routed](/blog/installing-tank-water-heater-what-to-know-first/) — it should terminate within 6 inches of the floor or to the exterior.

### 3. Drain Valve

The drain valve at the bottom of the tank is used for [flushing sediment](/blog/how-often-should-i-flush-my-water-heater-honest-guide/). These valves, especially the cheap plastic ones that come standard on most units, are prone to weeping.

**What you see:** A slow drip from the valve itself or from where the hose connection sits.

**The fix:** Try opening the valve fully, then closing it again — sediment caught in the seat can prevent a full seal. If that does not work, screw a brass hose cap onto the valve ($3). If the valve continues to leak through the cap, replace the valve with a brass ball valve ($10 to $20).

### 4. Anode Rod Port

The anode rod screws into the top of the tank. If the threads have corroded or the anode rod was not properly tightened during installation, water can seep around the port.

**The fix:** Tighten the anode rod with a 1-1/16 inch socket. If the threads are too corroded to seal, apply Teflon tape or pipe thread sealant and retighten. While you have access, check the [condition of the anode rod itself](/blog/how-often-should-i-flush-my-water-heater-honest-guide/) — if it is depleted, replace it.

### 5. Internal Tank (Bottom or Sides)

This is the one you do not want. When the glass lining inside the tank cracks and the steel shell corrodes through, water leaks from the tank body itself — usually near the bottom.

**What you see:** Water pooling under the tank with no identifiable source from fittings, valves, or the TPR. The water appears at the base or along a seam.

**The fix:** There is no fix. A leaking tank cannot be repaired. The lining has failed, the steel is corroding, and the problem will only get worse. Shut off the fuel source and cold water supply, drain the tank, and [replace the unit](/blog/installing-tank-water-heater-what-to-know-first/).

Average tank life is 8 to 12 years. If your tank is in that range and leaking from the body, replacement is the only option.

### 6. Internal Condensation Dripping onto the Burner Tray (Gas Only)

On gas water heaters, internal condensation can drip onto the burner assembly and sizzle, creating the sound and smell of a leak. Water may pool on the burner tray and drip out of the access panel.

**Is this serious?** Usually not, if it happens during heavy recovery (heating a full tank of cold water). If it happens constantly, excessive condensation may indicate a vent draft problem or oversized flue, which reduces efficiency and can create carbon monoxide risks.

## The Inspection Checklist

When you find water near your heater, work through this systematic check:

1. Dry the entire surface of the tank and all connections with towels
2. Place dry paper towels at each potential leak point: inlet/outlet fittings, TPR valve, drain valve, anode rod port, and the tank bottom
3. Wait 15 to 30 minutes and check which paper towels are wet
4. Trace any moisture upward to its highest point — water runs downhill, so the source is always above the drip

This process eliminates guesswork and prevents you from replacing a perfectly good water heater when the real problem was a $3 fitting.

## When to Act vs. When to Watch

**Act immediately:**
- Water is leaking from the tank body — this will get worse, and a sudden failure can dump 40 to 50 gallons onto your floor
- The TPR valve is discharging continuously — this indicates a dangerous temperature or pressure condition
- Water is near any electrical connections or the electrical panel

**Fix soon (within days):**
- Fitting connections are dripping — tighten or re-tape when convenient
- The drain valve is weeping — cap it to stop the drip

**Monitor:**
- Occasional condensation during heavy use — normal behavior, no action needed
- A single small drip from the TPR that stops on its own — may recur; track it

According to [EPA WaterSense guidelines](https://www.epa.gov/watersense), a small hot water leak of just 10 drips per minute wastes roughly 500 gallons per year. Beyond the wasted water and energy, persistent moisture around a water heater promotes mold growth and can damage flooring, especially in finished basements.

## Prevention Beats Repair

Annual maintenance dramatically reduces leak risk:

- Flush sediment to prevent [internal corrosion](/blog/how-often-should-i-flush-my-water-heater-honest-guide/)
- Check the anode rod every 3 to 5 years and replace when depleted
- Test the TPR valve annually by lifting the lever briefly — water should flow freely, then stop when you release it
- Inspect all fittings and connections for signs of corrosion or mineral buildup
- Ensure the drain pan is present and the drain line is clear

A well-maintained water heater rarely surprises you. The ones that flood basements are almost always the ones that sat in a corner untouched for a decade.
