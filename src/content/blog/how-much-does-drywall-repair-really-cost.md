Drywall repair costs $50 to $400 for small patches when hiring a handyman, or $200 to $800+ per room for extensive damage repaired by a drywall contractor. If you handle it yourself, materials for most repairs cost $10 to $75. The gap between DIY and professional is dramatic because drywall repair is highly labor-intensive relative to material costs — the joint compound and drywall sheets are cheap, but the skill and time to finish them smoothly is where the real cost lives.

## Cost by Repair Type

Different types of damage have very different repair costs. Here is what you can expect:

| Repair Type | DIY Cost | Pro Cost | Time (DIY) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nail pop (per pop) | $2–$5 | $50–$75 | 15–30 min |
| Small hole (<3 inches) | $5–$15 | $50–$150 | 30–60 min |
| Medium hole (3–6 inches) | $10–$25 | $75–$200 | 1–2 hours |
| Large hole (6+ inches) | $15–$40 | $125–$350 | 2–4 hours |
| Drywall seam crack | $10–$20 | $75–$200 | 1–3 hours |
| Water damage section | $20–$75 | $150–$500 | 3–8 hours |
| Full sheet replacement | $25–$50 | $200–$600 | 4–8 hours |
| Room retaping and skimming | $50–$150 | $500–$1,500 | 8–20 hours |

These costs assume the repair includes finishing to a paintable surface. Painting is additional. For painting costs, see our [professional painting guide](/blog/professional-painting-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/) and [interior painting pricing guide](/blog/painting-interior-pricing-guide-for-homeowners/).

Note that the DIY times listed include drying time between coats of joint compound. You cannot rush the drying — each coat needs to dry fully (4 to 24 hours depending on thickness and humidity) before sanding and applying the next coat.

## Small Hole Repairs: The Most Common Job

Nail holes, picture hanger holes, and minor dings are the most frequent drywall repairs. They are also the easiest.

**For holes under 1/2 inch:** Fill directly with lightweight spackle using your finger or a putty knife. Let dry (15 to 20 minutes for lightweight spackle). Sand smooth with 150-grit sandpaper. Prime the spot with a dab of primer and paint. Total material cost: under $5.

**For holes 1/2 inch to 3 inches:** Use a self-adhesive mesh patch ($3 to $5 for a pack) over the hole, then cover with two to three coats of joint compound, sanding between coats. The patch provides backing for the compound. This is a reliable repair that takes 24 to 48 hours total (mostly drying time between coats).

**For holes 3 to 6 inches:** Use a California patch (also called a butterfly patch). Cut a piece of drywall slightly larger than the hole. Score the back paper and snap off the gypsum, leaving the front paper as a flange. Apply joint compound around the hole, press the patch in with the paper flange overlapping the surrounding wall, and cover with more compound. Two to three coats with sanding produces an invisible repair.

## Large Hole and Section Repairs

Holes larger than 6 inches, doorknob damage, and sections damaged by water or impact require cutting out the damaged area and installing a new piece of drywall.

**The process:**

1. **Cut a clean rectangular opening** around the damage using a drywall saw or utility knife. Cut straight lines — you will be fitting a patch to this opening.

2. **Install backing.** For holes smaller than the stud spacing (typically 16 inches), attach furring strips or wooden backer boards behind the opening with screws. These give you something to screw the patch into. For holes spanning one or more studs, the studs themselves provide attachment.

3. **Cut and install the patch.** Measure the opening and cut a drywall patch to fit. Leave about 1/8 inch of gap on each side for the joint compound. Screw the patch to the backing or studs with drywall screws, slightly recessing each screw head.

4. **Tape the joints.** Apply paper tape or mesh tape over all seams. Paper tape with joint compound produces a stronger joint. Mesh tape with setting-type compound is faster but slightly more prone to cracking.

5. **Mud and sand.** Apply three coats of joint compound:
   - First coat: Fill the seams and embed the tape. Use a 6-inch knife.
   - Second coat: Feather wider (8 to 10-inch knife) to blend the repair into the surrounding wall.
   - Third coat: Final feathering with a 10 to 12-inch knife for a seamless blend.
   - Sand between each coat with 120 to 150-grit sandpaper or a sanding sponge.

6. **Prime and paint.** The repaired area must be primed before painting — raw joint compound absorbs paint differently than the surrounding wall, creating a visible difference in sheen. Priming equalizes the absorption.

Material cost for a large hole repair: $15 to $40. The expense is your time — expect 4 to 8 hours spread over 2 to 3 days for the compound to dry between coats.

## Drywall Seam and Crack Repair

Cracks along drywall seams — where two sheets meet — are caused by settling, truss uplift, or original taping that has failed. The repair approach depends on the cause:

**Hairline cracks:** These are usually cosmetic. Apply a thin coat of joint compound over the crack, feathering 4 to 6 inches on each side. Sand smooth and paint. This is a 30-minute repair plus drying time.

**Recurring cracks:** If a crack returns after repair, the underlying cause (structural movement, truss uplift) is still active. For these, remove the old tape over the crack, apply mesh tape (which is more flexible than paper tape), and cover with flexible joint compound or elastomeric compound. This accommodates ongoing movement rather than fighting it.

**Wide cracks or tape failure:** Cut out the loose tape and compound 2 to 3 inches beyond where it is firmly adhered. Apply new tape and three coats of compound as you would for a new joint. Feather widely — 12 inches or more on each side — for an invisible repair.

## What Affects Professional Pricing

When hiring a drywall professional, several factors affect your cost:

**Minimum charge.** Most drywall contractors and handymen have a minimum service charge of $100 to $250, regardless of how small the repair is. One nail pop costs the same to hire someone for as five nail pops in the same room. Batch your repairs to get value from the minimum charge.

**Access difficulty.** Repairs on high walls, stairwells, or above cabinets require ladders or scaffolding and take more time. Expect a 25 to 50 percent premium for difficult-access locations.

**Texture matching.** If your walls have a texture (orange peel, knockdown, skip trowel, popcorn), the repair must match. Texture application is a distinct skill — a smooth finish is easier for most repair techs. Textured ceiling repairs are particularly challenging to match. Texture matching can add $50 to $200 per repair area.

**Number of coats and quality level.** A Level 4 finish (standard for painted walls) requires three coats. A Level 5 finish (skim coat over the entire area, used under gloss paints or harsh lighting) adds another coat and more sanding. Level 5 is rarely necessary for a repair but may be required in certain situations.

**Painting.** Some drywall contractors also paint; others leave the painting to you or a separate painter. If painting is included, it adds $100 to $300 per room depending on scope.

## DIY Mistakes That Add Cost

**Using the wrong compound.** All-purpose pre-mixed joint compound works for most repairs. But for embedding tape on large repairs, setting-type compound (the powder you mix with water) is stronger and shrinks less. Using lightweight spackle on anything larger than a nail hole leads to cracking and a redo.

**Insufficient feathering.** The most visible DIY repair mistake is a compound buildup that stands proud of the surrounding wall. Every coat should extend wider than the previous one, gradually blending the repair into the flat wall. The final coat should feather to nothing over 8 to 12 inches on each side.

**Sanding too aggressively.** Aggressive sanding gouges the compound and exposes the tape. Use light pressure with 150-grit sandpaper. A sanding sponge is more forgiving than sandpaper on a block. Check your work with a raking light (a bright light held at an acute angle to the wall) — it reveals imperfections invisible under normal lighting.

**Skipping primer on the repair.** Raw joint compound absorbs paint differently than existing painted drywall. Without primer, the repair area will show as a dull spot (called "flashing") after painting. A coat of PVA drywall primer or stain-blocking primer over the repair eliminates this.

For safety guidance during drywall work, [OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/) recommends dust control measures when sanding joint compound. Wear an N95 respirator, use a dust screen on your sanding pole if doing large areas, and consider wet sanding for small repairs (a damp sponge instead of sandpaper — no dust, slightly less smooth finish). The [EPA's renovation guidelines](https://www.epa.gov/lead/renovation-repair-and-painting-program) apply if your home has lead paint that may be disturbed during the repair. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/) has general safety guidelines for home improvement projects.

## The Bottom Line

Drywall repair is one of the best DIY value propositions in home improvement. Materials are cheap, the techniques are learnable, and the savings over professional rates are 60 to 80 percent. The trade-off is time and patience — compound needs to dry, sanding generates dust, and achieving an invisible repair takes three coats where you might want to stop at two.

For a handful of small repairs, DIY is the obvious choice. For a room with extensive damage or a whole-house renovation, hiring a pro makes sense given the scale. For anything in between, your decision comes down to how much you value your time versus your money — and whether you are willing to invest in learning a skill that will serve you for decades of homeownership.
