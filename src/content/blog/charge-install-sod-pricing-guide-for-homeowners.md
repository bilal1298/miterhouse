Sod installation runs $1.00 to $2.50 per square foot fully installed, putting a 2,000-square-foot front yard at $2,000–$5,000 total. The sod itself is only $0.35–$0.85 per square foot — site preparation and labor make up the rest, and that's where budgets quietly balloon.

## Where the Money Goes

### Materials

- **Sod rolls or pallets:** $0.35–$0.85/sq ft depending on grass variety. Bermuda and St. Augustine in Texas or Florida run $0.35–$0.55 because they grow fast locally. Kentucky Bluegrass in the upper Midwest or Northeast costs $0.55–$0.85 — pickier growing conditions and longer transport.
- **Topsoil or soil amendments:** $25–$50 per cubic yard
- **Starter fertilizer:** $15–$30 per bag (one bag covers roughly 5,000 sq ft)
- **Delivery:** $50–$150 depending on distance and quantity

A pallet covers roughly 450–500 square feet. For a 2,000-square-foot yard, you need about four pallets. Most suppliers require a minimum order of one full pallet.

### Site Preparation

This is where most budgets go sideways:

- **Grading and leveling:** $0.50–$1.50/sq ft if the ground is rough
- **Old lawn removal:** $75–$150/day sod cutter rental, or $300–$600 for professional removal
- **Soil testing:** $15–$30 through your county extension office
- **Irrigation adjustments:** $200–$500 if sprinkler heads need repositioning

### Labor

Professional installation labor runs $0.50–$1.50 per square foot on top of materials. Regional rates vary — a crew in rural Alabama charges less than one in suburban New Jersey because of overhead and cost-of-living differences, not quality differences.

## The Site Prep Nobody Budgets For

The sod is only as good as what's underneath it. Lay fresh rolls over compacted clay, weedy soil, or ungraded ground and you'll end up with a patchy, bumpy lawn that dies in spots within the first season.

**1. Kill and remove existing vegetation.** A sod cutter ($75–$150/half day rental) strips old lawn cleanly. If using herbicide, wait at least two weeks before laying new sod.

**2. Test your soil.** A $15–$30 test through your county extension service tells you exactly what amendments you need. Guessing wastes money on products you may not need and skips ones you do.

**3. Grade the surface.** The ground must slope away from your foundation at roughly 1 inch per foot for the first 6 feet. This is non-negotiable for drainage — skip it and water pools against the house.

**4. Amend the soil.** Spread 2–4 inches of quality topsoil or compost and till it into the top 4–6 inches of existing soil.

**5. Rake smooth and roll.** A lawn roller (half-filled with water) firms the seedbed so sod makes solid soil contact without air pockets underneath.

Skip any of these steps and you'll spend more fixing the lawn later than you would have spent doing it right. If a [deck building project](/blog/deck-building-permit-cost-breakdown-real-numbers/) tore up your backyard, this prep work is especially critical.

## DIY vs. Hiring Out

DIY cuts your total cost by 40–60%, but sod installation is genuinely hard physical labor. A pallet weighs 1,500–3,000 pounds. Each roll weighs 30–50 pounds. You'll be bending, lifting, and kneeling for hours in the sun.

**DIY makes sense when:**
- The area is under 1,000 square feet
- The ground is flat with minimal grading needed
- You have truck or trailer access for delivery
- Soil is already in decent condition

**Hire a crew when:**
- Slopes, drainage issues, or heavy clay soil need professional grading
- The area exceeds 2,000 square feet
- Irrigation work needs to happen simultaneously
- You need it done fast — sod dies on a pallet within 24–48 hours in warm weather

If you go DIY, rent a sod cutter rather than stripping old lawn by hand. And have your soil prepped before the sod arrives — you can't store rolls in the sun and come back tomorrow. They heat up, yellow, and die faster than you'd expect.

## Getting Accurate Quotes

A quote that says "$1.50 per square foot installed" could mean wildly different things. Ask whether it covers:

- Removal of existing lawn or weeds
- Grading and soil preparation
- Soil amendments and starter fertilizer
- Delivery charges
- Post-installation watering setup

Get at least three written quotes with these items itemized separately. The [FTC's guide on hiring contractors](https://www.consumer.ftc.gov/articles/hiring-contractor) covers what to look for in written estimates.

A legitimate landscaper should name their grass supplier. Good sod comes from certified sod farms — verify through your state's turfgrass association. Cheap sod from an unknown source may carry weeds, nematodes, or fungal issues that don't show up for weeks.

## After Installation: The First 90 Days

New sod needs daily watering — 1 to 1.5 inches for the first two weeks, then tapering gradually over six weeks. Your water bill during establishment can spike $50–$200/month depending on yard size and local rates. Budget for it.

No mowing for the first 2–3 weeks. When you can tug a corner of sod and feel firm root resistance, it's safe to mow. That takes 3–4 weeks in warm weather, longer in cooler conditions.

The single most common mistake after installation: cutting water too early. Keep watering consistently until roots grip. A second fertilizer application around week 4–6 helps establishment.

After the 90-day window, maintenance drops to normal lawn care — roughly $30–$60/month for a 2,000-square-foot lawn if you handle mowing and basic care yourself.
