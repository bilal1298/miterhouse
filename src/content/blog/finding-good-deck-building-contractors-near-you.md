Hiring the right deck contractor is the difference between a deck that lasts 25 years and one that develops problems within 3. A good deck builder charges $15–$35 per square foot for labor on a standard build. The challenge is separating experienced professionals from the handymen and fly-by-night operations that flood the market every spring.

Here's how to make that decision — who to call, what to ask, and which answers should have you walking away.

## The Decision Starts with Where You Look

Skip the general "contractor finder" websites where anyone can pay for a listing. The source of the referral tells you a lot about the contractor's quality.

**Best sources:**

- **Neighbor referrals.** Walk through your neighborhood and look at decks that appear well-built and well-maintained. Knock on the door and ask who built it. People are surprisingly willing to share — especially after a good experience.
- **Lumber yard referrals.** Local lumber yards (not big-box stores) know which contractors buy quality materials, pay their bills, and build competently. Ask the sales staff who they'd hire. These referrals carry weight because lumber yards see the contractor's habits firsthand.
- **Material manufacturer directories.** Composite decking brands like Trex, TimberTech, and Fiberon maintain lists of certified or preferred installers. These contractors have completed manufacturer training and have more experience with specific materials.
- **Local building department.** Some departments can tell you which contractors pull the most permits and which have the fewest code violation callbacks. This is public information in most jurisdictions.

**Sources to use cautiously:**

- Online review platforms work for initial research but are easily gamed. Look for detailed reviews that describe specific project aspects, not generic praise.
- Social media groups can surface recommendations but also attract self-promotion.
- Contractor matching services charge contractors for leads, which incentivizes aggressive sales over quality.

## What Separates a Pro from a Pretender

Not all contractors who build decks are equally qualified. Here's the checklist that matters.

**License and insurance — non-negotiable.** Your state or municipality likely requires a contractor's license for deck building (it's structural work). Ask for the license number and verify it. Insurance must include general liability ($1 million minimum) and workers' compensation. Ask for certificates and verify directly with the insurer. If a worker gets injured on your property and the contractor has no workers' comp, you may be liable.

**Deck-specific experience.** A contractor who primarily does interior remodeling, roofing, or general carpentry may not understand the structural requirements, fastener compatibility issues, and drainage considerations unique to decks. Ask how many decks they build per year. A dedicated deck builder completes 15–40 annually and has encountered every type of site condition.

**Permit handling.** A reputable deck contractor pulls the building permit as standard. If a contractor suggests skipping the permit or asks you to pull it yourself, walk away. Contractors pull permits under their own license, which ties the quality of the work to their professional standing. For more on what [deck permits cost](/blog/deck-building-permit-cost-breakdown-real-numbers/) and why they matter, see our detailed breakdown.

**Code knowledge.** Ask a few specific questions: What's the maximum joist span for 2x10 at 16-inch centers in your area? What fastener spacing does code require for the ledger? What's the minimum railing height for a deck 36 inches above grade? A knowledgeable contractor answers confidently because they deal with inspectors regularly. The [AWC (American Wood Council)](https://www.awc.org/) publishes the span tables and design guides contractors should reference.

**A clear, detailed contract.** The contract should specify everything: materials (brand, size, species), timeline, payment schedule, permit responsibility, cleanup, warranty terms, and what happens if the scope changes. "Build 16x20 deck, $X" is not a contract — it's an invitation for misunderstanding.

## Getting and Comparing Quotes

Get at least three quotes. Give each contractor the same information:

- Deck size and shape
- Desired materials
- Railing type
- Stair locations and height
- Built-in features (benches, planters, pergola)
- Old deck demolition requirements
- Your timeline

**What the quote should include:**

A professional quote breaks down costs by category: materials, labor, permits, demolition (if applicable), and contingency. It specifies the decking brand and product, the framing lumber species and grade, the hardware brand, and the railing system. It includes a start date, estimated completion date, and payment schedule.

For context on what reasonable [deck costs look like](/blog/deck-building-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/) by material and region, see our full cost breakdown.

**Red flags in quotes:**

- Significantly lower than other bids (cutting corners on materials or skipping steps)
- Requires more than 30–50% upfront
- No mention of permits
- Vague material descriptions ("composite decking" without specifying brand/product)
- Verbal quote only, resistance to putting it in writing
- Pressure to sign immediately

**The lowest bid is rarely the best value.** On deck projects, the lowest bid means cheaper materials, fewer fasteners, skipped steps (like flashing the ledger properly or compacting gravel under footings), or inexperienced labor. The middle or highest bid from a well-reviewed contractor delivers better long-term value.

## Questions to Ask Before You Hire

1. **Who will actually be on my jobsite?** Is it the contractor and their crew, or will they subcontract? You want to know who's physically building your deck.
2. **Can I see 3–5 decks you've built in the last year?** Drive by and look at them. Better yet, ask if those homeowners would take a call.
3. **How do you handle the ledger board connection?** The most structurally critical joint on an attached deck. The answer should include lag screws or through-bolts, proper flashing, and siding removal at the attachment point.
4. **What happens if we hit unexpected conditions?** Rock underground, rotten rim joist behind the siding, or drainage issues can change the scope. A professional has a change-order process.
5. **What warranty do you offer on labor?** Most reputable deck builders offer 1–5 years on workmanship. Material warranties come from the manufacturer.
6. **What's your payment schedule?** Reasonable: 10–30% at signing, 30–40% at framing completion, balance at project completion. Never pay 100% upfront.

## Understanding the Contract

Before signing, verify these items are in the contract:

- **Total price and payment schedule** with clear milestones
- **Detailed material list** including brands, sizes, and quantities
- **Start and completion dates** with a clause addressing delays (weather, material availability)
- **Permit responsibility** (contractor handles)
- **Site cleanup and debris removal** included
- **Warranty terms** for both workmanship and materials
- **Change order process** (how scope changes are approved and priced)
- **Cancellation terms** for both parties
- **Lien waiver** provision (contractor provides lien waivers from subcontractors and suppliers upon final payment)

The lien waiver point matters. If your contractor doesn't pay their lumber supplier, the supplier can place a mechanic's lien on your property — even if you paid the contractor in full. Collecting lien waivers protects you.

## During the Build: What to Watch For

Once the project starts, stay engaged without micromanaging. Check progress daily and look for these quality indicators:

- **Footings are the right depth** and have gravel below the concrete
- **Ledger flashing is properly installed** under the siding, not just caulked over it
- **Joist hangers at every connection** (not just toenailed)
- **All hardware rated for the lumber treatment** (ACQ-compatible for pressure-treated)
- **The frame is level and square** before decking goes on
- **Decking gaps are consistent** and appropriate for the material
- **Screws driven flush**, not overdriven into the board surface

Attend each building inspection. The inspector works for you (through the permit process), and their findings confirm the work meets code. If the inspector requires corrections, make sure the contractor addresses them before moving to the next phase.

## After Completion

**Walk the finished deck carefully** before making final payment. Check every board for damage, verify railing stability by pushing against it, test every stair step, and look underneath for missed connections or debris.

**Get all documentation:** final inspection certificate, material warranties, contractor workmanship warranty, lien waivers, and maintenance instructions for your decking material.

**Schedule a 6-month follow-up.** Good contractors welcome this. After the deck has gone through temperature and moisture changes, minor adjustments — tightening a railing connection, adjusting a shifted board — are normal and should be covered by the workmanship warranty.

Finding a good deck contractor takes 2–3 weeks of research and conversations, but it's the highest-value time you can spend on this project. A skilled, honest contractor builds a deck that lasts decades with minimal maintenance. A bad one builds problems that surface after the final check clears. Verify licenses, check references, compare detailed quotes, and trust your instincts about the contractor's professionalism. If something feels off during the quoting process, it'll be worse during the build.
