Installing attic insulation costs between $1,500 and $3,500 for most homes, with the national average landing around $2,200 for a standard 1,000-square-foot attic. That range swings based on insulation type, existing conditions, your climate zone, and whether you hire a contractor or rent a blower and do it yourself.

The payback on attic insulation is among the best of any home improvement project. The Department of Energy estimates that proper attic insulation reduces heating and cooling costs by 10% to 50%, depending on what you are starting with. If your attic has thin, compressed, or missing insulation, this is one of the few upgrades that pays for itself within a few years.

## Cost Breakdown by Insulation Type

The material you choose is the biggest cost variable. Here is what each option actually costs installed in a typical attic:

### Blown-In Cellulose

- **Material cost:** $0.60 to $1.00 per square foot
- **Professional installation:** $1.00 to $1.80 per square foot
- **DIY cost (renting a blower):** $0.60 to $1.20 per square foot
- **R-value per inch:** R-3.2 to R-3.8
- **Best for:** Open attic floors, topping off existing insulation

Cellulose is recycled newspaper treated with borate fire retardant. It is the most cost-effective option for most attics and settles into gaps and irregular spaces better than batts. Big box stores like Home Depot often include free blower rental when you buy 20 or more bags.

### Blown-In Fiberglass

- **Material cost:** $0.70 to $1.20 per square foot
- **Professional installation:** $1.20 to $2.00 per square foot
- **DIY cost:** $0.80 to $1.40 per square foot
- **R-value per inch:** R-2.2 to R-2.7
- **Best for:** Attics where moisture is a concern (fiberglass does not absorb water like cellulose)

Fiberglass loose-fill requires more depth than cellulose to reach the same R-value, which means more material and slightly higher cost per R-value achieved.

### Fiberglass Batts

- **Material cost:** $0.50 to $1.00 per square foot
- **Professional installation:** $1.00 to $1.70 per square foot
- **DIY cost:** $0.50 to $1.00 per square foot (no blower needed)
- **R-value per inch:** R-3.0 to R-3.7
- **Best for:** Attics with standard joist spacing (16 or 24 inches on center) and minimal obstructions

Batts are the cheapest to DIY because they require no special equipment — just a utility knife and a straightedge. The downside is that batts leave gaps around pipes, wires, junction boxes, and irregular framing. Those gaps reduce the effective R-value significantly. Unless your attic floor is completely clean and regular, blown-in is the better choice.

### Spray Foam

- **Material cost:** Not practical for DIY at attic scale
- **Professional installation (open-cell):** $1.50 to $2.50 per square foot
- **Professional installation (closed-cell):** $3.00 to $6.00 per square foot
- **R-value per inch:** R-3.6 (open-cell), R-6.0 to R-7.0 (closed-cell)
- **Best for:** Attic roof decks (when converting attic to conditioned space), cathedral ceilings

Spray foam at the roof deck turns the attic into conditioned space, which is a different strategy than insulating the attic floor. It costs substantially more but eliminates attic ventilation requirements and can make the attic usable.

## What Else Affects the Price

### Existing Insulation Condition

If your attic has old, damaged, or contaminated insulation, it may need [removal before new installation](/blog/removing-insulation-from-attic-cost-breakdown-real-numbers/). Removal adds $1.00 to $2.50 per square foot. Old vermiculite insulation (found in homes from the 1920s through 1990s) should be tested for asbestos before disturbance — testing costs $25 to $50.

If existing insulation is in decent shape, you can often blow new insulation directly on top. This is the most cost-effective approach for homes that simply need more depth to reach current code R-values.

### Air Sealing

The biggest impact on insulation performance is not the insulation itself — it is the air sealing done before the insulation goes in. Warm air leaking through gaps around light fixtures, plumbing penetrations, electrical wires, and the attic hatch carries far more heat than conduction through insulation.

Professional air sealing before insulation adds $350 to $1,500 to the project. It involves sealing gaps with caulk, spray foam, and metal flashing. The [EPA's guidance on radon](https://www.epa.gov/radon) also recommends sealing penetrations between the living space and attic as part of radon mitigation — so this step has multiple benefits.

If you are spending $2,000 or more on insulation without air sealing, you are leaving significant performance on the table.

### Attic Access and Conditions

- **Walk-up stairs or large hatch:** Standard pricing
- **Small hatch or pull-down ladder:** May add $200 to $400 for restricted access
- **Low clearance areas:** Knee walls and areas under 3 feet require extra labor time
- **Old wiring or knob-and-tube electrical:** Must be addressed before covering with insulation (fire hazard). Rewiring is a separate cost, often $1,000 to $3,000 for the attic portion.

### Climate Zone and R-Value Targets

The [IRC building code](https://www.epa.gov/mold) and Energy Star recommendations set target R-values by climate zone:

- **Zones 1-3 (Southern states):** R-30 to R-38 (about 10 to 13 inches of cellulose)
- **Zone 4 (Mid-Atlantic, moderate climates):** R-38 to R-49 (13 to 17 inches)
- **Zones 5-8 (Northern states, mountains):** R-49 to R-60 (17 to 21 inches)

More insulation means more material, which directly increases cost. A Zone 7 home reaching R-60 will cost 40% to 60% more in materials than a Zone 2 home reaching R-30.

## DIY vs. Professional Installation

### The DIY Path

Blown-in cellulose is the most popular DIY attic insulation project, and it is genuinely doable. Here is what is involved:

1. Measure your attic square footage and determine target R-value depth.
2. Buy the bags (typically 15 to 25 bags for a 1,000 sq ft attic reaching R-38).
3. Rent the blower (often free with sufficient bag purchases).
4. Seal air leaks before insulating — this is critical and often skipped by DIYers.
5. Install depth markers (rulers stapled to joists every 6 feet).
6. Blow insulation to the target depth, working from the far end toward the access point.

**What to watch out for:**
- Do not cover soffit vents. Install baffles in each rafter bay at the eaves to maintain airflow.
- Do not cover recessed lighting fixtures unless they are IC-rated (insulation contact approved). Non-IC fixtures are a fire hazard.
- Wear an N95 respirator, safety glasses, long sleeves, and gloves. Attics are dusty and often hot.

### The Professional Path

Professional installation makes sense when:
- Air sealing is part of the scope (specialists have thermal cameras and know where to seal)
- You need [old insulation removed first](/blog/how-much-does-replace-attic-insulation-really-cost/)
- Attic access is difficult or space is tight
- You want the job done in one day rather than a weekend
- Rebates or incentives require professional installation (many utility programs do)

A professional crew typically handles a standard attic in 2 to 4 hours. They bring their own commercial-grade blower, which is significantly faster than the rental units available at home centers.

## Rebates and Incentives

This is where attic insulation gets even more attractive financially.

- **Federal tax credits:** The Inflation Reduction Act provides a 30% tax credit (up to $1,200 per year) for qualifying insulation improvements. This applies to both materials and installation labor.
- **Utility rebates:** Many electric and gas utilities offer $200 to $1,000 in rebates for attic insulation upgrades. Check with your local provider.
- **State programs:** Some states offer additional incentives through weatherization assistance programs.

Between federal tax credits and utility rebates, it is possible to offset 30% to 50% of the project cost. These incentives change annually, so check current availability before committing.

## Getting Quotes and Comparing Contractors

When getting professional quotes for [attic insulation installation](/blog/replacing-insulation-in-attic-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/):

- Get three written quotes minimum
- Ensure each quote specifies: insulation type, target R-value, whether air sealing is included, whether old insulation removal is needed
- Ask if the contractor offers assistance with rebate paperwork
- Verify they carry liability insurance
- Ask about warranty — both on the insulation product and the installation workmanship

The cheapest quote is not always the best value. A contractor who includes air sealing at $2,800 will deliver better energy savings than one who skips it at $1,800. The [total cost of attic insulation replacement](/blog/how-much-does-attic-insulation-replacement-really-cost/) includes the long-term energy savings, not just the installation bill.

## The Bottom Line

For most homeowners, the sweet spot is professional blown-in cellulose with air sealing, targeting your climate zone's recommended R-value. Budget $1,800 to $3,500 for a standard attic, subtract any available rebates and tax credits, and expect to recover the investment in energy savings within 3 to 7 years.

If you go DIY, blown-in cellulose with a rental blower brings the material cost to $600 to $1,200 for a typical attic. Add baffles, depth markers, and air sealing materials for another $100 to $300. The savings are significant, but only if you actually do the air sealing — that is the step that separates an effective insulation project from an expensive layer of fluff.
