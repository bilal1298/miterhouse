Painting kitchen cabinets professionally costs $3,000 to $7,000 for a standard kitchen with 20 to 30 cabinet doors and drawer fronts. DIY, you can do the same job for $200 to $600 in materials — but the gap between those two numbers is filled with labor, skill, and a finish quality that makes the difference between cabinets that look freshly painted for a decade and ones that chip and peel within two years.

This is the single highest-impact, lowest-cost kitchen transformation you can make. New cabinets start at $5,000 and climb past $20,000. Painting gives you a dramatically different kitchen for a fraction of that.

## Professional vs. DIY: The Real Comparison

Let me lay this out honestly, because this is one project where the DIY vs. pro decision is genuinely close:

| Factor | Professional | DIY |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | $3,000-$7,000 | $200-$600 |
| Time | 3-5 days | 3-6 weekends |
| Finish quality | Spray finish, factory-like | Brush/roller marks likely |
| Durability | 8-15 years before recoat | 3-7 years before touch-ups |
| Disruption | Kitchen unusable 3-5 days | Doors off intermittently for weeks |

Professional painters typically spray cabinets (often off-site in a spray booth), which produces a smoother, more durable finish than any brush or roller can achieve. That finish quality is what you are paying for above the material cost.

DIY painting works well if you have patience, proper prep discipline, and realistic expectations. The materials are the same — the difference is application method and skill.

## Material Costs (Same for DIY or Pro)

Regardless of who does the work, the materials required are:

| Item | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Primer (bonding/adhesion type) | $30-$60 | Covers 20-30 doors. BIN shellac or Stix are popular |
| Paint (cabinet-grade) | $40-$80/gallon | 2 gallons covers most standard kitchens |
| Sandpaper (120 and 220 grit) | $15-$25 | Multiple sheets, sanding sponges for profiles |
| Tack cloth | $5-$10 | Removes sanding dust before painting |
| Degreaser/TSP substitute | $8-$15 | Kitchen cabinets accumulate cooking grease |
| Painter's tape | $10-$20 | For hardware holes, hinges, and cabinet boxes |
| Drop cloths | $10-$20 | Protect counters, floors, and appliances |
| Brushes and rollers (if DIY) | $25-$50 | Foam rollers for doors, angled brush for details |
| **Total materials** | **$143-$280** | |

The paint choice matters more than anything else. Use a cabinet-specific enamel — Benjamin Moore Advance, Sherwin-Williams Emerald Urethane Trim Enamel, or similar. Regular wall paint will not hold up to the abuse kitchen cabinets take. These paints cost $50-$80 per gallon, and you need the good stuff. Do not economize here.

If your cabinets were built before 1978, test for lead paint before sanding. The [EPA's lead safety information](https://www.epa.gov/lead) explains the requirements and certified testing options. Disturbing lead paint without proper precautions creates a serious health hazard and legal liability.

## Professional Cabinet Painting Breakdown

When a professional painter quotes $3,000 to $7,000, here is where that money goes:

| Item | Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Materials (primer, paint, supplies) | $200-$500 |
| Removal of doors and hardware | $200-$400 |
| Surface preparation (clean, sand, repair) | $400-$800 |
| Priming (1-2 coats) | $300-$600 |
| Painting (2-3 coats) | $600-$1,200 |
| Cabinet box painting (interiors optional) | $400-$800 |
| Reinstallation and hardware | $200-$400 |
| Spray equipment and booth costs | $300-$600 |
| **Total** | **$2,600-$5,300** |

Larger kitchens, complex door profiles (raised panel, cathedral), and difficult colors (dark over light, or white over dark stain) push toward the higher end. Simple shaker-style doors in a light-to-light color change stay at the lower end.

Some painters do all the work on-site using HVLP sprayers. Others remove the doors and spray them in their shop, then paint the cabinet boxes on-site by brush and roller. The shop-sprayed approach usually produces a better finish on the doors (the most visible parts) while keeping the on-site mess manageable.

## The DIY Process: Step by Step

If you decide to tackle this yourself, the process is labor-intensive but not technically difficult. Success depends almost entirely on preparation — specifically, how thoroughly you clean, sand, and prime before paint ever touches the surface.

### Phase 1: Preparation (This Takes Longer Than Painting)

**Number and label everything.** Before removing a single door, number each door and its corresponding hinge location. Use blue painter's tape with numbers on both the door and the cabinet frame. Take a photo of your numbering system. Cabinets look similar but are not interchangeable — a door that is 1/8-inch too wide for the wrong opening will not close properly.

**Remove all doors, drawers, and hardware.** Bag and label the hardware from each door. If you plan to reuse hinges, keep each set together. Many homeowners use this as an opportunity to upgrade to soft-close hinges ($2-$5 per hinge).

**Clean every surface thoroughly.** Kitchen cabinets accumulate a film of cooking grease, especially above and beside the stove. TSP substitute (trisodium phosphate alternative) or a degreaser like Krud Kutter strips this film. Paint will not adhere to grease. This step is non-negotiable.

**Sand all surfaces.** 120-grit for initial scuffing on previously finished surfaces, then 220-grit for final smoothing after primer. You are not trying to remove the existing finish — just creating a roughened surface for the primer to grip. For profile details on raised-panel doors, use sanding sponges that conform to curves.

**Fill any dents, holes, or damage** with wood filler. Sand smooth after it dries. Old hardware holes that will not be reused should be filled and sanded flush.

### Phase 2: Priming

**Use a bonding primer** — not regular primer. BIN shellac-based primer, Zinsser Cover Stain, or Stix bonding primer are the standard choices for cabinet work. A bonding primer is formulated to adhere to slick, previously finished surfaces that regular primer cannot grip.

Apply one coat of primer to all surfaces. Let it cure fully (check the can — some bonding primers need 24 hours). Lightly sand with 220-grit after curing, then wipe with a tack cloth to remove dust.

### Phase 3: Painting

Apply 2 to 3 thin coats of cabinet-grade enamel. Thin coats are critical — one thick coat creates runs, sags, and uneven coverage. Each coat should be dried fully before the next is applied. Most cabinet enamels need 16 to 24 hours between coats at 70 degrees.

**For brush and roller application:** Use a 4-inch foam roller for flat panels and a 2-inch angled brush for profiles, edges, and the cabinet box interior. Roll in one direction, then lightly tip off with the roller to even out the texture.

**For sprayer application (if you own or rent one):** HVLP or airless sprayers produce a much smoother finish. Practice on cardboard first. Multiple light passes beat one heavy pass every time.

### Phase 4: Reassembly

Wait a minimum of 3 days after the final coat before reinstalling doors. Cabinet enamel is dry to the touch in hours but continues curing for 2 to 4 weeks. During that curing period, the paint is softer and more vulnerable to denting, sticking, and marking. Handle painted doors with cotton gloves, and do not close them fully for the first week (leave cracked open with a small piece of painter's tape as a spacer).

## Colors and Trends That Last

Avoid trendy colors unless you are comfortable repainting in 5 years. Colors with staying power:

- **White (various warm and cool shades)** — has been the most popular kitchen cabinet color for decades and shows no sign of fading
- **Light gray** — a modern alternative to white that hides wear slightly better
- **Navy or dark blue** — works well on lower cabinets with white uppers (two-tone)
- **Black or very dark charcoal** — dramatic, works in specific kitchen styles, shows dust readily

Colors to approach cautiously: sage green, forest green, and any very bold accent colors look great in design photos but can feel oppressive in a small kitchen and limit your decor flexibility.

## When Painting Is NOT the Right Choice

Painting cannot fix every cabinet problem:

- **Damaged or delaminating MDF/thermofoil.** If the cabinet surface is peeling, bubbling, or warped, paint will not hide these defects. Replacement is the only real fix.
- **Water-damaged cabinets.** Swollen particleboard or MDF cannot be sanded smooth enough for a quality paint finish.
- **Cabinets with construction issues.** Doors that do not close, drawers that stick, and shelves that sag are structural problems that painting does not address.
- **Very dark stain to white.** This is possible but difficult — it requires multiple coats of heavy-duty primer and paint to prevent bleed-through. The total cost approaches the territory where refacing (new doors on existing boxes) starts to make sense.

If your cabinets are structurally sound but the doors are dated styles, consider hybrid approach: paint the existing boxes and order new replacement doors in a simple shaker profile ($15-$40 per door from online suppliers). This gives you a fully refreshed look at 30-40% of the new cabinet cost.

## Protecting the Finish Long-Term

Cabinet paint in a kitchen endures more abuse than almost any other painted surface in your home — heat, steam, grease, repeated touching, and daily opening and closing.

To maximize the lifespan of your newly painted cabinets:

- Allow the full 2-4 week cure time before aggressive cleaning
- Use soft cloths and mild cleaners — avoid abrasive sponges or cleaners containing ammonia
- Address chips and scratches promptly with touch-up paint (keep a labeled jar of leftover paint)
- Install soft-close hinges to prevent door-slamming impact
- Use cabinet bumper pads where doors contact the frame

A properly prepped and painted cabinet job using quality materials should last 8 to 15 years before needing a refresh. Cut corners on prep or paint quality, and you are looking at 2 to 3 years before it looks tired.
