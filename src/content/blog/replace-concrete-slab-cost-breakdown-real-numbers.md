Replacing a concrete slab costs $6 to $16 per square foot for a standard 4-inch residential slab, including demolition, disposal, and the new pour. For a typical 400 sq ft patio or garage floor, that puts the total between $2,400 and $6,400. The actual number depends on slab thickness, access difficulty, soil conditions, and your local labor market.

Here is the full cost breakdown, what drives prices up or down, and how to decide between repair and full replacement.

## When Replacement Is Necessary vs. When Repair Works

Not every cracked or settled slab needs replacement. Understanding the difference saves thousands.

**Repair is usually sufficient when:**
- Hairline cracks (less than 1/4 inch wide) that are not growing
- Surface spalling or flaking without structural damage
- Minor settling (less than 1 inch) that can be corrected with mudjacking or foam lifting
- Cosmetic issues that a resurfacing overlay can address

**Replacement is necessary when:**
- Cracks exceed 1/2 inch wide and are actively growing
- The slab has settled more than 2 inches or is tilting significantly
- Tree roots have heaved sections beyond repair
- The subgrade has failed (voids, washout, organic material decomposition)
- The slab is too thin for its load (a 2-inch slab under a garage, for example)
- Plumbing or utility lines beneath the slab need replacement

## Complete Cost Breakdown

Here is what each phase of a concrete slab replacement costs, broken into its components.

### Demolition and Removal

| Item | Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Concrete demolition (per sq ft) | $2-$6 |
| Rebar/mesh removal | $0.50-$1.50 per sq ft |
| Hauling and disposal | $300-$800 per load |
| Dumpster rental (alternative) | $350-$600 for a 10-yard container |

Demolition costs vary enormously based on slab thickness, whether the concrete is reinforced, and site access. A 4-inch unreinforced patio slab that a skid steer can reach costs far less to remove than a 6-inch reinforced garage floor with narrow gate access.

Reinforced concrete (with rebar or wire mesh) takes 30-50% longer to demolish and creates heavier, harder-to-handle debris. Disposal fees vary by region; concrete is heavy (roughly 150 lbs per cubic foot), and haulers charge by weight or volume.

### Site Preparation

| Item | Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Grading and compaction | $1-$3 per sq ft |
| Gravel base (4-6 inches) | $1.50-$3 per sq ft |
| Vapor barrier (for interior slabs) | $0.15-$0.50 per sq ft |
| Form construction | $1-$2.50 per linear ft |

Proper site preparation is where cheap concrete jobs reveal themselves. The subgrade needs to be compacted to at least 95% density, and a gravel base (typically 4-6 inches of compacted crushed stone) provides drainage and a stable foundation. Skipping the gravel or under-compacting the soil leads to the same settling and cracking that caused the original failure.

For interior slabs (basements, garages), a 6-mil polyethylene vapor barrier between the gravel and concrete prevents moisture migration. It costs almost nothing and prevents significant problems.

### Concrete Materials and Pour

| Item | Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Ready-mix concrete (per cubic yard) | $130-$180 |
| Fiber mesh reinforcement | $0.15-$0.30 per sq ft |
| Wire mesh (6x6 W1.4) | $0.40-$0.80 per sq ft |
| Rebar (#4, 24" on center) | $0.75-$1.50 per sq ft |
| Pump truck (if needed) | $300-$800 |
| Concrete delivery (short load fee) | $50-$100 per yard under minimum |

A 400 sq ft slab at 4 inches thick requires approximately 5 cubic yards of concrete. At $150 per yard, that is $750 for the material alone. Most ready-mix companies have a minimum delivery (typically 3-5 yards), and they charge a short-load fee if you order less.

Reinforcement adds cost but dramatically extends slab life. At minimum, use fiber mesh reinforcement mixed into the concrete. For driveways and garages, wire mesh or rebar on chairs provides significantly better crack resistance, especially in freeze-thaw climates.

### Labor

| Item | Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Concrete finishing labor | $3-$8 per sq ft |
| Experienced finisher (per hour) | $35-$65 |
| Helper/laborer (per hour) | $18-$30 |

Labor is typically the single largest cost component. A crew of 3-4 can pour, screed, float, and finish a 400 sq ft slab in one day, but the labor cost for that day runs $1,200-$3,200 depending on your market.

Finishing quality matters enormously. A poorly finished slab shows trowel marks, has inconsistent texture, cracks at uncut control joints, and traps water in low spots. Experienced concrete finishers are worth every dollar.

### Finishing and Extras

| Item | Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Broom finish (standard) | Included in labor |
| Stamped concrete | Add $8-$18 per sq ft |
| Exposed aggregate | Add $4-$8 per sq ft |
| Stained/colored concrete | Add $2-$6 per sq ft |
| Control joint cutting | $1-$2 per linear ft |
| Curing compound | $0.10-$0.20 per sq ft |

Control joints (saw cuts or tooled grooves) must be placed at intervals no greater than 2-3 times the slab thickness in feet. For a 4-inch slab, that means joints every 8-12 feet. These joints control where the concrete cracks, turning random cracks into straight, planned lines. Skipping them is a guaranteed path to ugly random cracking.

Pulling all components together for common residential projects:

## DIY vs. Hiring a Contractor

Concrete work is one of those projects where the DIY savings are real but the risk of a bad outcome is high.

**What you can realistically DIY:**
- Small slabs under 100 sq ft (using bagged concrete mix)
- Demolition and site prep to reduce contractor costs
- Form building (with carpentry skills)

**What you should hire out:**
- Any slab over 200 sq ft (ready-mix coordination and finishing speed are critical)
- Structural slabs (garages, foundations)
- Slabs with plumbing or utility penetrations

The window for finishing concrete is unforgiving. Once the truck arrives, you have 60-90 minutes to place, screed, and begin finishing before the concrete sets beyond workability. An inexperienced person finishing a 400 sq ft slab alone will almost certainly end up with a poor result.

## How to Get Accurate Quotes

When requesting quotes for concrete slab replacement, provide these specifics to get comparable bids:

1. Exact dimensions and desired thickness
2. Current slab condition (photos help)
3. Access situation (can a concrete truck reach the site? Is there a gate?)
4. Desired finish type
5. Any utilities or plumbing under the slab
6. Whether you want to handle demolition yourself

Get three written quotes that itemize demolition, prep, materials, labor, and finishing separately. A single lump-sum number makes it impossible to compare bids meaningfully.

## Timeline Expectations

A typical residential slab replacement follows this timeline:

- **Day 1:** Demolition and debris removal
- **Day 2:** Grading, compaction, gravel base, and form setting
- **Day 3:** Concrete pour and finishing
- **Days 4-7:** Initial curing (keep wet, no traffic)
- **Day 28:** Full cure (light vehicle traffic on driveways)

Weather affects every phase. Concrete should not be poured when temperatures are below 40°F or above 90°F. Rain during the pour or within the first 24 hours can damage the surface. Plan accordingly and be prepared for schedule shifts.

## The Bottom Line

Concrete slab replacement is a significant but bounded expense. Budget $6-$16 per square foot for a standard replacement, add 10-20% for contingencies, and hire an experienced crew for anything larger than a small walkway section. The money you spend on proper subgrade preparation and quality finishing determines whether the new slab lasts 10 years or 40. That is not a place to cut corners.

Review your project alongside your [overall renovation timeline](/blog/bathroom-remodel-timeline/) to coordinate slab work with any other outdoor or structural projects you have planned. Concrete trucks and equipment need access, and scheduling overlapping projects carefully avoids costly rework.
