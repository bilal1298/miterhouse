Professional interior house painting services cost $2 to $6 per square foot of wall space, or roughly $1,500 to $4,500 for a typical 1,500-square-foot home. That includes labor, paint, primer, and basic prep work like filling nail holes and light sanding. More involved prep — wallpaper removal, significant drywall repair, or lead paint abatement — adds $500 to $3,000 depending on scope.

Whether you hire a painting crew or tackle it yourself, understanding what the job actually involves helps you get better results and avoid the most common mistakes.

## What Professional Painters Actually Do (That DIYers Often Skip)

The visible part of painting — rolling color on a wall — represents maybe 30 percent of the total work. The rest is preparation, and that is where professional results diverge from amateur ones.

### Surface Preparation

A good painting crew spends the first day (sometimes two) on prep before a roller ever touches a wall:

**Cleaning walls:** Kitchens, bathrooms, and hallways accumulate grease, smoke residue, and hand oils that prevent paint adhesion. Pros wash these surfaces with TSP (trisodium phosphate) or a TSP substitute. Most homeowners skip this entirely and wonder why their kitchen paint peels within a year.

**Filling and sanding:** Every nail hole, screw pop, hairline crack, and dent gets filled with lightweight spackle, dried, and sanded smooth. In a lived-in house, this can mean 50 to 200 individual spots per room. Pros use a shop light held at a raking angle to find imperfections invisible under normal lighting.

**Caulking gaps:** The joints where walls meet trim, where trim meets door frames, and where baseboards meet walls develop gaps over time as the house settles. Painters fill these with paintable latex caulk before cutting in. A clean caulk line is the single biggest visual difference between professional and amateur paint jobs.

**Priming problem areas:** Stains (water marks, smoke damage, marker, crayon), new drywall repairs, and areas where old paint is significantly darker than the new color all need primer. Not paint-and-primer-in-one — actual dedicated primer. Shellac-based primers like BIN block virtually any stain; latex primers handle lighter duties.

### Protection

Professional crews tape trim, cover floors with canvas drop cloths (not plastic — canvas absorbs drips, plastic spreads them), and plastic-sheet furniture. The time spent on protection directly correlates with the quality of the final result and the condition of your belongings.

## Choosing the Right Paint

Paint technology has improved dramatically in the past decade. Here is what matters for interior work:

### Sheen Levels

| Sheen | Where to Use | Why |
|-------|-------------|-----|
| Flat/Matte | Ceilings, low-traffic rooms, textured walls | Hides imperfections, no reflectivity. Not washable. |
| Eggshell | Living rooms, bedrooms, dining rooms | Slight sheen, washable, still forgiving on imperfections. The most popular interior sheen. |
| Satin | Hallways, kids' rooms, family rooms | More washable than eggshell, shows imperfections slightly more. Good for high-traffic areas. |
| Semi-Gloss | Kitchens, bathrooms, trim, doors | Moisture-resistant, very washable. Highlights every wall imperfection, so prep must be perfect. |
| High-Gloss | Trim, doors, cabinets (rarely walls) | Maximum durability and washability. Mirror-like finish amplifies any surface flaw. |

The general rule: higher sheen means more durable and easier to clean, but less forgiving of surface imperfections. Most homes use eggshell on walls and semi-gloss on trim.

### Paint Quality and VOCs

Good paint costs $40 to $70 per gallon. Budget paint costs $20 to $30. The difference is measurable:

- **Coverage:** Premium paint covers in two coats over most colors. Budget paint may need three.
- **Durability:** Premium paint resists scuffing, staining, and fading significantly better.
- **Color accuracy:** Premium lines have better pigment loads, meaning the dried color matches the swatch more accurately.
- **Low-VOC formulations:** Modern premium paints emit far less volatile organic compounds than older or cheaper formulations. The [EPA provides guidance on indoor air quality and VOCs](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality) that is worth reviewing, especially if household members have respiratory sensitivity.

For a 12x12 room with 8-foot ceilings (roughly 380 square feet of wall space), you will need 2 to 3 gallons for two coats. At $50 per gallon, that is $100 to $150 — a small fraction of the total project cost, and the wrong place to economize.

## Hiring a Painting Contractor: What to Evaluate

### Getting Quotes

Get at least three written quotes. A thorough quote includes:

- Square footage or room-by-room pricing
- Number of coats specified
- Paint brand and product line (ask if not listed)
- What prep work is included and what costs extra
- Timeline for completion
- Whether the contractor moves and covers furniture or expects you to handle it

**Red flags:** Phone-only quotes without a site visit, quotes that just list a lump sum with no detail, and quotes significantly below the others (they are cutting corners on prep, paint quality, or both).

### Licensing and Insurance

In most states, painting contractors need a general contractor's license or a specialty painting license. Always verify:

- **General liability insurance:** Covers damage to your property. Ask for a certificate of insurance, not just a verbal confirmation.
- **Workers' compensation:** If a painter is injured in your home and the contractor does not carry workers' comp, you could be liable.
- **Lead paint certification:** Any work on homes built before 1978 must follow the [EPA's Renovation, Repair, and Painting (RRP) rule](https://www.epa.gov/lead). Certified contractors follow specific containment and cleanup procedures to protect your family from lead dust. This is not optional — it is federal law.

### What Good Contractors Do Differently

- They walk through every room with you before quoting and point out issues (cracked plaster, water stains, wallpaper seams) that will affect the job
- They specify the exact paint products they will use
- They provide a written timeline with start and completion dates
- They do a final walkthrough with you at the end, touching up any spots you identify
- They leave the leftover paint labeled by room for future touch-ups

## The DIY Alternative: Realistic Expectations

Painting your own interior can save 60 to 70 percent of the cost of hiring out. For that same 1,500-square-foot home, you are looking at $400 to $1,000 in materials (paint, primer, tape, brushes, rollers, drop cloths) versus $1,500 to $4,500 installed.

The cost is time. A professional crew of two paints a room in 4 to 6 hours. A homeowner doing the same room — including all prep, two coats, and cleanup — should budget 8 to 12 hours. A whole house takes a pro crew a week. It takes most homeowners 3 to 6 weekends.

### Where DIYers Get the Best Value

**Bedrooms and living rooms with smooth walls in good condition.** Low ceilings, simple geometry, eggshell finish. This is straightforward work where the learning curve is short and the results are immediately gratifying.

### Where DIYers Struggle

**Stairwells.** The height, awkward angles, and safety considerations make stairwell painting genuinely difficult. Extension poles only go so far, and ladder work on stairs is dangerous without specialized articulating ladders.

**Trim cutting-in.** Getting a clean, sharp line where the wall color meets the trim color requires a steady hand and a good-quality angled brush. It takes practice. Blue tape helps but does not guarantee crisp lines (paint bleeds under tape edges). The pro technique is a loaded 2.5-inch angled brush, held at 45 degrees, with one smooth stroke. It takes maybe 50 linear feet of practice to get decent at this.

**Ceilings.** Rolling a ceiling is physically exhausting and the flat-sheen paint shows every lap mark if your technique is inconsistent. The key: maintain a wet edge, roll in one direction, and work in manageable sections. Swap your roller cover for every room — dried paint on the nap creates streaks.

## Lead Paint: The Non-Negotiable Safety Issue

If your home was built before 1978, there may be lead paint somewhere in the house — on trim, windowsills, doors, and walls. Sanding or scraping lead paint creates toxic dust that is especially dangerous for children and pregnant women.

The [EPA's lead safety guidelines](https://www.epa.gov/lead) require specific containment procedures when disturbing lead paint. At minimum: plastic sheeting on the floor, wet-sanding or chemical strippers (not dry sanding), HEPA vacuuming after the work, and proper disposal of debris.

Professional painters working on pre-1978 homes must be EPA Lead-Safe Certified. If a painting contractor does not ask about the age of your home, that is a significant red flag. For workplace safety standards that extend to work-at-height situations like painting, [OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/) provides additional guidance on ladder and scaffold safety.

## The Final Walkthrough

Whether you hired a crew or did it yourself, inspect the work with a critical eye before calling it done. Use a bright flashlight held at a raking angle to check for:

- Lap marks on walls and ceilings
- Drips or runs on trim and doors
- Bleed-through of old stains
- Missed spots behind doors, above closet door frames, and at the top of walls near the ceiling
- Clean, straight cut lines between wall and trim colors

Touch-ups are inevitable. Keep the leftover paint sealed tightly — it stays usable for years if stored properly (indoors, away from freezing temperatures). Label each can with the room and the date. Future-you will be grateful.
