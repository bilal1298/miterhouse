Bathroom tile installation costs $7–$25 per square foot installed. A 50–80 sq ft ceramic floor comes in at $900–$2,200 total; a fully tiled porcelain shower enclosure runs $1,500–$4,500. Those numbers don't mean much until you understand what moves them — so let's get into it.

## The Tile You Pick Sets the Floor on Everything

Your tile choice doesn't just set the material cost — it also drives labor, because harder materials are slower to cut and install.

| Tile Type | Material Cost | Installed Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Ceramic | $0.50–$3.00/sq ft | $7–$12/sq ft |
| Porcelain | $1.50–$7.00/sq ft | $9–$20/sq ft |
| Natural Stone (marble, travertine) | $5–$20+/sq ft | $15–$35/sq ft |
| Glass Mosaic | $7–$30/sq ft | $18–$40/sq ft |
| Cement/Encaustic | $3–$12/sq ft | $11–$22/sq ft |

**Ceramic** is the right call for most bathroom floors if you're not trying to impress anyone. It's soft, cuts fast, and a competent installer moves through it quickly — that's good for your labor bill. Floor & Decor's ceramic selection starts around $0.79/sq ft and the quality is perfectly fine for a guest bath or a rental. You do need to seal it in wet areas.

**Porcelain** is what I'd use in my own bathroom. It's denser than ceramic, nearly impervious to water without sealing, and doesn't punish you as hard if grout lines fail. Daltile and MSI both make porcelain tile that's well-calibrated and consistent from tile to tile — that matters when you're setting 1/16" joints. The downside is that porcelain fights back when you cut it, which slows installation and adds a bit to labor. Large-format porcelain (24"×24" and up) needs a skilled installer who knows how to achieve full back-coverage — otherwise you get hollow spots and cracked tiles.

**Natural stone** — marble, travertine, slate, limestone — looks genuinely beautiful and holds its value. It's also unforgiving. Stone is porous, needs sealing immediately on install and again annually, and requires a substrate that's dead-flat. If a marble tile cracks because the subfloor had a soft spot, you're either sourcing a matching replacement from the same dye lot or living with a mismatched patch. Expect to pay a premium for installers who know what they're doing with stone.

**Glass mosaic** is the most expensive category to install because the small tile format (often 1"×1" or 2"×2" on mesh sheets) means every cut is a production. Use it as an accent wall or shower niche detail — not as a field tile unless you've got real budget room. Floor & Decor carries decent glass mosaic starting around $8/sq ft; specialty tile shops will run $15–$30+ for the interesting stuff.

## Floor, Wall, or Shower — Three Different Jobs

These surfaces look the same but carry completely different technical requirements and price tags.

### Bathroom Floor Tile

Standard floor tile installation runs $7–$15/sq ft installed for ceramic or standard porcelain. The substrate is whatever's under your current floor — concrete slab, or a wood subfloor with cement board on top.

If your subfloor flexes or isn't level within 1/8" over 10 feet, the installer needs to float a mortar bed or add cement board before any tile goes down. Budget $2–$4/sq ft extra for that. Don't skip it — tile telegraphs every imperfection in the substrate, and grout cracks out of joints that flex.

One thing to confirm with your installer: floor tile needs a PEI (Porcelain Enamel Institute) wear rating of at least 3 for residential bathrooms. A wall tile rated PEI 0–2 on a bathroom floor will wear through the glaze. If an installer proposes using wall tile on your floor to save money, that's your cue to ask more questions.

### Bathroom Wall Tile

Wall tile runs $9–$18/sq ft installed. The substrate is usually cement board screwed to studs, with seams taped and thinset-bedded. Labor is slower than floor work because of all the obstacles — outlet boxes, towel bar blocking, window sills, soap dishes. Every inside corner, outside corner, and edge needs a clean finish, whether that's a bullnose tile, a Schluter metal trim strip, or a mitered cut.

### Shower Tile

Shower tile is the most expensive surface to install, at $12–$25+/sq ft, and it deserves that premium. A shower gets fully saturated daily. The waterproofing membrane — not the tile — is what keeps water out of your wall cavity. A proper shower installation goes in this order:

1. Pre-sloped shower pan (mortar float or prefab)
2. Waterproofing membrane (Schluter KERDI, RedGard, or Wedi) over the entire interior surface
3. Cement board or tile backer on walls
4. Tile set in modified thinset
5. Epoxy grout (standard grout in a shower is a slow-motion mistake)

Skip or rush any of those steps and you'll have mold or structural damage within a few years — and a full shower demo is a $3,000–$8,000 problem. When you're getting quotes on shower tile work, ask each installer to walk you through their waterproofing process specifically. Anyone who gets vague or defensive isn't someone you want waterproofing your shower.

## What Labor Actually Costs by Region

Labor is 40–60% of your total tile installation bill. National averages run $4–$9/sq ft for labor alone, but the spread by region is wide:

- **High cost of living metros** (NYC, SF, LA, Boston, Seattle): $8–$14/sq ft labor only
- **Mid-tier cities** (Chicago, Denver, Atlanta, Dallas, Phoenix): $5–$9/sq ft
- **Lower cost regions** (Midwest, rural Southeast): $4–$7/sq ft

Pattern layouts add to labor everywhere. A diagonal layout costs 15–20% more in labor because of the increased cuts and waste. Herringbone adds 25–35%. A shower with a built-in niche, bench seat, or curved walls can double the labor time for those sections — not the whole job, but those specific elements.

One thing that catches homeowners off guard: most tile installers have a minimum charge of $300–$500 regardless of square footage. For a very small bathroom — say a 35 sq ft powder room — that minimum dominates the math and makes the per-square-foot cost look absurd.

## The Materials People Forget to Budget For

Here's where estimates go off the rails: tile gets quoted, and everything else gets forgotten. A complete tile installation requires a lot more than tile.

**Cement board / tile backer:** $0.50–$1.25/sq ft material. Required under floor tile on wood subfloors and on all shower walls. Needs to be screwed to studs, seams taped with alkali-resistant mesh tape, and thinset-bedded at joints.

**Thinset mortar:** $0.25–$0.75/sq ft. Use modified thinset for porcelain and all natural stone — unmodified thinset doesn't bond reliably to dense materials. Large-format tiles need a medium-bed mortar to avoid hollow spots.

**Grout:** $0.20–$0.50/sq ft for standard; $1–$2/sq ft for epoxy grout. Unsanded grout for joints under 1/8", sanded for wider joints. Epoxy grout in a shower is worth every extra cent — it resists staining, won't crack out of joints, and doesn't need sealing. Daltile's TruGrout and Mapei's Kerapoxy are solid options.

**Waterproofing membrane:** $1–$3/sq ft material, $1–$2/sq ft to apply. Non-negotiable in any shower. Worth adding around the toilet base and bath surround too.

**Tile sealer:** $0.15–$0.30/sq ft. Required for natural stone and unglazed ceramic on installation. Grout sealer on shower grout lines is a smart move even if you used epoxy grout everywhere else.

**Trim pieces and transitions:** Schluter strips, bullnose tile, and threshold transitions run $5–$25/linear foot depending on material and profile. A standard 36" doorway transition strip from tile to hardwood runs $30–$80.

**Waste factor:** Buy 10% extra tile for cuts and breakage. Go 15% for pattern layouts, natural stone (which varies in size), or any room with lots of obstacles. Running short and reordering risks a dye-lot mismatch — even the same SKU from Floor & Decor can shift slightly between production runs.

## Honest Take on DIY Tile Work

On a basic rectangular bathroom floor with 12"×12" or larger tiles, an experienced DIYer can get genuinely good results. The wet saw rental runs $40–$80/day. Plan for the job to take 2–3x longer than a pro. [DIY Bathroom Remodel Cost](/blog/diy-bathroom-remodel-cost/) has a full breakdown of what's worth tackling yourself versus hiring out.

Where DIY gets expensive fast:

**Shower waterproofing.** The membrane application isn't rocket science, but corners, seams, and drain flashings need to be done right. Schluter's official KERDI installation videos are excellent — watch them all the way through before touching anything. A pinhole failure in the membrane shows up as mold and rot 2–4 years later, at which point you're demo-ing the whole shower.

**Substrate prep.** This is the step pros spend the most time on and DIYers skip fastest. If the subfloor flexes under foot traffic, any tile on it will crack. If it's out of level more than 1/8" over 10 feet, your grout lines will show it. Fix the substrate before the tile goes down, not after.

**Large-format tile.** 18"×18" and larger requires back-buttering every tile and achieving close to 100% mortar coverage. Hollow spots beneath large tiles crack under point loads — a chair leg, a dropped shampoo bottle, someone stepping wrong.

My recommendation: for a basic floor with no tricky angles, DIY is a reasonable bet. For a full shower surround, consider hiring a pro for the waterproofing and backer board work, then tiling yourself once the substrate is done. You keep the critical failure points in professional hands and still save half the labor cost.
