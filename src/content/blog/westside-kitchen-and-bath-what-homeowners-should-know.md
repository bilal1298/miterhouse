Kitchen and bath renovations are the two highest-impact home improvements you can make, both for daily livability and resale value. A combined kitchen and bathroom remodel runs $15,000 to $50,000 for most homeowners, depending on whether you are refreshing surfaces or gutting to the studs. The kitchen alone accounts for 60 to 70 percent of that budget because it involves more cabinetry, countertop space, and appliances.

Whether you are working with a local showroom or managing the project yourself, this guide covers what drives costs, where to prioritize your budget, and the sequencing that keeps a dual renovation from turning into chaos.

## Planning a Kitchen and Bath Together

Renovating both rooms simultaneously has advantages if you manage it right. You get volume pricing on labor (contractors prefer larger jobs), coordinate trades efficiently (the plumber is already on site), and compress the overall disruption window into one painful period instead of two.

The flip side: you lose both your kitchen and a bathroom at the same time. For families, this means eating takeout and sharing one bathroom for 4 to 8 weeks. Plan for that reality before committing to a simultaneous timeline.

**Sequencing matters.** The optimal approach is to start the bathroom first. It is smaller, finishes faster, and getting one functional bathroom back online reduces household stress. Then overlap kitchen demolition with the final stages of bathroom finishing.

## Kitchen Renovation: Where the Budget Goes

A mid-range kitchen remodel ($15,000-$35,000) for a standard 10x12 kitchen breaks down roughly as follows:

| Category | % of Kitchen Budget | Cost Range |
|---|---|---|
| Cabinets (semi-custom) | 30-35% | $5,000-$12,000 |
| Countertops | 10-15% | $2,000-$5,000 |
| Appliances | 12-18% | $2,500-$6,000 |
| Labor (all trades) | 20-25% | $3,500-$8,000 |
| Flooring | 5-8% | $1,000-$2,500 |
| Plumbing + electrical | 5-8% | $1,000-$2,500 |
| Backsplash | 3-5% | $500-$1,500 |
| Lighting | 2-4% | $400-$1,200 |
| Permits | 1-2% | $200-$600 |

Cabinets dominate the budget. Stock cabinets from home centers ($80-$200 per linear foot installed) save thousands over semi-custom ($150-$400/LF) and custom ($500-$1,200/LF). For most kitchens, stock cabinets with soft-close hinges and full-extension drawers are perfectly functional and look good.

If the existing cabinet boxes are solid, refacing (new doors and drawer fronts) or even just [painting the cabinets](/blog/cabinet-painting-pricing-guide-for-homeowners/) saves 50 to 75 percent of the cabinet budget.

## Bathroom Renovation: Where the Budget Goes

A mid-range bathroom remodel ($5,000-$15,000) for a standard full bath follows this split:

| Category | % of Bath Budget | Cost Range |
|---|---|---|
| Labor (all trades) | 40-50% | $2,500-$7,500 |
| Tile (floor + shower) | 15-20% | $750-$3,000 |
| Vanity + countertop | 10-15% | $500-$2,000 |
| Shower/tub fixtures | 8-12% | $400-$1,800 |
| Toilet | 3-5% | $200-$500 |
| Plumbing connections | 5-8% | $300-$1,000 |
| Electrical + ventilation | 3-5% | $200-$600 |

The labor percentage is higher in bathrooms because the room is smaller and the work is more intricate — tile work, waterproofing, and plumbing connections all require skilled trades working in tight quarters.

## Choosing Materials That Work in Both Rooms

When you renovate a kitchen and bathroom together, you can create a cohesive look while buying in bulk:

**Flooring.** Using the same tile or LVP through both rooms (and potentially the connecting hallway) creates visual flow and simplifies the material order. A single flooring order also gets you past minimum quantity thresholds for better pricing.

**Fixtures.** Matching faucet finishes (brushed nickel, matte black, etc.) across both rooms creates design consistency without requiring identical fixtures. Buy all faucets and hardware at once to ensure finish-lot matching — the same "brushed nickel" from two different batches can look noticeably different.

**Countertops.** If you are using quartz or granite, ordering kitchen and bathroom countertops from the same slab saves fabrication setup costs and guarantees color matching. A single slab can often yield kitchen countertops plus a bath vanity top.

## Hiring for a Combined Project

A general contractor who handles both rooms is typically more efficient than hiring separate specialists. They coordinate the plumber, electrician, tile installer, and other subs across both rooms and manage the schedule.

When evaluating contractors for a kitchen and bath project:

- Ask specifically about their experience with simultaneous multi-room renovations
- Request a detailed schedule showing how the two rooms overlap
- Clarify which bathroom (if you have more than one) remains usable throughout
- Get the payment schedule tied to project milestones, not calendar dates

If the project is large enough, look into the [International Residential Code requirements](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) for your area. Kitchen and bath renovations often trigger code upgrades for electrical (GFCI outlets, dedicated circuits for appliances), ventilation, and plumbing that were not required when the home was originally built.

## The Renovation Sequence

A well-planned combined renovation follows this general order:

**Weeks 1-2: Demolition and rough-in**
- Demo both rooms (or stagger bathroom demo in week 1, kitchen in week 2)
- Inspect for hidden damage — water rot, mold, outdated wiring
- Plumber runs new supply and drain lines for both rooms
- Electrician runs new circuits, adds GFCI outlets, rough-in for lighting

**Weeks 2-3: Substrate and waterproofing**
- Bathroom cement board and waterproof membrane installation
- Kitchen subfloor repair or leveling
- Both rooms get insulation if exterior walls are open

**Weeks 3-4: Major installations**
- Bathroom tile (shower walls, floor)
- Kitchen cabinets
- Both rooms get rough plumbing connections

**Weeks 4-5: Finish work**
- Countertops fabricated and installed (kitchen + bath vanity)
- Kitchen backsplash
- Bathroom fixtures (toilet, vanity faucet, shower trim)
- Kitchen appliance installation
- Painting in both rooms

**Week 5-6: Punch list and completion**
- Hardware installation (handles, pulls, towel bars)
- Final plumbing and electrical connections
- Caulking and touch-up
- Final cleaning

This 5-6 week timeline assumes a professional crew working full days. Add 2-3 weeks if the project uncovers significant hidden issues, which happens in roughly 30% of kitchen and bath renovations on homes older than 30 years.

## Where to Save Without Regret

Having done this type of project, here are the savings that actually work:

**Do your own demolition.** Between two rooms, DIY demo saves $1,000 to $2,500 in labor. It is satisfying, requires no special skill, and only requires caution around plumbing and electrical connections. Rent a dumpster ($300-$500 for a week) rather than making countless trips to the dump.

**Keep plumbing in place.** Moving a sink, toilet, or shower by even three feet can add $1,000 to $3,000 in plumbing costs. If the current layout works functionally, keep it.

**Choose stock cabinets with upgrades.** Base-model stock cabinets with added soft-close hinges and full-extension drawer slides give you 90% of the semi-custom experience at 40% of the price.

**Source your own fixtures.** Buying faucets, lighting, and hardware yourself (rather than through the contractor's markup) saves 15-25%. Just coordinate timing so materials are on-site when the installer needs them.

**Time the purchase for sales.** Kitchen appliance packages during holiday weekends (Memorial Day, Black Friday, Labor Day) regularly offer 20-30% savings or significant rebates.

## Return on Investment

Kitchen and bath renovations consistently rank among the highest-ROI projects. A mid-range kitchen remodel typically recoups 60 to 80 percent of its cost at resale, and a bathroom remodel returns 55 to 70 percent. The daily quality-of-life improvement during the years you live with the upgrade is the real payoff.

Our [master bathroom remodel cost analysis](/blog/master-bathroom-remodel-cost/) breaks down the financial case for bathroom renovations specifically, including regional variations in ROI.

The real financial risk in kitchen and bath projects is scope creep — starting with a surface refresh and gradually escalating to a gut renovation as you discover problems or fall in love with upgrade options. Set your budget ceiling before demolition starts, build in that 10-15% contingency, and resist the urge to make changes once work is underway. Every mid-project change adds cost, delays the timeline, and increases the chance of errors.
