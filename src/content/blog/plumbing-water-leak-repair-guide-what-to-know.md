A water leak in your plumbing system can cost anywhere from $5 to fix (a worn faucet washer) to $10,000 or more (a slab leak requiring jackhammering and repiping). The key to keeping costs down is finding the leak fast, understanding what is causing it, and knowing which repairs you can handle versus which ones need a licensed plumber.

Here is the practical breakdown.

## Finding the Leak: Where to Look First

Before you can fix anything, you need to figure out where the water is actually coming from. Water travels — a stain on a ceiling might originate 10 feet away from where you see it. Start systematic.

### Check Your Water Meter

Turn off every water-using fixture and appliance in the house. Go to your water meter and watch the flow indicator (the small triangle or dial on the face). If it is moving with everything off, you have an active leak somewhere in the supply system between the meter and your fixtures.

Write down the meter reading, wait two hours without using any water, and read it again. If the number changed, the leak is confirmed.

### Inspect Common Leak Locations

**Under sinks:** Look at supply line connections, the P-trap, the garbage disposal connection, and the dishwasher drain hose. Feel the connections with a dry hand — even small drips leave moisture you can detect by touch.

**Toilets:** Drop food coloring into the tank. Wait 15 minutes without flushing. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking. This silent leak can waste 200 gallons per day and is a $10 fix.

**Water heater:** Check the pressure relief valve, drain valve, and all connections at the top. Corrosion around fittings or water pooling at the base indicates a problem. Our [water heater leak guide](/blog/the-real-deal-on-water-leaking-from-ac-vent-in-ceiling/) covers diagnosis in detail.

**Behind walls:** Listen for running water when nothing is on. Look for unexplained damp spots, bubbling paint, or warped baseboards. A thermal imaging camera (rentable from most tool rental shops for $50 to $75 per day) can reveal temperature differences behind drywall that indicate hidden moisture.

**In the yard:** Soft, muddy areas when it has not rained, or patches of unusually green grass, can signal an underground supply line leak.

## Common Types of Water Leaks and Their Costs

### Supply Line Leaks

Supply lines are under pressure (40 to 80 PSI in most homes), so even a small crack or loose fitting leaks constantly. These are the leaks that run up your water bill and cause the most damage per hour.

- **Compression fitting leak:** Tighten the nut a quarter turn. Free fix, takes 30 seconds.
- **Braided supply hose failure:** Replace the hose for $8 to $15. Ten-minute DIY job.
- **Copper pipe pinhole leak:** Temporary patch with a pipe repair clamp ($8) or permanent fix by sweating a new fitting ($20 in materials, requires soldering skill). A plumber charges $150 to $400.
- **Hidden supply line leak in a wall:** $200 to $1,000+ for a plumber depending on accessibility and damage.

### Drain Leaks

Drain leaks are gravity-fed, so they only leak when water is flowing through them. They are less urgent than supply leaks but can still cause mold and structural damage over time.

- **P-trap leak:** Tighten slip-nut connections by hand or replace the P-trap for $10 to $20.
- **Cracked drain pipe:** Replace the damaged section. PVC drain pipe is cheap — $5 to $15 in materials. Labor adds $150 to $300 if you call a plumber.
- **Main drain line leak:** This typically requires professional repair. Costs range from $500 for an accessible section to several thousand for a [sewer line repair](/blog/plumbing-drain-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/).

### Slab Leaks

A slab leak occurs in the water lines running through or beneath your concrete foundation. Signs include warm spots on the floor, the sound of running water when everything is off, and unexpectedly high water bills.

Slab leak repair costs $2,000 to $6,000 depending on the approach — direct access through the slab, tunneling underneath, or rerouting the line entirely. This is not a DIY project under any circumstances.

## DIY Leak Repairs You Can Handle Today

### Fixing a Dripping Faucet

Most faucet drips come from worn internal components — cartridges, O-rings, or valve seats.

1. Turn off the water supply valves under the sink
2. Remove the faucet handle (usually a hidden screw under a decorative cap)
3. Pull the cartridge or valve stem
4. Take it to the hardware store and match the replacement part
5. Install the new component, reassemble, turn on water, and test

Total cost: $5 to $25 in parts. Time: 30 to 60 minutes including the hardware store trip.

### Replacing a Toilet Fill Valve or Flapper

A running toilet wastes water and money. Replacement kits cost $10 to $25 and install in 15 minutes.

1. Turn off the supply valve behind the toilet
2. Flush to empty the tank
3. Disconnect the supply line from the bottom of the tank
4. Remove the old fill valve (unscrew the nut underneath the tank)
5. Install the new valve per the kit instructions
6. Replace the flapper while you are in there — they cost $5 and take two minutes
7. Reconnect, turn on water, adjust the float height

### Patching a Pinhole Leak in Copper

For an emergency fix while you wait for a plumber (or as a permanent fix if you are comfortable with it):

**Temporary:** Pipe repair clamp. Wrap the rubber gasket around the pipe over the leak, position the clamp, and tighten the bolts. Takes five minutes and holds indefinitely for pinhole leaks.

**Permanent:** Cut out the damaged section and sweat in a new coupling. This requires a pipe cutter, flux, solder, and a propane torch. If you have never soldered copper before, SharkBite push-fit couplings are a code-approved alternative that requires zero soldering — just cut, deburr, and push on.

## When to Call a Professional

Some leaks are strictly professional territory:

- **Slab leaks** — require specialized detection equipment and repair techniques
- **Main water line leaks** — from the meter to the house, often requiring permits
- **Gas water heater connections** — any repair near gas lines needs a licensed plumber
- **Leaks inside finished walls** — accessing and repairing without excessive demolition takes experience
- **Whole-house repiping** — if you have galvanized pipes with multiple leaks, spot repairs are band-aids

When hiring, follow the guidance in our [plumbing company guide](/blog/plumbing-repair-diy-or-call-a-pro/) for vetting contractors. For emergency situations, see our [24-hour plumbing resource](/blog/how-to-install-xwfe-water-filter-the-right-way/).

Always follow [OSHA safety practices](https://www.osha.gov/) when working around plumbing, especially in confined spaces or near electrical equipment. The [NFPA 70](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70) standard is relevant whenever plumbing work is near electrical wiring.

## Preventing Future Leaks

Prevention is cheaper than repair every time:

**Inspect supply hoses annually.** The braided stainless steel supply lines under sinks, behind toilets, and to your washing machine have a finite lifespan. Replace them every 8 to 10 years, or immediately if you see corrosion, bulging, or cracking on the outer braid.

**Monitor your water pressure.** Pressure above 80 PSI accelerates wear on fittings, valves, and appliances. A pressure gauge costs $10 and screws onto any hose bib. If your pressure exceeds 80 PSI, install a pressure-reducing valve — about $200 to $400 installed.

**Know your shutoffs.** Every adult in your household should know where the main water shutoff is and how to operate it. In an emergency, shutting off the water in 30 seconds versus 10 minutes can be the difference between a wet floor and a destroyed ceiling.

**Address hard water.** If your water is hard (above 120 mg/L of dissolved minerals), mineral buildup accelerates pipe deterioration and valve failure. A whole-house water softener costs $1,000 to $3,000 installed and extends the life of your entire plumbing system. The [EPA WaterSense program](https://www.ul.com/) provides guidelines on water quality that are worth reviewing.

**Insulate exposed pipes.** Pipes in unheated spaces — garages, crawl spaces, exterior walls — are freeze risks. Foam pipe insulation costs under $1 per linear foot and takes minutes to install. A single frozen and burst pipe can cause $5,000 to $50,000 in water damage.

## Understanding Your Insurance Coverage

Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden, accidental water damage — a burst pipe, a failed supply line, or an appliance malfunction. It generally does **not** cover:

- Gradual leaks you knew about or should have noticed
- Damage from deferred maintenance
- Sewer backups (unless you have a specific rider)
- Flood damage from external sources

Document everything with photos before you touch anything. Call your insurance company before starting repairs if you think the damage might exceed your deductible. The adjuster needs to see the damage, or at least quality photos of it, to process a claim.

## The Bottom Line

Most residential water leaks are either quick DIY fixes or straightforward professional repairs. The expensive ones are the ones you ignore — a slow drip behind a wall that goes unnoticed for months will cause more damage than a dramatic burst pipe you catch immediately.

Check your water meter periodically. Inspect under sinks and around toilets during your regular cleaning. Replace aging supply lines before they fail. These simple habits catch leaks early, when they are cheap to fix.
