The installed cost of a quartz countertop runs $55 to $150 per square foot when you add up the slab, fabrication, and professional installation. For a kitchen with 40 to 50 square feet of counter space, that means $2,750 to $7,500 before plumbing work or old countertop removal. The sweet spot for most homeowners is a mid-range quartz at $75 to $100 per square foot installed, landing in the $3,500 to $5,000 total range for an average kitchen.

Those numbers hold true across most US metro areas in 2026, though coastal cities and low-competition rural markets tend to sit 10 to 15% above the national median.

## What "Installed" Actually Includes

When a fabricator quotes you an "installed" price, it should cover these items. If it does not, the quote is incomplete.

**Template measurement:** A technician visits your home and creates precise measurements of your countertop layout, marking sink placement, seam locations, and edge returns. This visit typically takes 30 to 60 minutes.

**Slab material:** The quartz itself, cut from a full-size slab to your dimensions. You are paying for the square footage you use plus a waste factor (typically 10 to 15%).

**Fabrication:** CNC cutting to your template, edge polishing, cutout creation (sink, cooktop, faucet holes), and quality control. This is precision work.

**Delivery and installation:** Transporting the finished pieces to your home, setting them on adhesive, leveling, seaming, mounting the undermount sink (if applicable), and applying caulk.

**Initial cleanup:** Removing packaging and debris from the installation.

What is almost never included in the "installed" price:
- Removal and disposal of old countertops ($150–$500)
- Plumbing disconnect and reconnect ($150–$400)
- Cabinet repair or leveling ($100–$400)
- Backsplash work ($200–$2,000)
- Electrical modifications ($200–$500)

Always ask for a line-item quote rather than a lump sum. It protects you from hidden charges and makes comparing fabricators straightforward.

## Regional Price Differences

Geography affects quartz countertop pricing more than most homeowners expect. Here is what drives the regional variation:

**Northeast (Boston, NYC, Philadelphia):** $70–$160/sq ft installed. Higher labor rates, expensive commercial rents for fabrication shops, and cold-weather delivery challenges push prices up. However, competition in dense metro areas can offset some of this.

**Southeast (Atlanta, Charlotte, Tampa):** $55–$130/sq ft installed. Moderate labor rates and a high volume of new construction keep the fabricator market competitive.

**Midwest (Chicago, Minneapolis, Columbus):** $55–$125/sq ft installed. Similar dynamics to the Southeast. Cold winters can slow installation schedules but do not significantly affect pricing.

**West Coast (LA, Seattle, Portland):** $65–$150/sq ft installed. High labor costs but also high fabricator density in metro areas. Competition holds prices somewhat in check.

**Mountain/Rural areas:** $60–$140/sq ft installed. Fewer fabricators means less competition, and delivery costs from distant distribution centers add up. If you live 100+ miles from the nearest metro, factor in a potential $200 to $500 delivery surcharge.

## Quartz Thickness: 2cm vs. 3cm

Most residential quartz countertops are fabricated in 3cm (roughly 1.25 inches) thickness, but 2cm (roughly 3/4 inch) slabs are available at a lower cost. Here is the trade-off:

**3cm quartz:**
- Standard for kitchens
- Self-supporting on standard cabinet spans up to 24 inches
- No subtop (plywood underlayment) required in most cases
- Substantial, solid feel
- Most edge profile options available

**2cm quartz:**
- Typically $5–$15/sq ft less expensive
- Requires a plywood subtop for structural support, adding $3–$5/sq ft back
- Suitable for bathroom vanities, backsplashes, and light-use surfaces
- Thinner look that some find more contemporary
- Fewer edge profile options

For kitchens, 3cm is the standard and the practical choice. The material savings on 2cm mostly disappear once you add the required plywood subtop, and you lose the satisfying heft that makes quartz feel premium.

For bathroom vanities, 2cm is perfectly adequate and keeps the project cost lower where the thinner profile is not a visual concern.

## Sink Options and Their Cost Impact

Your sink choice has a surprising impact on the total countertop cost.

**Undermount sink (most popular with quartz):** The sink mounts from below, creating a clean edge where countertop meets sink. Cutout cost: $100 to $300. This is the standard choice because it shows off the quartz edge and makes cleanup easier — you can wipe crumbs directly into the sink.

**Drop-in/top-mount sink:** Sits on top of the countertop with a visible rim. The cutout is simpler and often $50 to $100 cheaper. Drop-in sinks hide the quartz edge at the cutout, which some homeowners consider a waste of a premium surface.

**Farmhouse/apron-front sink:** Requires a custom cutout and specific cabinet modifications. The cutout cost is typically $200 to $400 due to the tight tolerances needed. The front of the sink is exposed, so the quartz must be cut precisely to meet flush against it. Budget an extra $300 to $600 overall for a farmhouse sink versus a standard undermount.

**Integrated quartz sink:** Some fabricators can create a sink basin from the same quartz as the countertop. This is premium territory — $800 to $2,000 for the integrated sink — but it creates a seamless, striking look with zero gaps for bacteria or water to collect.

## Avoiding Fabricator Red Flags

Not all quartz fabricators deliver the same quality. Here is what to watch for when vetting candidates.

**Red flag: No slab yard visit.** A reputable fabricator encourages you to visit and pick your actual slab (or at minimum view a large-format sample). If they insist on selling from small chips only, they may be ordering whatever is cheapest at the time of your project.

**Red flag: Vague quotes.** "Around $4,000" is not a quote. You need line items: slab cost per square foot, fabrication charges, each cutout priced individually, edge profile specification, and installation labor. Vague quotes always grow.

**Red flag: No references or portfolio.** Any fabricator with more than a year of experience should be able to show you completed kitchens and provide homeowner references. Check online reviews, but also ask for direct contact with recent customers.

**Red flag: Demanding full payment upfront.** Standard payment structure is 40 to 50% deposit at contract signing and the balance upon completion. A fabricator asking for 100% upfront has either cash flow problems or is positioned to disappear.

**Green flag: They ask detailed questions.** Good fabricators want to know about your cabinet condition, existing plumbing layout, any access challenges (narrow hallways, tight turns), and your timeline expectations. The more they ask, the fewer surprises on installation day.

If your quartz project is part of a [broader kitchen renovation](/blog/the-real-deal-on-kitchen-renovation-services/), make sure your general contractor and the countertop fabricator communicate directly about scheduling. Countertop templating cannot happen until cabinets are set and leveled, and backsplash installation should wait until countertops are in place.

## DIY Quartz Installation: The Honest Truth

I am not going to pretend this is viable for most homeowners. Quartz slabs weigh 15 to 20 pounds per square foot — a 4-foot section weighs 150 to 200 pounds. You need a team, proper equipment, and experience with precision placement. Cutting quartz generates dangerous silica dust and requires specialized wet-cutting equipment.

What you can do yourself to save money:
- Remove old countertops ($150–$500 savings)
- Disconnect simple plumbing (basic P-trap and supply line disconnection)
- Handle demolition debris and disposal
- Paint or prep the kitchen before the fabricator's template visit

These prep tasks save $300 to $800 without any risk to the quality of the final installation. For [lead safety](https://www.epa.gov/lead) during any demolition in pre-1978 homes, follow EPA guidelines for containment and cleanup.

Check that your planned countertop project meets local building codes. The [ICC residential code](https://www.tcnatile.com/) provides standards for structural requirements that your installation should satisfy.

## Timeline: From Decision to Done

Here is a realistic schedule for a quartz countertop installation:

- **Week 1:** Research, get quotes, visit slab yards
- **Week 2:** Select fabricator and slab, sign contract, pay deposit
- **Week 3:** Template visit at your home
- **Weeks 4-5:** Fabrication (7–14 business days)
- **Week 5-6:** Installation day (2–5 hours)

Total timeline: 4 to 6 weeks from contract to completed installation. Rush orders are possible at most fabricators for a 15 to 25% surcharge, cutting the timeline to 2 to 3 weeks.

Plan to be without a functioning kitchen sink for 24 to 48 hours around installation day. Set up a temporary dishwashing station and plan simple meals.

## Final Budget Recommendation

For a standard kitchen quartz countertop project in 2026:

- **Material + fabrication + installation:** $3,000–$6,000
- **Old countertop removal:** $150–$500
- **Plumbing:** $150–$400
- **Contingency (15%):** $500–$1,000
- **Realistic total:** $3,800–$7,900

The mid-range of that scale — around $5,000 to $6,000 total — gets most homeowners a beautiful, maintenance-free quartz countertop that will last 15 to 25 years and recoup the majority of its cost at resale. That is a solid investment by any measure.
