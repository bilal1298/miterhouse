When water is actively flooding your home — a burst pipe, a failed water heater, a backed-up sewer line — you have about 24 to 48 hours before moderate water damage becomes severe structural and mold damage. That window matters enormously. Acting fast within the first few hours limits the damage and reduces repair costs by thousands of dollars. Here is exactly what to do, what you can handle yourself, and when to call a professional.

## The First 30 Minutes: Stop the Water

Before anything else, stop the flow. Everything that follows depends on cutting off the water source.

**Burst pipe or failed supply line:** Turn off the main water shutoff valve. In most homes, it is in the basement near the front foundation wall, in a utility closet, or outside near the meter. Know where yours is before an emergency — looking for it while water is spraying is not the time.

**Water heater failure:** Close the cold water supply valve on top of the heater. If you cannot reach it safely (standing water near an electric heater is dangerous), shut off the main water supply. Also turn off the gas valve or the circuit breaker for the heater.

**Sewer backup:** Do not try to unblock a main sewer line yourself. This is Category 3 water (contaminated with sewage) and poses serious health risks. Stop using all drains and toilets in the house and call a plumber immediately.

**Roof leak during a storm:** You cannot stop the rain, but you can contain the water. Place buckets or bins under drip points and use towels or plastic sheeting to direct water away from electronics, furniture, and finished surfaces.

After stopping the source, turn off electricity to any affected areas at the breaker panel. Standing water and electricity are a lethal combination — do not wade through flooded areas if the power is still on. If you cannot safely reach the breaker panel, call your utility company.

## The First 2 Hours: Damage Assessment and Extraction

Once the water source is controlled and the power is off in affected areas, start removing standing water.

**Tools you need immediately:**
- A wet/dry shop vacuum (the single most useful tool for water emergencies)
- Mops, buckets, and old towels
- A fan or two for air circulation
- A dehumidifier if you have one

Extract as much standing water as possible. A shop vac moves water far faster than mopping. For significant flooding (more than an inch of standing water), a submersible pump rental ($50 to $100 per day from most rental centers) speeds things up dramatically.

While extracting water, assess the scope:
- How many rooms are affected?
- What type of flooring is wet (carpet, hardwood, tile, laminate)?
- How high did the water reach on the walls?
- Is the water clean (supply pipe) or contaminated (sewer, flood water)?

This assessment determines your next steps and whether you need professional help.

## What You Can Handle Yourself

Clean water damage (Category 1 — from a supply line, faucet, or ice maker) that affected a limited area is often manageable as a DIY project. Here is what that looks like:

**Removing wet materials.** Pull up soaked carpet and carpet pad. The pad is almost never salvageable — it absorbs water like a sponge and breeds mold within 48 hours. The carpet itself can sometimes be dried and saved if it is pulled up promptly and professionally cleaned, but budget for replacement. If water was on hardwood floors for less than a few hours, you may save them — more on that below.

**Cutting out wet drywall.** Drywall wicks moisture upward, so the wet zone extends higher than the visible water line. Cut the drywall out at least 12 inches above the visible water mark. Use a utility knife to score the line, then cut with a drywall saw. Remove baseboards first and save them if they are not warped.

**Drying.** This is the critical step. Run fans, dehumidifiers, and your HVAC system continuously. Open windows if outdoor humidity is lower than indoor humidity. The goal is to get wood framing, subfloor, and remaining drywall below 15 percent moisture content before closing anything up. A moisture meter ($25 to $40 at any hardware store) takes the guesswork out of this.

Drying typically takes 3 to 5 days for moderate damage. Do not rush this — closing up walls or installing new flooring over damp framing is a guaranteed mold problem.

## When to Call a Professional Immediately

Several situations require professional water damage restoration. Do not attempt these yourself:

**Contaminated water (Category 2 or 3).** Gray water from washing machines, dishwashers, or sump pump failures carries bacteria and chemicals. Black water from sewer backups or flooding contains sewage, pathogens, and potentially hazardous materials. Professional remediation companies have the equipment and PPE to handle these safely.

**Large-scale flooding.** If multiple rooms are significantly flooded, or water has been standing for more than 24 hours, professional extraction and drying equipment (commercial dehumidifiers, air movers, and moisture monitoring) makes the difference between a salvage job and a tear-out.

**Electrical system exposure.** If water reached electrical outlets, junction boxes, or your breaker panel, an electrician needs to inspect and certify the system before power is restored. Water in electrical components creates fire and electrocution hazards. The [National Fire Protection Association's NFPA 70 standard](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70) governs electrical safety in wet conditions.

**Structural concerns.** If water has been present long enough to soften subfloors, compromise joists, or cause visible sagging in ceilings, a structural assessment is necessary before any repair work begins.

## Cost Ranges for Emergency Water Damage

Repair costs vary enormously based on the category of water, the area affected, and how quickly you respond.

| Scenario | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Small area, clean water, caught quickly | $500–$1,500 (mostly DIY) |
| Single room, clean water, moderate damage | $1,000–$3,500 |
| Multiple rooms, clean water | $2,500–$7,500 |
| Any size, contaminated water | $3,000–$10,000+ |
| Significant structural damage | $5,000–$25,000+ |
| Full basement flood (professional restoration) | $7,500–$20,000+ |

Insurance coverage depends on the source. Burst pipes and sudden leaks are usually covered under homeowners insurance. Gradual leaks, deferred maintenance, and external flooding are typically not — flood insurance is a separate policy.

## The 48-Hour Mold Clock

Mold can begin growing on wet materials within 24 to 48 hours. Once established, mold remediation adds $500 to $5,000+ to your project costs and introduces health concerns.

To prevent mold:
- Begin drying immediately — every hour counts
- Remove wet porous materials (carpet pad, wet insulation, soaked drywall) within the first 24 hours
- Maintain airflow with fans and dehumidifiers running continuously
- Keep indoor humidity below 60 percent during the drying process
- Do not paint or seal over damp surfaces — this traps moisture and accelerates mold growth

If you see or smell mold (a musty, earthy odor) within the affected area, stop DIY work and consult a mold remediation specialist. Disturbing established mold colonies without proper containment spreads spores throughout the house.

## Documenting the Damage for Insurance

Before you start cleanup, document everything:
- Take photos and video of all affected areas, including the water source
- Measure the depth and extent of standing water
- Photograph damaged materials, contents, and structural elements
- Keep a written log of actions taken and their timing
- Save receipts for all emergency purchases (shop vac, dehumidifier, cleaning supplies)

Contact your insurance company within 24 hours. Most policies require prompt notification and have specific requirements about what steps the homeowner must take to mitigate further damage. Your policy likely requires you to make reasonable efforts to prevent additional damage — this is called the "duty to mitigate" and failing to act can reduce your claim.

## Recovery Timeline

Once the emergency is past and drying is complete, the repair timeline depends on the scope:

- **Minor damage (one room, clean water):** 1 to 2 weeks for drying, drywall repair, and painting
- **Moderate damage (multiple rooms):** 2 to 4 weeks including drying, demolition, and reconstruction
- **Major damage or contaminated water:** 4 to 8 weeks or longer, depending on structural repairs needed

If your [water heater was the source of the problem](/blog/is-it-normal-for-water-heater-to-leak-honest-guide/), address that issue before rebuilding. A replacement water heater should be installed on a drain pan with a connected drain line. Consider a [water heater leak detection system](/blog/water-heater-constantly-running-a-diyers-honest-take/) that shuts off the supply when moisture is detected.

For safety equipment standards during cleanup, the [UL (Underwriters Laboratories)](https://www.ul.com/) certifies the electrical safety of tools like shop vacs, dehumidifiers, and fans used in wet conditions. Using UL-certified equipment is not just a good idea — it may be required by your insurance company.

The honest truth about emergency water damage: speed matters more than perfection. Getting water out fast, drying thoroughly, and removing compromised materials within the first 48 hours will save you far more money and grief than any amount of careful restoration work done a week later.
