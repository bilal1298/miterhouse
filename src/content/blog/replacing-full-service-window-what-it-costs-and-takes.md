Full service window replacement means the entire window comes out — frame, sashes, hardware, and often the surrounding trim — and a completely new unit goes in. This is different from a pocket or insert replacement where the existing frame stays and only the operable parts swap out. Full-frame replacement costs more and takes longer, but it is the right choice when the existing frames are rotted, warped, out of square, or when you want to change window sizes.

For an average home with 15–20 windows, full service replacement runs $8,000–$20,000 installed. Here is what drives those numbers and how to navigate the process.

## Full-Frame vs. Insert Replacement: When You Need the Full Job

Insert (pocket) replacement works when the existing window frame is structurally sound, square, and in good condition. You are just swapping out the glass and sash within the existing opening. This is faster, cheaper, and less disruptive — typically $300–$600 per window installed.

Full-frame replacement is necessary when:

- **Wood frames are rotted.** Poke the sill and frame corners with an awl or screwdriver. If it sinks in easily, the wood is compromised and cannot support a new insert.
- **The frame is out of square.** Measure diagonals of the opening — if they differ by more than 1/4", the frame has shifted and an insert will not seal properly.
- **You want to change the window size.** Going from a small window to a larger one, or adding a window where there was not one, requires full-frame work.
- **There is water damage to the rough opening.** If water has reached the framing around the window, you need to address that before installing anything new.
- **You are switching window styles.** Going from single-hung to casement, or adding a bay window, requires full-frame installation.

## What Full Service Window Replacement Costs

Costs vary significantly by window type, material, size, and your local labor market. Here are realistic ranges per window, installed:

| Window Type | Budget | Mid-Range | Premium |
|-------------|--------|-----------|---------|
| Vinyl double-hung | $400–$600 | $600–$900 | $900–$1,200 |
| Wood double-hung | $600–$900 | $900–$1,400 | $1,400–$2,000+ |
| Fiberglass | $700–$1,000 | $1,000–$1,500 | $1,500–$2,200 |
| Casement (vinyl) | $450–$700 | $700–$1,100 | $1,100–$1,500 |
| Picture window (fixed) | $300–$600 | $600–$1,000 | $1,000–$1,800 |
| Bay/bow window | $1,500–$2,500 | $2,500–$4,000 | $4,000–$6,500 |

**What is included in "installed" pricing:** Removal and disposal of old windows, installation of new units, insulation around the frame, interior and exterior trim, caulking, and cleanup. What is typically NOT included: structural framing repairs, lead paint abatement (pre-1978 homes), rotted siding replacement around the opening, and permit fees.

### Cost Factors That Move the Needle

**Number of windows.** Contractors give volume discounts — replacing 10+ windows at once typically saves 10–15% per unit compared to replacing one or two.

**Floor level.** Second-story windows cost 15–25% more than ground floor due to ladder work or scaffolding requirements. Third story or above may require special equipment.

**Custom sizes.** Standard sizes are stocked and ship quickly. Non-standard dimensions are custom-ordered, adding 3–6 weeks lead time and 20–40% to the window cost.

**Energy efficiency features.** Low-E coatings, argon gas fill, and triple-pane glass improve thermal performance but increase cost. In most US climate zones, double-pane with Low-E and argon provides the best return on investment. Triple-pane makes financial sense mainly in extreme climates (northern states, mountain regions).

## The Installation Process

Understanding the process helps you evaluate contractors and set realistic expectations for disruption.

### Step 1: Measurement and Ordering (2–6 Weeks Before Install)

A proper measurement visit takes 15–20 minutes per window. The installer measures the rough opening in three places (top, middle, bottom for width; left, center, right for height) and uses the smallest dimension. They also check for square and plumb.

Windows are typically ordered after measurement and take 2–6 weeks to arrive depending on manufacturer and whether custom sizing is needed.

### Step 2: Removal of Old Windows (30–60 Minutes Per Window)

The installer removes interior trim, cuts caulking and sealant, removes screws or nails holding the frame, and lifts out the entire old window assembly. In older homes, this step sometimes reveals surprises — rotted framing, missing or inadequate flashing, or insect damage. A good contractor prices a contingency for this, and reputable companies discuss findings before proceeding with repairs at additional cost.

Per the [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/), window openings must maintain proper structural headers. If header damage is found, framing repair is necessary before the new window goes in.

### Step 3: Preparing the Rough Opening (15–30 Minutes)

The installer checks the rough opening for square and level, repairs any damaged framing, installs or replaces the sill pan flashing (critical for water management), and ensures the opening is the correct size for the new window — typically 1/2" larger than the window frame on each side.

### Step 4: Installing the New Window (30–45 Minutes)

The window is set into the opening on shims, leveled and plumbed, then fastened through the frame into the rough opening framing. Low-expansion spray foam fills the gap between the window frame and rough opening — using the wrong foam (high-expansion) can bow the frame and prevent proper operation.

### Step 5: Sealing and Trimming (30–60 Minutes)

Exterior trim or casing is installed, caulked with a quality exterior sealant, and painted or finished to match. Interior trim goes on, and the installer checks that the window operates smoothly — opens, closes, locks, and tilts for cleaning.

A crew of two installers typically completes 4–6 full-frame window replacements per day for standard sizes. Expect your whole-house project to take 3–5 days for 15–20 windows.

## Choosing the Right Window Material

Each material has genuine trade-offs. Here is the honest assessment:

**Vinyl** — The value leader. Low maintenance, good thermal performance, will not rot or need painting. Drawbacks: limited color options (paint does not adhere well), can warp in extreme heat, and the appearance is clearly vinyl. For most homeowners focused on performance and budget, vinyl is the practical choice.

**Wood** — The best-looking option, period. Stains and paints beautifully, provides excellent insulation, and adds architectural character. The downside: maintenance. Wood windows need repainting or restaining every 5–7 years, and any failure of the finish leads to rot. Wood-clad windows (wood interior, aluminum or fiberglass exterior) offer a compromise.

**Fiberglass** — The emerging middle ground. Stronger than vinyl, paintable, extremely low maintenance, and excellent thermal performance. The frame profiles can be slimmer than vinyl, letting in more glass area. Drawbacks: higher cost and fewer manufacturer options, though that is changing. Per [American Wood Council](https://www.awc.org/) standards, fiberglass frames offer comparable structural integrity to wood in most residential applications.

**Aluminum** — Mostly used in commercial applications and warm climates. Strong and slim-profile, but terrible thermal performance unless the frame includes a thermal break. Not recommended for heating-dominated climates.

## Permits and Codes

Most jurisdictions require a building permit for full-frame window replacement, particularly if:

- You are changing the size of the opening
- The work involves structural modifications (header changes)
- You are in a historic district
- You are adding egress windows (bedrooms require minimum window opening sizes per code)

Permit costs range from $50–$300 depending on your municipality. Your contractor should pull the permit — if they suggest skipping it, that is a red flag. Unpermitted work can create problems when you sell the home and the building inspector finds non-conforming windows. Hardware and fastening should meet structural requirements per [Simpson Strong-Tie](https://www.strongtie.com/) or equivalent engineering specifications.

## How to Evaluate Window Replacement Contractors

Get at least three quotes from different companies. When comparing:

- **Ask about their warranty.** Most window manufacturers offer 20-year to lifetime warranties on the product. The installation warranty is separate and comes from the contractor — look for at least 5 years on labor and workmanship.
- **Check for proper licensing and insurance.** Window installation typically falls under a general contractor or specialty contractor license.
- **Ask to see completed projects.** Drive by past installations if possible — check the trim work, caulking lines, and how the windows look in the wall.
- **Understand their payment terms.** A deposit of 30–50% is standard for custom-ordered windows. Final payment should be due upon completion and your satisfaction.
- **Ask about their process for surprises.** What happens if they open the wall and find rot or structural issues? How is that priced and communicated?

## Energy Savings: Setting Realistic Expectations

Window companies love to quote dramatic energy savings numbers. The honest reality is more modest: replacing single-pane windows with modern double-pane Low-E units saves approximately 10–15% on heating and cooling costs for most homes. Replacing functional double-pane windows from the 1990s or later with newer double-pane units saves very little — the technology has not changed that dramatically.

Where window replacement does make a noticeable comfort difference: eliminating drafts near windows, reducing cold spots in winter, and lowering noise from outside. These quality-of-life improvements are often more valuable than the energy savings alone.

## The Bottom Line

Full service window replacement is a significant investment, but it is the right one when your existing frames are beyond repair. Budget $500–$1,200 per window for quality vinyl or fiberglass with professional installation. Do not rush the decision — get multiple quotes, verify licensing, and choose a contractor based on their process and reputation rather than the lowest number. The windows will be in your walls for 20–30 years, so the quality of both product and installation matters far more than saving $50 per opening.
