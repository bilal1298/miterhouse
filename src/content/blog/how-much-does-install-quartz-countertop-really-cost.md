Quartz countertop installation costs $55 to $150 per square foot fully installed in 2026, putting a standard 40-square-foot kitchen countertop project in the $2,200 to $6,000 range. For a larger kitchen with an island (55 to 65 square feet of counter space), expect $3,300 to $9,750 all in. These numbers include the slab, fabrication, cutouts, edge profiling, and professional installation — but not plumbing, old countertop removal, or backsplash work.

Quartz is engineered stone, not quarried like granite. It is made from roughly 90 to 94% ground natural quartz bound with polymer resins and pigments. That manufacturing process is why quartz offers more consistent coloring than natural stone — and why it costs a bit more than equivalent granite grades.

## Quartz Price Tiers Explained

Like granite, quartz comes in tiers, but the grading system reflects brand, pattern complexity, and design trend popularity rather than raw material quality.

**Entry-level ($55–$75/sq ft installed):** Solid colors and simple patterns from brands like Silestone's basic line or LG Viatera. These look clean and modern. A white or gray solid quartz countertop is the most popular choice for kitchen flips because it photographs well and appeals to broad buyer tastes.

**Mid-range ($75–$110/sq ft installed):** Marble-look patterns, veining, and more complex colorways. Caesarstone, Cambria, and Silestone's premium lines sit here. This tier gives you the visual appeal of natural marble without marble's maintenance headaches — no etching from lemon juice, no sealing required.

**Premium ($110–$150+/sq ft installed):** Designer collaborations, thick-slab formats, and ultra-realistic natural stone patterns. Calacatta-look quartz in this range is almost indistinguishable from genuine Calacatta marble in photographs, though up close the resin binder is detectable under certain light.

## Quartz vs. Granite: An Honest Comparison

I get asked this question constantly, so here is a straightforward comparison based on living with both materials.

**Maintenance:** Quartz wins decisively. It never needs sealing, resists staining better, and cleans up with soap and water. Granite needs annual sealing and can stain if acidic liquids sit on it too long.

**Heat resistance:** Granite wins. You can set a hot pan directly on granite without damage. Quartz resin can scorch or discolor from sustained high heat — always use a trivet. This is the most common damage I see on quartz countertops.

**Appearance consistency:** Quartz wins if you want predictable results. What you see in the showroom is what you get. Granite varies slab to slab, which is either a feature or a bug depending on your preference.

**Durability:** Roughly equal for daily kitchen use. Both resist scratching well (though neither is scratchproof), and both last decades with normal care.

**Cost:** Granite is typically 10 to 20% less expensive at comparable quality levels. Entry-level granite starts lower than entry-level quartz.

**Resale impact:** In 2026, quartz and granite perform roughly equally in buyer perception for most markets. Quartz has a slight edge in contemporary and modern kitchens; granite holds up better in traditional settings.

## How the Installation Process Works

The quartz installation process mirrors granite closely, but with a few differences worth knowing about.

**Templating (Week 1):** A fabricator measures your exact layout using digital templating tools or physical templates. This visit takes 30 to 60 minutes. Your cabinets must be fully installed, level, and secured before this step.

**Fabrication (Weeks 2-3):** The fabricator cuts the quartz to your template specs, polishes edges, and creates cutouts. Quartz fabrication generates significant silica dust, which is why this happens at the fabricator's shop — not in your kitchen. The [International Code Council](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) outlines residential construction standards that apply to this work.

**Installation (Week 3-4):** A crew of two to four people delivers and sets the slabs. The process is similar to granite: dry fit, adhesive application, slab placement, seam filling, and sink installation. Expect 2 to 5 hours for a standard kitchen.

**One critical difference from granite:** Quartz seams tend to be more visible than granite seams because the uniform patterning highlights the joint line. A skilled fabricator minimizes this with tight cuts and pattern-matched epoxy, but on solid-color or subtly veined quartz, seams are harder to hide than on busy granite patterns.

## Where to Save Money on Quartz

**Choose from in-stock inventory.** Fabricators stock popular colors from their primary suppliers. Choosing from available inventory avoids special-order surcharges and often speeds up the timeline by a week.

**Skip the waterfall edge.** Waterfall edges (where the countertop continues vertically down the side of an island) look dramatic but require significantly more material and fabrication time. On a 3-foot island end, a waterfall adds $500 to $1,200. If budget matters more than aesthetics, a standard finished edge looks clean and costs nothing extra.

**Stick with standard edge profiles.** The eased edge (slightly rounded square) is the most popular quartz edge in 2026 and it suits the material's modern aesthetic perfectly. Ornate profiles like ogee actually look odd on quartz in most contemporary kitchens.

**Handle demo yourself.** Removing old laminate countertops is well within DIY ability. Unscrew from below, pry loose, haul away. This saves $150 to $500.

**Consolidate faucet holes.** A single-hole faucet eliminates extra drilling charges. Single-hole models are also the current design preference for most quartz kitchen installations.

## Common Quartz Installation Problems

Having been through this and heard from plenty of others, here are the issues that surface most often.

**Uneven cabinets.** Quartz is rigid and unforgiving. Even slight cabinet unevenness causes rocking or visible gaps. If your cabinets are out of level by more than 1/8 inch over 8 feet, get them shimmed before the templating visit.

**Sink compatibility surprises.** If you are switching from a drop-in sink to undermount during the countertop swap, confirm the cabinet base supports the weight from below. Undermount sinks hang from the countertop, and the clips and adhesive need solid wood to anchor into — not just particleboard.

**Color mismatch between samples and slabs.** Small showroom samples can appear different from full slabs under your kitchen lighting. Ask to see a full slab or at least a large format sample before committing. Most fabricators allow slab yard visits.

**Damage from harsh chemicals.** Quartz resists most household chemicals, but bleach-based cleaners and abrasive pads degrade the resin surface over time. Stick to mild soap and water for daily cleaning.

## Warranty Considerations

Most major quartz brands offer 10 to 15-year warranties on the slab material itself, covering manufacturing defects and premature staining or discoloration. Fabrication and installation warranties are separate — provided by your fabricator — and typically cover 1 to 2 years for seams, cutout integrity, and adhesion.

Read both warranties carefully. Slab warranties often exclude damage from heat (trivets are not optional — they are required), impact, or chemical exposure. Fabrication warranties usually exclude damage from structural movement, water exposure to seams, or modifications after installation.

If your quartz project is part of a [broader renovation](/blog/the-real-deal-on-kitchen-renovation-services/), coordinate warranty documentation so you know exactly who to contact for which type of issue. As per [NFPA standards](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70), any electrical work done alongside countertop installation should also be separately documented and warranted.

## Bottom Line

For a standard kitchen, budget $3,500 to $6,500 for quartz countertops fully installed with mid-range material and a standard edge profile. Add old countertop removal, plumbing, and a 15% contingency, and your realistic total is $4,500 to $8,000.

Quartz makes the most sense for homeowners who prioritize zero-maintenance surfaces, consistent appearance, and modern aesthetics. If heat resistance and natural variation matter more, [granite remains a strong alternative](/blog/painting-kitchen-cabinets-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/) at a slightly lower price point. Either way, you are investing in a surface that will look good and function well for the next 15 to 25 years.
