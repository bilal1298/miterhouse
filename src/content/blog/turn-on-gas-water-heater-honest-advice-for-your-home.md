Turning on a gas water heater is something most homeowners need to do at some point — after a move-in, a power outage, seasonal shutdown, or after the pilot light has gone out. The process takes about 10 minutes, requires no tools, and is well within any homeowner's ability. But there are safety steps that matter, and skipping them is how people get hurt or damage the unit.

Here is the complete process for both standing pilot and electronic ignition models, plus what to do when things do not go as planned.

## Before You Start: Safety Checks

Run through these before touching the gas valve:

**Smell the area.** If you detect a strong gas odor (rotten egg smell), do not attempt to light the water heater. Open windows, leave the area, and call your gas utility from outside. Their emergency inspection is free.

**Check that the water supply is on.** A gas water heater should never fire with an empty or partially filled tank — it will overheat and damage the tank lining. Open a hot water faucet somewhere in the house. If water flows (even cold), the tank is filled. If nothing comes out, the water supply valve above the heater may be closed. Open it and wait until water flows from the faucet before proceeding.

**Check the vent pipe.** Make sure the metal exhaust vent above the water heater is connected at all joints and not disconnected or crushed. Exhaust gases must have a clear path out. Per [OSHA safety requirements](https://www.osha.gov/), proper ventilation is mandatory when operating gas appliances.

**Verify the gas supply valve is open.** Follow the gas line from the wall to the water heater. There is a shutoff valve — typically a lever handle. Parallel to the pipe means open; perpendicular means closed. Turn it to the open position if it is not already.

## Lighting a Standing Pilot Water Heater

Most water heaters manufactured before 2010 and many current economy models use a standing pilot light. Here is the step-by-step:

### Step 1: Set the Gas Valve to Off

The gas control valve is the large knob on the front or side of the water heater, typically at knee height. Turn it to the "OFF" position and wait a full 5 minutes. This allows any residual gas in the combustion chamber to dissipate. Do not skip this wait — it exists to prevent a flash ignition.

### Step 2: Set the Temperature Dial

While waiting, turn the temperature dial to the lowest setting. You will adjust it later, but starting low prevents the burner from immediately firing at full capacity.

### Step 3: Turn to Pilot and Prepare to Light

After 5 minutes, turn the gas valve to "PILOT." On most models, you need to push the knob in and hold it to allow gas to flow to the pilot assembly.

Locate the pilot light. It is visible through a small window or opening at the base of the water heater. On some models, you need to remove a small access panel.

### Step 4: Ignite the Pilot

**If your water heater has a piezo igniter** (a button or separate switch near the gas valve): Press and hold the gas valve knob, then click the igniter button. You should see a spark through the viewing window. Keep clicking until the pilot lights — sometimes it takes 3–5 clicks.

**If there is no igniter** (older units): Press and hold the gas valve knob, then use a long-reach lighter or long fireplace match to reach the pilot assembly through the access opening. Light the pilot. Keep your face away from the opening.

### Step 5: Hold and Release

Continue holding the gas valve knob in for 30–60 seconds after the pilot flame appears. This heats the thermocouple — a safety sensor that tells the gas valve it is safe to allow gas flow. If you release too early, the pilot goes out.

After holding for 60 seconds, release the knob slowly. The pilot should stay lit. If it goes out, repeat the process. If it will not stay lit after three attempts, the thermocouple likely needs replacement (covered below).

### Step 6: Turn to On

Turn the gas valve from "PILOT" to "ON." You should hear the main burner ignite — a soft whooshing sound followed by visible flame through the viewing window.

### Step 7: Set Your Temperature

Turn the temperature dial to your desired setting. The Department of Energy recommends 120 degrees Fahrenheit — hot enough for comfort, low enough to prevent scalding and reduce energy costs. Per the [Department of Energy's water heating guide](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/water-heating), each 10-degree reduction saves 3–5% on water heating costs.

Most dials are marked "A" through "E" or "warm" to "hot" rather than specific temperatures. "B" or the midpoint typically corresponds to approximately 120 degrees. You can verify by running hot water at a faucet for 2 minutes and checking with a cooking thermometer.

## Lighting an Electronic Ignition Water Heater

Newer water heaters use electronic ignition instead of a standing pilot. These are more energy efficient because they only produce a flame when the burner needs to fire.

The process is simpler:

1. Make sure the gas supply valve is open
2. Set the temperature dial to your desired setting
3. Turn the gas control to "ON"
4. The electronic ignition system handles the rest — you may hear a clicking sound followed by the burner lighting

If the electronic ignition fails to light, many units display error codes through an LED indicator on the gas valve. Check your manual for code meanings. Common causes of ignition failure include a dead igniter (replacement part: $30–$60) or a failed gas valve control module.

## How Long Until You Have Hot Water

After the burner fires, a standard 40-gallon gas water heater takes 30–40 minutes to heat from cold to full temperature. A 50-gallon tank takes 40–50 minutes. Recovery rate (how fast the heater reheats after use) is typically 30–40 gallons per hour for gas units.

If you are turning on the water heater after a long vacancy, run the hot water at a faucet for a minute first to flush any stale water from the lines.

## Troubleshooting: When the Pilot Will Not Stay Lit

This is the most common frustration homeowners face. The pilot lights, you hold the knob for a full minute, release it, and the pilot goes out. Here are the causes in order of likelihood:

### Bad Thermocouple

The thermocouple is a small copper tube that sits in the pilot flame. It generates a tiny electrical voltage when heated, telling the gas valve to stay open. When it fails, the gas valve shuts off as soon as you release the knob.

**DIY replacement:** Turn off the gas. Unscrew the thermocouple from the gas valve (one nut, typically 7/16" wrench) and from the pilot assembly bracket. Take it to the hardware store to match the length. Universal thermocouples cost $10–$20. Install the replacement, making sure the tip sits squarely in the pilot flame path. Do not overtighten the connection — snug plus a quarter turn is sufficient.

### Dirty Pilot Assembly

Dust and debris can partially block the pilot orifice, producing a weak flame that does not adequately heat the thermocouple.

**Fix:** With the gas off, use compressed air to blow out the pilot orifice. A fine sewing needle can carefully clear a clogged orifice — be gentle, as the opening is precision-sized.

### Drafts

A strong draft in the room can blow out the pilot. Check for open windows, running exhaust fans, or gaps around the combustion chamber access panel. The pilot flame should be steady, not flickering wildly.

### Failed Gas Valve

If a new thermocouple does not solve the problem and the pilot assembly is clean, the gas control valve itself may be faulty. This is a professional repair — gas valve replacement runs $150–$300 for the part plus labor. On a water heater over 8–10 years old, it is worth comparing this cost to full replacement.

## Situations Where You Should Not DIY

Call a licensed plumber or HVAC technician in these cases:

- **You smell gas and cannot identify the source.** Call your gas utility first.
- **The water heater is newly installed.** First-time gas hookup should be inspected by a professional to verify the gas connection, venting, and code compliance per the [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/).
- **The vent pipe is damaged, disconnected, or improperly routed.** Backdrafting is a carbon monoxide hazard.
- **You see scorch marks or melted components.** These indicate past unsafe combustion events that need professional evaluation.
- **The water heater is making banging, popping, or rumbling sounds.** Sediment buildup at the bottom of the tank can trap gas bubbles that pop violently — this tank needs flushing or replacement. ## Seasonal Startup Tips

If you have shut down the water heater for a vacation home or extended absence:

**Before shutting down:** Turn the gas valve to "PILOT" (or "vacation" mode if available) rather than fully off. This maintains a minimal flame, prevents the combustion chamber from developing condensation, and makes restart easier. The gas cost is about $5–$8 per month.

**On startup after full shutdown:**
1. Check the T&P (temperature and pressure) relief valve by lifting the lever — water should flow and stop when released. If it does not, the valve is stuck and needs replacement before full operation.
2. Open the cold water supply slowly and let the tank fill completely before lighting.
3. Check all visible connections for drips after the tank is full and pressurized.
4. Light using the steps above and monitor the first full heating cycle — listen for unusual sounds and check for leaks around the drain valve and connections.
