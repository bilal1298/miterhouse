Cyclone fencing — also called chain-link fencing — is the most practical fencing option when you need to enclose a large area on a reasonable budget. It will not win any beauty contests, but it lasts 15 to 20 years with zero maintenance, keeps pets and children contained, and costs a fraction of what wood or vinyl alternatives run. If you are considering installing one yourself, this guide covers what the project actually involves, from material selection through tensioning the mesh.

For materials and professional installation, cyclone fencing runs $8 to $25 per linear foot. DIY installation drops that to $5 to $12 per linear foot for materials only. A typical 150-foot residential perimeter fence costs $1,200 to $3,750 professionally installed, or $750 to $1,800 for materials if you do the work yourself.

## Materials You Will Need

A cyclone fence system has more components than most people expect. Here is the complete parts list for a standard 4-foot residential fence:

**Posts:**
- **Terminal posts** (corners, ends, and gate openings): 2-3/8 inch outside diameter galvanized steel, 8 feet long (3 feet goes in the ground, 5 feet above grade — the extra foot above the 4-foot mesh accounts for the post cap). Budget 1 terminal post per corner plus 2 per gate opening.
- **Line posts** (everything in between): 1-5/8 inch outside diameter, 8 feet long, spaced every 10 feet along the run.

**Hardware:**
- Tension bands (3 per terminal post for a 4-foot fence)
- Brace bands (1 per terminal post connection)
- Rail ends (1 per terminal post connection)
- Tension bars (1 per terminal post — the vertical bar that the mesh fabric attaches to at starts, ends, and corners)
- Tie wires or fence ties (1 per foot of fence for attaching mesh to the top rail, plus 3 per line post)
- Post caps (dome or loop style for line posts, loop style for terminal posts if using a top rail)
- Top rail: 1-3/8 inch galvanized steel, sold in 10.5-foot sections that telescope together

**Mesh fabric:** Galvanized chain-link mesh, typically 4 feet high x 50-foot rolls. Standard residential gauge is 11 or 11.5 gauge. For more durability (dog runs, high-traffic areas), use 9 gauge.

**Concrete:** One 80-pound bag per post minimum. Terminal posts and gate posts get two bags each.

## The Installation Process

### Step 1: Survey and Mark the Line

Before you dig a single hole, you need to know exactly where the fence goes.

Call 811 (the national utility locate service) at least 48 hours before digging. This is free, required by law, and has saved me from hitting a gas line on more than one project.

Drive stakes at each corner, end, and gate location. Run a mason's line between them — this is your fence line. Every post, every piece of mesh, and every rail follows this line. Getting it straight at this stage saves endless frustration later.

Check with your local building department about setback requirements. Most municipalities require fences to be set 2 to 6 inches inside the property line, and heights are typically limited to 4 feet in front yards and 6 feet in back yards. Some HOAs have additional restrictions.

### Step 2: Set Terminal Posts First

Dig holes for corner, end, and gate posts. These holes should be 8 to 10 inches in diameter and at least 30 inches deep (one-third of the total post length below grade). In frost-prone areas, dig below the frost line — typically 36 to 48 inches.

Set each terminal post plumb in the hole, brace it with temporary supports, and fill with concrete. Use a level on two adjacent sides to verify plumb. The post top should be 2 inches higher than the fence height (so 50 inches above grade for a 4-foot fence).

Let the concrete cure for 24 to 48 hours before hanging any weight. Rushing this step is probably the single biggest DIY mistake — I have seen posts lean within weeks because someone could not wait a day.

### Step 3: Set Line Posts

Run a string between terminal posts at the top height. This string guides the height of every line post in between. Dig holes at 10-foot intervals along the fence line, set each post to the string height, and concrete them in. Line posts should be 2 inches lower than terminal posts (the top rail sits on top of line post caps and connects into terminal post rail ends, creating a level top line).

### Step 4: Install Top Rail

Slide the top rail through the loop caps on the line posts. Where sections meet, the smaller end telescopes into the larger end. At terminal posts, the rail connects to rail ends attached to tension bands. Cut the last section to length with a pipe cutter or hacksaw.

### Step 5: Hang and Tension the Mesh

This is the step that makes or breaks the installation.

Unroll the mesh fabric along the fence line. Stand it up against the posts. Weave a tension bar through the end loops of the mesh and bolt it to the tension bands on the first terminal post.

Walk the roll along the fence line to the next terminal post, stretching it by hand as you go. When you reach the terminal post, you need a fence stretcher (also called a come-along) to pull the mesh tight before attaching the final tension bar.

The right tension is firm enough that the mesh does not sag or ripple, but not so tight that it pulls the posts inward. A good rule: you should be able to push the mesh in about 1/2 to 1 inch with your hand at the midpoint between posts.

Once tensioned, tie the mesh to the top rail and each line post using tie wires or aluminum fence ties. This prevents the mesh from sagging between attachment points.

### Step 6: Install Gates

Gates hang from the terminal posts at the gate opening. Standard walk gates are 3 to 4 feet wide; drive gates are 10 to 12 feet wide. Gate hardware includes hinges, a latch, and a gate frame with pre-attached mesh.

Hang the gate so the bottom clears the ground by 2 inches (more if the ground is uneven or slopes). Adjust the hinges until the gate swings freely and the latch engages without forcing it.

## DIY vs. Professional Installation

**DIY makes sense when:**
- The run is relatively straight without complex grading changes
- You have access to a post hole digger (renting a gas-powered auger costs $50 to $100 per day and is highly recommended)
- You have a helper — stretching mesh solo is technically possible but miserable
- Budget is the primary concern

**Hire a pro when:**
- The terrain is sloped or uneven (racking chain link on a grade requires experience)
- You need to remove an existing fence first
- The project is large (300+ feet) — the time savings from a professional crew is significant
- Local code requires a permit and you are not comfortable navigating the process

Professional installation for cyclone fencing is among the cheapest per-foot of any fence type. If the budget difference between DIY and pro is $500 to $1,000, hiring out might be worth the saved weekend.

## Cost Comparison with Other Fence Types

For context, here is how cyclone fencing compares to alternatives per linear foot installed:

| Fence Type | Material Cost/ft | Installed Cost/ft | Lifespan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyclone/chain-link (4 ft) | $5 - $12 | $8 - $25 | 15-20 years |
| [Cedar privacy](/blog/cedar-fence-installation-guide-diy-or-hire-out/) | $12 - $25 | $20 - $45 | 15-20 years (with maintenance) |
| [Vinyl/PVC](/blog/installing-pvc-fence-what-to-know-first/) | $15 - $30 | $25 - $50 | 20-30 years |
| [Wrought iron](/blog/wrought-iron-fence-installation-costs-and-tips/) | $20 - $40 | $30 - $75 | 50+ years |

Cyclone fencing wins on cost per foot every time. If aesthetics matter or privacy is the goal, consider adding vinyl privacy slats to the chain-link mesh ($3 to $8 per linear foot) — they thread into the mesh diamond pattern and block most of the view while keeping the structural advantages of chain-link.

## Maintenance and Longevity

Galvanized cyclone fencing needs almost nothing:

- Check post plumb annually — frost heave or soil settling can shift posts over time
- Replace any broken tie wires or fence ties before the mesh sags
- Treat surface rust spots with a wire brush and cold galvanizing spray ($8 to $15 per can)
- Keep vegetation trimmed away from the mesh — vines and aggressive plants can distort the fence over time

For [additional privacy options](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) and landscaping ideas along fence lines, consider planting fast-growing shrubs like privet or arborvitae on the inside of the fence. They fill in within 2 to 3 years and create a green privacy screen that complements the utilitarian look of chain-link.

A cyclone fence is not glamorous, but it is honest work — affordable, durable, and effective at the basic job of marking a boundary and keeping things where they belong.
