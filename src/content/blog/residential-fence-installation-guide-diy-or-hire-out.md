A residential fence serves three purposes: defining your property boundary, providing privacy or security, and adding curb appeal. Getting the installation right affects all three for the next 15 to 25 years. Whether you build it yourself or hire a contractor, the fundamentals are the same: proper post setting, correct materials for your climate, and compliance with local codes.

Here is the complete guide to residential fence installation, covering every major fence type, the permit process, and an honest cost-vs-time comparison between DIY and professional installation.

## Before You Start: The Planning Phase

Skipping planning is the most expensive mistake in fence building. These steps take a few hours and prevent thousands of dollars in problems.

### Know Your Property Line

This is not optional. A fence built even a few inches over the property line is your neighbor's fence, legally. They can demand removal at your expense.

**Option 1: Find your survey pins.** Most properties have iron pins at the corners, typically 12 to 18 inches below grade. A metal detector ($30 rental) helps locate them.

**Option 2: Order a new survey.** Costs $300 to $600. Worth it if you cannot find pins, if you have had any boundary disputes, or if the property line is ambiguous.

**Option 3: Check your plat map.** Available from your county recorder's office, usually free or a few dollars. Shows property dimensions but does not physically mark the line.

Most fence codes require a setback of 2 to 6 inches from the property line. Some municipalities require the "good side" (the smooth, finished face) to face the neighbor. Check before building.

### Understand Local Codes

Fence regulations typically cover:

- **Maximum height.** Front yard: usually 3 to 4 feet. Side and rear yard: usually 6 feet. Some jurisdictions allow 8 feet with a variance.
- **Setback from property line.** Varies from 0 to 12 inches.
- **Setback from sidewalks and streets.** Sight-line triangles at intersections and driveways typically restrict fence placement.
- **Material restrictions.** Some areas prohibit chain link in front yards. HOA rules may limit material and color choices further.
- **Permit requirements.** Most jurisdictions require a permit for fences over 4 feet. Fees range from $50 to $200. The [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) provides baseline standards that most local codes reference.

### Call 811

Every state requires you to call 811 at least two business days before digging. They mark buried gas, electric, water, cable, and phone lines at no charge. Hitting a gas line is a safety emergency. Hitting a cable line is a billing emergency. Both are preventable.

## Material Selection Guide

Your climate, budget, and intended use narrow the material choice quickly.

### Wood

The most popular residential fence material. Available in dozens of styles from simple picket to board-on-board privacy.

**Pressure-treated pine.** The budget option. Costs $12 to $22 per linear foot installed. Lasts 15 to 20 years with regular staining or sealing. Susceptible to warping and splitting if not maintained.

**Cedar.** Naturally rot-resistant and insect-resistant. Costs $18 to $35 per linear foot installed. Weathers to a silver-gray if left untreated, or maintains its warm color with periodic staining. Lasts 20 to 25 years.

**Redwood.** Premium option with superior rot resistance. Costs $25 to $45 per linear foot installed. Limited availability outside the West Coast, which drives up shipping costs elsewhere.

### Vinyl/PVC

Zero-maintenance alternative to wood. Costs $22 to $40 per linear foot installed. Does not rot, warp, or need painting. Available in white, tan, gray, and wood-grain textures. Lasts 20 to 30 years. Drawback: cracks in extreme cold and can look artificial up close.

### Chain Link

Most affordable physical fence. Costs $8 to $18 per linear foot installed for 4-foot residential height. Virtually maintenance-free. Lasts 20+ years. Not the most attractive, but privacy slats ($2 to $5 per foot extra) or climbing plants improve appearance.

### Aluminum

Ornamental metal fencing that mimics wrought iron at a fraction of the weight and cost. Costs $25 to $45 per linear foot installed. Does not rust. Lasts 30+ years. Best for decorative boundaries and pool enclosures. Not suitable for privacy (open picket design).

### Composite

Engineered from wood fibers and plastic. Costs $25 to $50 per linear foot installed. Combines the appearance of wood with the low maintenance of vinyl. Heavy, so installation requires robust post setting.

## DIY Installation: Step by Step

If you decide to build it yourself, here is the process for a standard 6-foot wood privacy fence.

### Tools You Need

| Tool | Own or Rent | Approximate Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Post hole digger or auger | Rent auger ($50-$100/day) | - |
| Level (4 ft) | Own | $20 - $40 |
| String line and stakes | Own | $10 |
| Tape measure (25 ft) | Own | $15 - $25 |
| Circular saw | Own | $60 - $150 |
| Drill/driver with bits | Own | $60 - $120 |
| Speed square | Own | $8 - $12 |
| Safety glasses and gloves | Own | $15 - $25 |

### The Build Process

**1. Mark the layout.** Drive stakes at every corner and gate post location. Run string lines between stakes. Measure and mark post locations along the string, 8 feet on center (standard spacing). Verify all corners are square using the 3-4-5 triangle method.

**2. Dig post holes.** Each hole should be 10 to 12 inches in diameter and one-third the total post length deep plus 6 inches for gravel drainage. For a 6-foot fence with 4x4x8 posts, that means 30-inch deep holes. Pour 4 to 6 inches of gravel in the bottom for drainage.

**3. Set corner and end posts first.** These are your reference points. Plumb each post with a level, brace it, and fill with concrete. Use two bags of 80-pound fast-setting concrete per post. Add water to the hole per the instructions. Let these cure for 24 hours minimum before attaching any rails.

**4. Set line posts.** With the string line running between the set corner posts, align each intermediate post to the string. This ensures a straight fence line. Plumb, brace, and concrete each one.

**5. Attach rails.** Typically three horizontal 2x4 rails: one 6 inches from the top, one 6 inches from the bottom, and one centered. Use galvanized or stainless steel brackets or toe-screw them into the posts. Check level as you go.

**6. Attach pickets.** Start at a corner or gate post. Use a spacer (a 16d nail works well) between pickets for consistent gaps that allow wood expansion. Use two screws per rail per picket (6 screws per picket for 3-rail construction). Check plumb every 10 pickets.

**7. Install gates.** Hang gate hardware on the posts first. Build or assemble the gate to fit the opening with 1/2-inch clearance on each side. Use a gate wheel or caster on the bottom of heavy gates to prevent sagging.

**8. Finish.** Apply stain or sealer within 30 days of installation for pressure-treated wood. Cedar can be stained immediately. Cap all post tops to prevent water infiltration.

## Maintenance by Material

Your annual maintenance commitment varies dramatically by material.

| Material | Annual Maintenance | Estimated Annual Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure-treated wood | Stain/seal every 2-3 years, replace warped boards | $100 - $300 |
| Cedar | Optional stain every 3-5 years, minor repairs | $50 - $200 |
| Vinyl | Wash with soap and water annually | $0 - $20 |
| Chain link | Check tension, repair bent sections | $0 - $50 |
| Aluminum | Wash annually, check for loose fasteners | $0 - $30 |

Over 20 years, a pressure-treated pine fence that costs $3,000 to install plus $4,000 in maintenance totals $7,000. A vinyl fence that costs $6,000 to install plus $400 in maintenance totals $6,400. Factor in maintenance when choosing your material, not just the installation price.

The [American Wood Council](https://www.awc.org/) publishes specifications for wood treatment and structural requirements that are worth reviewing if you are going the wood route. Choosing the right material for your climate and maintenance tolerance is half the battle --- the other half is getting those posts set correctly. Do both, and your fence will outlast the mortgage.
