A flooded basement creates a situation where the decisions you make in the first 24 to 48 hours determine whether restoration costs $2,000 or $20,000. The immediate priority is removing water and starting the drying process. Everything else — restoration, repairs, preventing future floods — comes after the space is dry.

The three biggest decisions that shape your final bill: handle extraction yourself or call a [water damage remediation company](/blog/finding-good-water-damage-remediation-contractors-near-you/), which materials to save versus demolish, and whether to file insurance or pay out of pocket. Here's how to make each one.

## The First 24 Hours

### Safety First

Before entering a flooded basement, check for electrical hazards. If the water level is above any outlets, switches, or the electrical panel, do not enter until the main breaker is turned off — and turn it off from the main panel upstairs, not by wading into the water. If you can't safely reach the main breaker, call your utility company to disconnect power.

Also be aware of:
- **Structural damage.** If the flooding was severe, foundation walls may have shifted. Look for new cracks or bowing.
- **Contaminated water.** If the flood involved sewage backup (Category 3 water), wear rubber boots, waterproof gloves, and an N95 respirator. Do not let this water contact your skin.
- **Gas leaks.** If you smell gas, leave immediately and call your gas utility.

### Decision 1: DIY Extraction or Call a Pro?

**Under 2 inches of clean water:** A wet/dry shop vacuum ($80 to $150) handles this. You'll need to empty it repeatedly. This is a clear DIY situation.

**2 inches to 12 inches of clean water:** A submersible utility pump ($100 to $200) with a garden hose routed outside is the fastest approach. These pump 30 to 50 gallons per minute. Still manageable as DIY if you can get the pump running within a few hours.

**Over 12 inches or any contaminated water:** Call a water damage restoration company. They have industrial pumps, protective equipment, and the expertise to handle contaminated water safely. Professional extraction for a single room runs $500 to $2,000.

**Important:** Do not pump out a deeply flooded basement all at once. If the surrounding soil is still saturated, the hydrostatic pressure against the empty walls can crack or collapse the foundation. Remove water in stages — no more than one-third of the volume per day.

### Drying the Space

After standing water is removed, the real work begins. Every surface below the flood line is saturated and needs to dry completely before any restoration starts.

**Industrial dehumidifiers** ($200 to $500 to buy, $50 to $75 per day to rent) pull moisture from the air. Commercial-grade units remove 70 to 150 pints per day compared to 30 to 50 for residential models.

**Air movers/fans** ($50 to $150 each) create airflow across wet surfaces. You need at least one per room, aimed at the walls and floor.

**Target timeline:** 3 to 5 days of continuous dehumidification and air movement for a standard basement flood. Use a moisture meter ($25 to $40) to verify surfaces are dry before proceeding.

## Decision 2: What to Save and What to Demolish

Once the space is dry, assess what can be saved and what needs to go. Getting this wrong in either direction costs money — replacing materials that were fine wastes budget, but keeping materials that harbor moisture creates mold problems months later.

### What Almost Always Needs Replacement

- **Carpet and pad.** If the carpet was submerged in contaminated water, it must go. Even clean-water-soaked carpet can be saved only if dried within 24 to 48 hours — and it rarely is.
- **Drywall below the flood line.** Water wicks up drywall 12 to 18 inches above the actual water level. Cut the drywall 2 feet above the visible water line and replace.
- **Fiberglass insulation that got wet.** It doesn't dry effectively and becomes a mold incubator.
- **Any paper-faced material that stayed wet more than 48 hours.** This includes cardboard, books, and paper-backed anything.

### What Can Usually Be Saved

- **Metal stud framing.** Clean, dry, and inspect. If it's not corroded, it's fine.
- **Wood framing** that is dried thoroughly within 48 to 72 hours. Test moisture content with a meter — below 15% is acceptable.
- **LVP and tile flooring.** These are waterproof materials. Remove, clean, dry the subfloor, and reinstall if undamaged.
- **Concrete and masonry.** These materials handle water. Clean, disinfect, and allow to dry.

### Mold Assessment

Mold can begin growing within 24 to 48 hours in warm, humid conditions. After a flood, watch for:

- Visible mold growth (black, green, or white fuzzy patches)
- Musty odors that persist after drying
- Staining on surfaces that wasn't present before the flood

If you find mold covering an area larger than 10 square feet, the [EPA recommends](https://www.epa.gov/mold) hiring a professional mold remediation company rather than attempting DIY cleanup. Small areas (under 10 square feet) can be cleaned with detergent and water — skip the bleach, which is less effective than most people think.

## Restoration Costs

### Cleanup and Drying

| Service | DIY Cost | Pro Cost |
|---------|----------|----------|
| Water extraction | $100–$250 (pump rental) | $500–$2,000 |
| Dehumidifier rental (5–7 days) | $250–$500 | Included in pro service |
| Debris removal | $100–$300 | $500–$1,500 |
| Mold treatment (< 10 sq ft) | $50–$100 | $500–$3,000 |
| Mold remediation (> 10 sq ft) | Not recommended DIY | $1,500–$5,000+ |

### Rebuilding (1,000 sq ft basement)

The cost to rebuild depends on what was damaged and the finish level you want. If the flood destroyed a finished basement:

- **Drywall replacement** (lower 4 feet of walls): $2,000 to $5,000
- **Insulation replacement:** $1,000 to $2,500
- **Flooring replacement:** $2,000 to $6,000
- **Electrical repairs:** $500 to $3,000
- **Painting:** $1,000 to $2,500
- **Trim and doors:** $500 to $2,000

**Total for restoring a finished basement:** $7,000 to $22,000

For a previously unfinished basement, cleanup costs are lower but you still face the expense of waterproofing and rebuilding. See our [basement finishing pricing guide](/blog/cost-to-finish-a-basement/) for new finishing costs.

## Decision 3: Insurance or Out of Pocket?

### What Homeowner's Insurance Covers

Standard homeowner's insurance covers:
- Sudden and accidental water damage (burst pipe, water heater failure)
- The resulting damage to finishes, contents, and structure

It does **not** cover:
- Flooding from rising groundwater or storm surge (requires separate flood insurance)
- Sewer backup (requires a specific endorsement, usually $40 to $100 per year)
- Gradual water damage from a slow leak you should have noticed
- Mold remediation (often excluded or severely limited)

If your damage falls under a covered cause, file the claim — deductibles run $500 to $2,500 for most policies, and restoration costs almost always exceed that. If the cause is excluded, you're paying out of pocket regardless, and the decision is whether to DIY or hire pros for the work itself.

### Documentation Steps

1. **Photograph and video everything** before cleanup begins. Every wall, every item, every inch of water line.
2. **Save damaged materials** until the adjuster has seen them. Don't throw away flooring, drywall, or contents before they're documented.
3. **Keep every receipt** for cleanup supplies, equipment rental, hotel stays, and professional services.
4. **File the claim within 24 to 48 hours.** Most policies require prompt notice.
5. **Get a written estimate** from a restoration company before proceeding with major work.

## Preventing the Next Flood

Once you've restored the basement, invest in prevention:

**Sump pump with battery backup:** $300 to $800 installed. The battery backup runs the pump during power outages, which is exactly when storms cause flooding.

**Backwater valve on the sewer line:** $200 to $500 installed. Prevents sewage backup from the municipal system during heavy rain.

**Exterior grading.** Ground should slope away from the foundation at a minimum of 1 inch per foot for the first 6 feet. Correcting grading costs $500 to $2,000 but is one of the most effective flood prevention measures.

**Downspout extensions.** Route downspouts at least 4 to 6 feet away from the foundation. Cost: $5 to $15 per extension.

**Interior French drain system.** For basements with chronic hydrostatic pressure issues, an interior French drain with a sump pump is the definitive solution. Cost: $5,000 to $12,000 for a full perimeter system.
