Getting a permit for deck building is a one-time process that typically costs $75 to $500, takes 1 to 3 weeks for approval, and protects you from fines, insurance headaches, and resale problems down the road. The permit process is administrative, not adversarial, and nearly every standard residential deck project can get through it without hiring a professional designer or engineer.

Here is how to handle the permit process efficiently so you can get to the actual building.

## Who Issues Deck Permits and How to Find Them

Deck permits are issued by your local building department, which operates at the city or county level. The fastest way to find yours is to search "[your city/county name] building permit" online. Most departments have websites with application forms, fee schedules, and submission instructions.

If you live in an unincorporated area, the county building department handles your permits. If your property is in an HOA, you may need HOA approval in addition to the building permit. These are separate processes — the building department does not coordinate with your HOA.

Start the permit process before you buy materials. Permit review may result in design changes, and you do not want a stack of lumber in your driveway while the building department asks you to move your post locations.

## What You Need to Apply

Most building departments require four things with your deck permit application.

### 1. A Site Plan

This is a bird's-eye view of your property showing the house footprint, property lines, and the proposed deck location. You do not need a professional survey for this (though having one helps). A hand-drawn scaled sketch on graph paper is accepted by most departments.

Include these measurements:
- Distance from the deck edge to each property line
- Distance from the deck to the house
- Overall deck dimensions
- Location of any existing structures (garage, shed, pool, septic system)

The critical numbers are the setbacks. Your zoning code specifies minimum distances between structures and property lines. Side setbacks are typically 5 to 15 feet; rear setbacks are 10 to 25 feet. The deck cannot encroach into these zones.

### 2. Deck Construction Plans

These show how the deck will be built. At minimum, include:

**Plan view (top-down):** Overall dimensions, joist direction and spacing, beam locations, post locations, stair location, and ledger board attachment point.

**Elevation view (side):** Post height, beam depth, joist depth, decking thickness, railing height, and the relationship of the deck surface to the house floor level and existing grade.

**Footing detail:** Footing diameter, depth below grade (must be below your local frost line), and post-to-footing connection method.

**Ledger detail:** How the deck attaches to the house, including fastener type, spacing, and flashing configuration.

**Railing detail:** Railing height (minimum 36 inches residential, 42 in some jurisdictions), baluster spacing (maximum 4 inches clear), post connection to the frame, and top rail configuration.

The [American Wood Council](https://www.awc.org/) publishes prescriptive deck construction guides that include span tables, connection details, and code-compliant designs. These are free, and building departments accept designs based on their tables.

### 3. Material Specifications

List the lumber species and grade for each structural component. A standard material spec for a residential deck looks like this:

- Posts: 6x6 pressure-treated Southern Yellow Pine, ground contact rated
- Beams: double 2x10 pressure-treated Southern Yellow Pine
- Joists: 2x10 pressure-treated Southern Yellow Pine at 16" on center
- Decking: 5/4x6 pressure-treated or composite (specify manufacturer)
- Ledger: 2x10 pressure-treated, attached with 1/2" lag screws per IRC table
- Hardware: [Simpson Strong-Tie](https://www.strongtie.com/) post bases, joist hangers, and angles (specify model numbers)

### 4. The Permit Fee

Fees vary by jurisdiction and project value. Expect:
- Small deck (under 200 sq ft): $75 to $200
- Medium deck (200 to 400 sq ft): $150 to $350
- Large or elevated deck (over 400 sq ft): $250 to $500

Some jurisdictions also charge a plan review fee separate from the permit fee. Ask upfront so the total is not a surprise.

## The Review and Approval Process

After you submit, a plan reviewer examines your documents against the local building code (which is typically the [IRC](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/) with local amendments). Review takes 3 to 15 business days for most residential decks.

The reviewer may return your plans with correction notes. Common correction requests include:

- **Missing footing details.** You showed where posts go but not how deep the footings are or how the post connects to the footing.
- **Inadequate ledger detail.** The connection between the deck and the house needs specific fastener sizing, spacing, and flashing shown on the plan.
- **Span issues.** Your joist span exceeds what the lumber size and grade allows per code tables. Either increase joist size or add a beam to reduce the span.
- **Railing non-compliance.** Missing guardrails where required, insufficient height, or incorrect baluster spacing.

Corrections are not rejections. Address each note, resubmit, and the review continues.

## Required Inspections During Construction

Your permit specifies inspection points. You must call for an inspection at each stage and get sign-off before proceeding. Typical inspection points:

### Footing Inspection

Called after you dig footing holes but before you pour concrete. The inspector checks that holes are the correct depth (below frost line), diameter, and in the locations shown on your approved plan. This takes 10 to 15 minutes.

**Pro tip:** Dig your holes, have them inspected, and pour concrete the same day if the weather cooperates. Open holes collect rainwater, and saturated soil at the bottom weakens the footing.

### Framing Inspection

Called after the substructure is complete — posts set, beams installed, joists hung, ledger bolted to the house — but before you install decking. The inspector verifies:

- Post-to-footing connections
- Beam-to-post connections
- Joist-to-beam and joist-to-ledger connections
- Ledger flashing and fastener pattern
- Joist spacing and blocking
- Overall structural integrity

This is the most thorough inspection. If something is wrong, it is much easier to fix before decking covers the framing.

### Final Inspection

Called after the deck is fully complete — decking, railings, stairs, and any electrical. The inspector checks finished heights, railing compliance, stair dimensions, and overall completion per the approved plans.

Once the final inspection passes, your permit is closed and the deck is officially permitted and code-compliant.

## Special Situations

### Freestanding Decks

Some jurisdictions apply different rules to freestanding decks (decks not attached to the house with a ledger board). Freestanding decks avoid the moisture-intrusion risk of a ledger connection and may have a simpler permit process. However, they still need footings, structural framing, and railing compliance. Do not assume "freestanding" means "no permit needed."

### Elevated Decks

Decks more than 8 feet above grade may require engineering review or sealed plans from a licensed engineer or architect. The permitting timeline is longer and the fee is higher. If your yard has a significant slope and you are building an elevated deck, ask the building department about engineering requirements during your initial inquiry.

### Replacing an Existing Deck

Replacing an existing deck may or may not require a new permit depending on jurisdiction. If you are replacing in-kind (same size, same footprint, same height), some departments issue a simpler repair permit or waive the permit entirely. If you are changing the size, height, or adding features, expect a full permit application.

## How Long the Entire Permit Process Takes

| Phase | Typical Duration |
|-------|-----------------|
| Preparing documents | 2–5 days |
| Submitting application | 1 day |
| Plan review | 3–15 business days |
| Corrections (if needed) | 3–7 additional days |
| **Total before building** | **1–4 weeks** |

Start the permit process 3 to 4 weeks before you want to begin construction. If your project has a specific deadline (finishing before a party or event), back-plan the permit timeline first.

## Common Mistakes in the Permit Process

**Submitting incomplete plans.** Missing details trigger correction rounds that add weeks. Include footing details, ledger connections, and material specs in your first submission.

**Ignoring the site plan.** A deck that violates setback requirements gets denied regardless of how good the construction plans are. Know your property lines.

**Not checking for easements.** Utility easements, drainage easements, and access easements can cross your property in unexpected locations. The building department may flag these, or you may discover them after you pour footings. Check your deed or plat before designing.

**Starting before the permit is issued.** Building without an approved permit, even if your application is pending, is unpermitted construction. Wait for the approval.

**Forgetting to call for inspections.** The permit is not complete until the final inspection passes. An open permit (started but never closed) creates the same resale and insurance problems as no permit at all.

## Using the Permit Process to Your Advantage

Think of the permit process as a free structural review of your deck design by someone who reviews deck plans every day. If the plan reviewer catches a span issue or a weak connection detail, that feedback prevents a potential failure.

The inspection process similarly catches construction errors while they are still easy to fix. A framing inspector who notices a joist hanger installed incorrectly is doing you a favor, not creating a problem.

Good builders welcome inspections. The permit and inspection process costs a few hundred dollars and a few weeks of time. The alternative — an unpermitted deck that fails, injures someone, or blocks your home sale — costs orders of magnitude more.

Apply for the permit, draw clear plans, and schedule your inspections on time. The process is routine, and it is the responsible way to build.
