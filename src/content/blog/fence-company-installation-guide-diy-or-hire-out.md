Hiring a fence installation company sounds simple until you start getting quotes. Prices vary wildly, timelines are vague, and it is hard to tell whether you are paying for quality craftsmanship or just overhead. On the other hand, DIY fence installation is physically demanding and more time-consuming than most homeowners expect.

Here is the honest breakdown of when hiring a fence company makes sense, when DIY is the better call, and how to avoid overpaying either way.

## What a Fence Company Actually Does

A full-service fence installation company handles everything from planning to cleanup. Here is what the process typically looks like from the homeowner's side.

**Step 1: Site visit and estimate (free, usually).** A sales rep walks your property, measures the perimeter, notes any obstacles (trees, slopes, utilities, existing structures), and asks about your requirements. They should ask about your property line --- if they do not, that is a red flag.

**Step 2: Quote.** You receive a written quote breaking out materials, labor, permits, and any additional work (old fence removal, grading, etc.). Good companies itemize. Bad companies give you a single number and hope you do not ask questions.

**Step 3: Permit and utility location.** The company should handle the permit application and call 811 for utility marking. Some include this in the price; others charge separately.

**Step 4: Installation.** A typical residential fence takes 1 to 3 days for a two-person crew. They dig post holes, set posts in concrete, let it cure (some crews work ahead on other posts while previous ones cure), attach rails and panels, install gates, and clean up.

**Step 5: Walkthrough.** After installation, walk the fence with the foreman. Check that posts are plumb, panels are level, gates swing properly, and the work matches the agreed-upon specifications.

DIY saves on labor, which represents 40 to 60 percent of the installed price. But DIY is not free labor --- it is your time, your body, and your tools.

A fence company charges $3,000 to $5,600 for the same fence installed. That is $1,500 to $3,200 in labor savings.

### DIY Time Investment

For 200 linear feet on flat terrain with normal soil:

- Layout, measuring, and string lines: 2 to 3 hours
- Digging 26 post holes (manual): 8 to 13 hours
- Setting posts in concrete: 4 to 6 hours
- Concrete cure time: 24 to 48 hours (not working time, but calendar time)
- Attaching rails: 4 to 6 hours
- Attaching pickets: 6 to 10 hours
- Hanging gates: 1 to 2 hours
- **Total labor: 25 to 40 hours over 2 to 4 weekends**

Renting a power auger ($50 to $100 per day) cuts post hole time by 60 to 70 percent. Highly recommended for more than 10 posts.

## When to Hire a Fence Company

The DIY-vs-pro decision is not just about money. Some situations strongly favor hiring out.

**Sloped terrain.** Building a fence on a slope requires stepping or racking panels, both of which demand experience and precision. A professional crew handles this routinely. A DIY first-timer on a slope often ends up with panels that look like they were installed during an earthquake.

**Property line disputes.** If there is any ambiguity about where your property ends, hire a surveyor first and a fence company second. A professional installer working from survey stakes carries less risk than a DIY job that ends up 6 inches on the neighbor's land.

**Rocky or difficult soil.** If your post holes hit rock, ledge, or heavy clay, you need equipment (rock bar, jackhammer, or mini excavator) that most homeowners do not own or have experience operating.

**Permit complexity.** Some municipalities require engineered drawings for fences over a certain height or in wind-load zones. A fence company navigates this routinely.

**Time pressure.** If you need the fence done in a week rather than over several weekends, hiring is the only realistic option.

**Access limitations.** If the fence line runs through narrow side yards, close to existing structures, or along easements, a professional crew works more efficiently in tight spaces.

## When DIY Makes Sense

**Flat, clear terrain.** Open yards with no slopes, trees in the fence line, or rock produce the most straightforward DIY installs.

**Simple layouts.** Straight runs with minimal corners. Every corner adds complexity and time.

**You enjoy the work.** Building a fence is physically satisfying work. If you find that kind of project rewarding rather than tedious, the labor savings are a bonus.

**You have a helper.** Solo fence building is possible but miserable. Posts are heavy, panels are awkward, and holding things plumb while fastening requires an extra set of hands.

**Budget is the primary constraint.** If the project does not happen unless you DIY, then DIY it is. A self-built fence that keeps the dog contained is better than a professional fence you cannot afford.

## How to Evaluate a Fence Company

Not all fence companies are equal. Here is how to separate the good from the bad.

### Green Flags

- **Detailed, itemized written quotes.** Materials, labor, permits, and any additional charges broken out separately.
- **Licensed and insured.** Ask for their contractor license number and verify it online. Ask for a certificate of insurance naming you as additionally insured for the project.
- **Willing to pull permits.** A company that suggests skipping the permit is saving themselves hassle at your expense.
- **References from recent local jobs.** Ask for three references from the past year and actually call them.
- **Clear warranty terms.** What do they cover, and for how long? Most reputable companies offer 1 to 5 years on labor and pass through the manufacturer's material warranty.
- **Published on [AARP's community housing resources](https://www.aarp.org/livable-communities/housing/) or similar directories.** Not required, but a trust signal.

### Red Flags

- **Demanding large deposits.** A 10 to 25 percent deposit is normal. Fifty percent or more upfront, especially before materials are delivered, is risky.
- **No written contract.** A handshake deal for a multi-thousand-dollar project is asking for problems.
- **Vague timelines.** "We'll get to it when we can" is not a timeline. Get a start date and estimated completion date in writing.
- **Refusing to pull permits.** This transfers all liability and code compliance risk to you.
- **Pressuring you to decide today.** The "I can only hold this price until Friday" tactic is a sales technique, not a legitimate constraint.

## Getting Better Quotes

Three quotes is the minimum. Five is better for large projects. Here is how to make the comparison useful:

1. **Specify the same materials to everyone.** "6-foot pressure-treated dog-ear privacy fence" is a specification. "A fence" is not.
2. **Include gates in the scope.** Gate costs are where quotes often diverge.
3. **Ask about concrete type.** Some companies use dry-set concrete (pour dry mix in the hole and let ground moisture cure it). Others use wet-mixed concrete. Wet-mixed is stronger and sets faster, but costs more in labor.
4. **Ask about post spacing.** Standard is 8 feet on center. Some budget installers go to 10 feet, which saves on posts but results in a weaker fence.
5. **Ask about post depth.** Posts should be set in concrete to a depth of one-third the above-ground height (24 inches for a 6-foot fence). Going deeper is better; going shallower is a problem waiting to happen.

## The Verdict

Hire a fence company if you have slopes, difficult soil, tight timelines, or want it done right without the physical toll. DIY if you have flat terrain, a helper, time on weekends, and want to save $1,500 to $5,000. In either case, the fence is only as good as its posts, so never compromise on post depth, concrete quality, or spacing. Get those right, and the rest is straightforward.
