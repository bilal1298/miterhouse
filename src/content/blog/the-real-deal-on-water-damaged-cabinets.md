Water damaged cabinets are one of the most common kitchen and bathroom problems homeowners face, and the repair-or-replace decision comes down to what the cabinets are made of, how long they were wet, and how far the damage has progressed. In many cases, you can salvage cabinets that look terrible on the surface — but in some cases, cabinets that look okay on the outside are structurally compromised and need replacing. Here is how to tell the difference.

## Assessing the Damage: What to Look For

Before deciding on a repair approach, you need to understand the extent of the damage. Open all cabinet doors and drawers in the affected area and examine:

**The cabinet floor (bottom panel).** This is typically the first surface to show damage from a under-sink leak or dishwasher failure. Press firmly with your finger. If the material gives, feels spongy, or has delaminated (layers separating), that panel needs replacement. If it is damp but still firm, drying may save it.

**The side panels and kick plate.** Water wicks upward through particleboard and MDF. Check how high the moisture has traveled by pressing the material at various heights. Staining without softness is cosmetic. Softness, swelling, or delamination is structural.

**The face frames and doors.** Solid wood frames and doors tolerate water much better than particleboard or MDF. Solid wood may warp or stain but retains structural integrity when dried properly. MDF doors and drawer fronts swell permanently — they do not return to their original dimensions when dried.

**The back panel.** Often the thinnest material in the cabinet (1/4-inch plywood or hardboard). Check for warping, delamination, or mold growth on the back surface, especially where it contacts the wall.

**The wall behind the cabinets.** If you can see or access the wall behind, check for moisture. Wet drywall behind cabinets is a hidden mold risk — the dark, enclosed space is an ideal mold environment.

## Cabinet Materials: The Damage Varies Dramatically

The construction material determines whether water damage is repairable or terminal.

**Solid wood (plywood boxes, hardwood face frames).** The most water-tolerant cabinet material. Plywood can withstand moderate water exposure without delaminating if dried promptly. Solid hardwood face frames and doors may warp but can often be sanded, refinished, or clamped flat during drying. These cabinets are almost always worth repairing unless they were submerged for days.

**Particleboard.** The most vulnerable material. Particleboard swells permanently when wet — it absorbs water, expands, and does not return to its original dimensions or structural integrity when dried. If the particleboard is swollen, it needs replacement. Brief exposure (a few hours) caught and dried quickly may be salvageable, but once the material puffs or crumbles, it is done.

**MDF (medium-density fiberboard).** Similar to particleboard in water vulnerability but denser. MDF swells at the edges first and delaminates from the inside. Damaged MDF components need replacement — they cannot be reliably repaired.

**Thermofoil or laminate over particleboard.** The vinyl or laminate skin may initially hide the damage underneath. Peel back edges if possible and check the substrate. If the particleboard core is swollen, the cabinet component is compromised regardless of how the exterior looks.

## Repair Option 1: Dry and Refinish (Minor Damage)

If the damage is limited to surface staining and the structural material is sound, drying and refinishing is the best approach.

**When this works:** Brief water exposure caught within a few hours. Cabinets made of plywood or solid wood. Surface staining without material softening. No mold present.

**The process:**

1. **Remove contents and dry.** Empty the cabinet completely. Remove drawers and doors if possible — they dry faster when removed. Wipe down all surfaces with clean towels.

2. **Set up drying.** Place a fan inside the cabinet box pointing at the wettest surfaces. Run a dehumidifier in the room. Keep air moving continuously for 48 to 72 hours.

3. **Check moisture.** Use a moisture meter to verify the material is dry. Wood should read below 12 percent. Do not seal or refinish until dry.

4. **Address staining.** Water stains on wood can be treated with oxalic acid (wood bleach), available at hardware stores for $10 to $15. Mix according to directions, apply to the stain, let it work for 30 minutes, then neutralize and rinse. This removes most water marks from bare wood.

5. **Sand and refinish.** Lightly sand the affected areas with 120-grit sandpaper, then apply matching stain (if needed) and at least two coats of polyurethane or the original finish type. For painted cabinets, prime with a stain-blocking primer and repaint.

**Cost: $20–$75 in materials per cabinet section.** Most of that is finish product.

## Repair Option 2: Replace Damaged Components (Moderate Damage)

When some parts of the cabinet are damaged but the basic box structure is sound, replacing individual components is more cost-effective than replacing entire cabinets.

**When this works:** The cabinet face frame and box are structurally sound, but the floor panel, doors, or drawer fronts are damaged.

**Replacing the cabinet floor panel:**
1. Remove the cabinet contents, shelves, and any plumbing connections
2. Cut out the damaged floor panel with a circular saw set to the panel thickness (typically 1/2 inch or 3/4 inch)
3. Cut a new panel from exterior-grade plywood (better water resistance than standard plywood)
4. Install the new panel, securing it with screws and construction adhesive to the cabinet sides
5. Seal all edges with polyurethane or waterproof sealant

Cost: $15–$40 per cabinet for materials.

**Replacing doors and drawer fronts:**
Contact the cabinet manufacturer with model and size information — many offer replacement doors years after installation. Aftermarket companies also produce standard-size replacement doors and drawer fronts in various styles. Expect $25 to $100 per door and $15 to $60 per drawer front, depending on material and style.

## Repair Option 3: Full Cabinet Replacement (Severe Damage)

If the cabinet boxes are structurally compromised — swollen particleboard, delaminated panels, soft framing, or visible mold within the structure — replacement is the right call.

**Options for replacement:**

**Stock cabinets** from home improvement stores run $75 to $300 per cabinet (uninstalled). They come in standard sizes (12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 30, 33, 36 inches wide) and ship in 1 to 2 weeks. Quality varies widely — check that the box material is plywood rather than particleboard for better long-term durability.

**Semi-custom cabinets** offer more size options and finish choices. Expect $150 to $650 per cabinet and 3 to 6 weeks lead time.

**Custom cabinets** are built to your exact specifications. They cost $500 to $1,200+ per cabinet and take 6 to 12 weeks. Only necessary if you have non-standard dimensions or specific requirements.

**Installation cost** for professional cabinet installation runs $50 to $200 per cabinet, or $1,500 to $5,000 for a full kitchen. If you are replacing only the damaged cabinets, matching the existing ones is the challenge — discontinued styles and finishes may require refinishing the entire kitchen for a uniform look.

## Dealing With Mold in Cabinets

Mold in water-damaged cabinets is common and requires prompt attention.

**Surface mold on solid wood or plywood** can be treated by scrubbing with a mold-specific cleaner or a solution of 1 cup bleach to 1 gallon of water. Scrub with a stiff brush, rinse, and dry thoroughly. Apply an antimicrobial sealant after drying.

**Mold inside particleboard or MDF** cannot be treated. The mold grows within the porous material and cannot be reached by surface cleaning. Replace the affected components.

**Mold on the wall behind cabinets** requires removing the cabinet to access the wall. Cut out any moldy drywall, treat the framing, dry completely, and install new drywall before reinstalling the cabinet.

If mold covers more than 10 square feet, or if you see mold and also notice musty odors in other rooms, professional mold assessment is recommended.

## Prevention After Repair

Once your cabinets are repaired or replaced, take these steps to prevent a repeat:

**Install a drip tray under the sink.** A simple plastic tray ($10–$25) catches slow drips before they reach the cabinet floor. Check it monthly.

**Use braided stainless steel supply lines.** Replace any rubber or plastic supply connections under the sink with braided stainless steel ($8–$15 each). They are more durable and less likely to fail.

**Fix leaks immediately.** A drip that seems minor — a slow faucet leak, a damp spot at a drain connection — is the beginning of cabinet damage. Address it the day you notice it.

**Seal cabinet interiors.** Apply a coat of polyurethane or waterproof sealant to the floor and lower 6 inches of the inside of under-sink cabinets. This creates a moisture barrier that buys you time to notice and fix a leak before it damages the structure.

**Consider a leak detector.** A $15 to $30 water sensor placed on the cabinet floor under the sink alerts you to moisture before it becomes visible damage. Smart models send a notification to your phone.

The bottom line on water-damaged cabinets: solid wood and plywood cabinets caught early are almost always repairable. Particleboard and MDF cabinets with anything beyond surface moisture are usually replacement candidates. Make the right call based on material and damage extent, and you will save both money and frustration.
