Patio enclosure builders charge $8,000 to $50,000+ to convert an open patio into a screened or glass-enclosed room, with most projects landing in the $15,000 to $30,000 range. The massive price spread comes down to one decision: are you adding a basic screen room on your existing slab, or building a fully insulated, climate-controlled sunroom that functions as year-round living space?

That distinction matters more than anything else you will read about patio enclosures, because it determines whether you need a handyman with screen framing experience or a licensed general contractor with structural, electrical, and HVAC capabilities.

## Types of Patio Enclosures

### Screen Room (Budget Option)

An aluminum-framed structure with screen panels, built on an existing patio slab. No climate control, no insulation, no glass.

- **Cost:** $5,000 to $15,000 installed
- **Timeline:** 2 to 5 days
- **Permit:** Usually required but straightforward
- **Best for:** Bug-free outdoor living, three-season use in mild climates

Screen rooms are the simplest enclosure option. Pre-engineered aluminum framing systems (from companies like Screen Tight, Screeneze, or EZE-Breeze) bolt together on-site with minimal structural modification. The roof is typically aluminum panels or a polycarbonate sheet that ties into the existing roofline or extends from the house wall.

### Three-Season Room

A step up from a screen room — aluminum or vinyl-framed walls with glass or vinyl window panels that open and close. Not insulated or climate-controlled, but protects against wind and rain.

- **Cost:** $10,000 to $30,000 installed
- **Timeline:** 1 to 3 weeks
- **Permit:** Required; may need engineer-stamped plans
- **Best for:** Extended-season use, areas with moderate winters

Three-season rooms use window panels that slide or tilt to open, giving you the feel of a screen room in summer and a protected space in spring and fall. They are not comfortable in winter without supplemental heat, and running a space heater in an uninsulated enclosure is expensive and largely ineffective.

### Four-Season Sunroom (Premium)

A fully insulated, climate-controlled room addition with glass walls, a proper roof, HVAC connection, and electrical service. Functionally, this is a room addition with more glass than typical.

- **Cost:** $25,000 to $80,000+ installed
- **Timeline:** 4 to 12 weeks
- **Permit:** Full building permit, possibly architectural review
- **Best for:** Year-round living space, adding square footage and home value

A four-season sunroom adds to your home's conditioned square footage. It requires a foundation that meets local code (your existing patio slab may or may not qualify), insulated framing, code-compliant electrical, and connection to your home's heating and cooling system.

## What to Look for in a Builder

### Screen Room Builders

For a basic screen room, you need someone experienced with aluminum framing systems — not necessarily a licensed general contractor. Aluminum framing companies, screen installers, and some fence companies handle these projects.

**Verify:**
- Experience with the specific framing system they propose
- Photos of completed screen rooms (ideally ones you can visit)
- Proper insurance (general liability at minimum)
- Understanding of local wind load requirements (critical in hurricane and high-wind zones)

### Three-Season and Four-Season Room Builders

These are full construction projects. Hire a licensed general contractor or a sunroom-specific company with general contractor credentials.

**Verify:**
- State contractor license (required in most states)
- General liability and workers' compensation insurance
- References from similar sunroom projects (not just general contracting)
- Understanding of energy codes, insulation requirements, and HVAC sizing
- Whether they handle permits or expect you to

### Specialized Sunroom Companies vs. General Contractors

Sunroom-specific companies (Champion, Patio Enclosures, Betterliving) sell proprietary systems with factory-built components assembled on-site. The advantages are speed and consistency — these are engineered systems that go together predictably.

The disadvantages: limited design flexibility, proprietary parts that only the original company can service, and sometimes aggressive sales tactics. The in-home consultation often involves a 2-hour sales pitch with manufactured "discounts" that disappear if you do not sign immediately. Take their quote, tell them you need to compare, and do not be pressured by artificial urgency.

General contractors offer more design freedom and use standard building materials that any contractor can maintain or modify later. The build takes longer and depends more heavily on the contractor's skill, but the result is a truly custom room.

## Cost Factors That Move the Number

**Existing slab condition.** If your patio slab is level, structurally sound, and properly sized, you save $3,000 to $8,000 in foundation work. If it is cracked, settling, or too small, you may need a new slab or slab extension.

**Roof integration.** Tying the enclosure roof into the existing roofline is cleaner and more weather-resistant than a standalone roof, but it costs more ($2,000 to $5,000 more) because it involves modifying the existing roof structure.

**Electrical and lighting.** A basic screen room needs minimal electrical — maybe an outlet and a ceiling fan. A four-season room needs a full electrical circuit, lighting, outlets, and possibly a sub-panel. Add $1,500 to $4,000.

**HVAC.** Extending your existing system to heat and cool a sunroom ranges from $1,500 (adding a duct run) to $5,000+ (a mini-split heat pump for the new space). A mini-split is often the better choice because it is independently controlled and does not strain the existing system.

**Glass quality.** For four-season rooms, the glass (or polycarbonate) panels are a major cost component. Standard double-pane insulated glass costs $15 to $30 per square foot. Low-E, argon-filled glass costs $20 to $45 per square foot but significantly improves energy efficiency.

**Site access and complexity.** A flat backyard with easy truck access costs less than a sloped site, a second-story deck enclosure, or a project where materials must be hand-carried through a narrow side yard.

## The Permit Process

Most jurisdictions require permits for any attached structure, including screen rooms. Four-season rooms almost always trigger:

- Building permit (structural, framing, roofing)
- Electrical permit
- Mechanical permit (HVAC)
- Possibly plumbing if you are adding a wet bar or similar

The permit process adds 2 to 8 weeks before construction begins and costs $300 to $2,000 in fees. Your builder should handle this — if they seem reluctant to pull permits, that is a red flag.

Some HOAs require architectural review committee approval in addition to municipal permits. This adds another 2 to 6 weeks and may impose restrictions on size, style, materials, and roof pitch.

## DIY Feasibility

**Screen room kit:** Feasible for a handy homeowner. Kits from companies like Screen Tight cost $2,000 to $6,000 for materials, and installation is essentially aluminum framing assembly. You save $3,000 to $8,000 in labor. The work involves measuring, cutting aluminum, assembling the frame, attaching screen, and basic roofing.

**Three-season room kit:** Borderline DIY. The framing is similar to a screen room, but the window panels are heavier, the roof must be more substantial, and the tie-in to the existing house requires weatherproofing skill. A competent DIYer with construction experience can handle this, but a first-timer should not attempt it.

**Four-season room:** Not a DIY project. This is a room addition requiring structural engineering, code-compliant framing, insulation, electrical, and HVAC — each of which requires either trade expertise or licensed professionals.

## Making It Work with Your Existing Space

Before committing to a builder, think through how the enclosure interacts with your existing layout:

**Access.** Where will the door from the house to the enclosure go? Ideally through an existing sliding glass door or French door. Cutting a new opening through an exterior wall adds $1,000 to $3,000 and possibly a structural header.

**Sight lines.** Will the enclosure block views from interior rooms? A glass or screen enclosure is transparent, but the framing members are not. Walk through the interior rooms with the proposed enclosure dimensions taped on the patio to visualize the impact.

**Drainage.** Enclosing a patio changes drainage patterns. Roof runoff needs gutters and downspouts directing water away from the foundation. If the existing patio slopes toward the house (it should not, but it happens), correct this before enclosing.

**Furniture and function.** Plan the room's purpose before finalizing the size. A seating area for four people needs roughly 10x12 feet. A dining area for six needs 12x14 feet. A multi-use space with seating and dining needs 14x18 feet or larger.

If you are investing in outdoor space, consider how a patio enclosure fits with your broader plans — whether that includes [composite decking upgrades](/blog/composite-deck-building-a-diyers-honest-take/) or [landscaping improvements](/blog/sprinkler-valve-repair-cost-what-to-expect-in-2026/).

The best patio enclosure builders will walk you through all of these considerations during the design phase. If a builder jumps straight to pricing without discussing how you will use the space, how it connects to your home, and what permits are required, find a different builder.
