The blow off valve on your water heater — properly called the temperature and pressure relief valve, or T&P valve — is the most important safety device on the unit. When it leaks, it is either doing its job (releasing excess pressure or temperature) or it has failed and needs replacement. The distinction matters because one scenario means your water heater has a dangerous condition that needs correcting, and the other means you need a $15 part and 20 minutes.

Here is how to diagnose why your T&P valve is leaking and what to do about it.

## What the T&P Valve Actually Does

The T&P valve is mounted on the side or top of the tank with a discharge pipe running down to within 6 inches of the floor (or piped to an exterior drain). It opens automatically when water temperature exceeds 210 degrees Fahrenheit or tank pressure exceeds 150 PSI. Without this valve, a malfunctioning water heater could build enough pressure to rupture the tank — and a tank failure under pressure is a genuinely catastrophic event.

This is not a part you cap off, plug, or remove. Per the [International Residential Code](https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/2024-i-codes/irc/), every water heater must have a functioning T&P valve rated for the unit's BTU input and storage capacity.

## Why Your T&P Valve Is Leaking

There are four main causes, and each has a different fix.

### 1. Excessive Water Pressure (The Most Common Cause)

Your home's water supply pressure directly affects your water heater. When municipal water pressure is too high — above 80 PSI — the T&P valve can weep or drip to relieve the excess pressure. This is the valve working correctly, protecting the tank from damage.

**How to check:** Buy a water pressure gauge ($10–$15 at any hardware store) that threads onto a hose bib. Check pressure at an outdoor faucet first thing in the morning (before any water use in the house). Normal residential pressure is 40–80 PSI.

**If pressure is above 80 PSI:** You need a pressure reducing valve (PRV) installed on your main water line where it enters the house. If you already have one, it may need adjustment or replacement. A PRV costs $40–$80 for the part. Professional installation runs $200–$400. This is a moderate DIY project if you are comfortable with soldering or using push-fit fittings on your main line.

**If pressure is normal but fluctuates:** Thermal expansion may be the issue — covered next.

### 2. Thermal Expansion (No Expansion Tank Installed)

When your water heater heats cold water from 50 degrees to 120 degrees, the water expands. In an open plumbing system (no check valve or backflow preventer), this expanded water pushes back into the city main. No problem.

But many homes have a check valve or pressure reducing valve that creates a "closed system" — expanded water has nowhere to go, so pressure builds in the tank and the T&P valve opens to relieve it.

**Signs it is thermal expansion:** The T&P valve drips during or shortly after a heating cycle, then stops. You may also notice fluctuating water pressure when the heater is firing.

**The fix:** Install a thermal expansion tank. This is a small tank (typically 2–5 gallons) that mounts on the cold water supply line above the water heater. It contains a rubber bladder backed by compressed air — when water expands, it pushes into the bladder instead of building pressure.

Expansion tanks cost $30–$60 for residential sizes. Installation is straightforward — it threads onto a tee fitting on the cold water line. Professional installation: $150–$250. The tank's air charge must match your water pressure (use a tire gauge on the Schrader valve at the top; adjust with a bicycle pump).

Expansion tanks have a limited life — the bladder fails after 5–8 years. A waterlogged expansion tank (heavy when you tap it, instead of partially hollow-sounding) needs replacement.

### 3. Faulty T&P Valve

T&P valves do wear out. Mineral deposits, corrosion, and sediment can prevent the valve from seating properly, causing a constant slow drip even when pressure and temperature are normal.

**How to test:** Lift the valve lever quickly and let it snap back. Water should shoot out the discharge pipe while the lever is up and stop completely when you release it. If it continues to dribble after releasing the lever, the valve is not seating properly.

**The fix:** Replace the T&P valve. This is a DIY-friendly repair:

1. Turn off the gas (or power for electric heaters) and the cold water supply
2. Open a hot water faucet upstairs to break the vacuum
3. Attach a garden hose to the drain valve and drain 2–3 gallons (just enough to drop the water level below the valve)
4. Unscrew the old valve with a pipe wrench — it is standard 3/4" NPT threads
5. Wrap the new valve's threads with Teflon tape (6–8 wraps, clockwise)
6. Thread in the new valve and tighten firmly — orient it so the discharge pipe can be reconnected
7. Reconnect the discharge pipe, close the drain, turn on the water supply, and check for leaks
8. Relight the pilot or restore power

A replacement T&P valve costs $10–$20. Match the pressure and BTU ratings on your old valve. Professional replacement: $100–$200.

### 4. Temperature Set Too High

If the water heater thermostat is set above 140 degrees, the T&P valve may release water as temperatures approach the 210-degree safety threshold — especially during long heating cycles or if the thermostat overshoots.

**The fix:** Check your temperature setting. The [Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/water-heating) recommends 120 degrees for safety and efficiency. Lower the setting and monitor. If the valve continues to leak at normal temperature settings, the thermostat itself may be malfunctioning — a condition where the heater does not shut off at the set temperature and continues heating. This requires a new thermostat or gas valve, which is a professional repair.

## The Discharge Pipe Matters

The pipe attached to the T&P valve is not optional — it directs released water safely to the floor or an exterior drain instead of spraying scalding water toward anyone standing nearby. Code requirements for the discharge pipe per [NFPA standards](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-70-standard-development/70):

- Must be the same diameter as the valve outlet (typically 3/4")
- Must terminate within 6 inches of the floor or drain
- Must not have any valves, caps, or restrictions installed
- Must slope downward continuously from the valve
- Must be made of material rated for the temperature (copper, CPVC, or stainless steel — not regular PVC, which warps at high temperatures)

If your discharge pipe is missing, terminated in the wrong location, or made of the wrong material, correct it when you replace the valve.

## When to Call a Professional

Handle the T&P valve replacement yourself if you are comfortable with basic plumbing — it is genuinely straightforward. Call a professional when:

- The valve keeps leaking after replacement (indicating a systemic pressure or temperature problem)
- You need a PRV installed on the main line
- The water heater is producing water above 140 degrees despite normal thermostat settings
- The T&P valve is corroded in place and will not unscrew without risk of breaking the fitting in the tank
- You suspect the water heater has other issues — our guide on [whether water heater leaking is normal](/blog/is-it-normal-for-water-heater-to-leak-honest-guide/) covers the full range of leak sources

## Never Do This

A few things homeowners do that create dangerous situations:

- **Never cap or plug a leaking T&P valve.** The valve is leaking for a reason. Blocking it removes the only pressure safety device on the tank.
- **Never install a T&P valve with a lower pressure rating** than what came on the heater. It must match or exceed the tank's rated working pressure.
- **Never redirect the discharge pipe upward** or into a closed container. Released water must have a free path to drain.
- **Never ignore chronic T&P valve leaking.** It indicates a pressure or temperature condition that is stressing the tank. Resolve the root cause.

Per [EPA WaterSense guidelines](https://www.epa.gov/watersense), a chronically leaking T&P valve can waste significant amounts of water — and it is almost always a symptom of a fixable underlying issue.

## Maintenance Schedule

Add these to your annual home maintenance checklist:

- **Every 6 months:** Lift the T&P valve lever briefly to verify it opens and closes properly. Place a bucket under the discharge pipe first.
- **Annually:** Check the thermal expansion tank (if installed) by tapping it — it should sound partially hollow. Check the air charge with a tire gauge.
- **Every 3–5 years:** Replace the T&P valve proactively. They are cheap and the replacement takes 20 minutes.
- **When you replace the water heater:** Always install a new T&P valve — never reuse the old one on a new tank.
