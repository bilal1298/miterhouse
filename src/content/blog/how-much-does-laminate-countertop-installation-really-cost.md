Laminate countertop installation costs $300–$1,500 for a standard kitchen when you combine materials and labor, making it the most affordable countertop installation by a wide margin. The material itself runs $10–$30 per linear foot for post-form (ready-made) pieces, and professional installation adds $15–$35 per linear foot. Here's every cost detail you need to plan the project accurately.

## Post-Form vs. Custom Laminate: Two Different Price Points

Understanding the distinction between post-form and custom laminate countertops is essential because the cost difference is substantial.

### Post-Form (Factory-Made)

Post-form laminate countertops arrive as finished pieces with the laminate already wrapped around a rounded front edge and a short backsplash lip. You buy them off the shelf in standard lengths from 4 to 12 feet.

**Material costs:**
- 4-foot section: $50–$120
- 6-foot section: $75–$180
- 8-foot section: $100–$240
- 10-foot section: $125–$300
- 12-foot section: $150–$360

These prices cover the most common patterns. Premium patterns (stone-look, wood-look) from Formica 180fx or Wilsonart HD run 20–40% more.

**Pros:** Cheapest option, widely available, same-day purchase, DIY-installable.
**Cons:** Limited depth (standard 25.5-inch only), limited edge profiles (rounded front with integrated backsplash only), require miter joints at corners.

### Custom Laminate (Shop-Built)

Custom laminate countertops are built to your exact specifications in a local fabrication shop. The laminate sheet is applied to a custom-cut substrate (typically MDF or plywood), allowing any shape, depth, and edge profile.

**Material + fabrication costs:**
- $25–$60 per square foot (compared to $10–$25/sq ft for post-form)

**Pros:** Any size or shape, multiple edge profile options, undermount sink compatible (with solid surface buildup), no seams in standard-length runs.
**Cons:** 5–10 day lead time, requires professional measurement, 2–3x the cost of post-form.

For most straightforward kitchen layouts with standard-depth cabinets, post-form delivers excellent results at the lowest cost. Reserve custom laminate for unusual layouts, extra-deep counters, or when you want specific design details like a waterfall edge or integrated drainage grooves.

## Installation Cost Breakdown

### DIY Installation (Post-Form Only)

Total out-of-pocket cost for a typical L-shaped kitchen:

| Item | Cost |
|------|------|
| Post-form laminate (two sections) | $200–$450 |
| Miter corner bolt kit | $15–$25 |
| End splash kits (2) | $16–$30 |
| Silicone caulk and sealant | $10–$15 |
| Sink cutout jigsaw blade | $8–$12 |
| **Total DIY** | **$250–$530** |

Add a plumber's visit for reconnection if you're not comfortable with supply lines and drains: $100–$200.

### Professional Installation

| Item | Cost |
|------|------|
| Post-form laminate (supplied by installer) | $300–$600 |
| Removal of old countertop | $100–$200 |
| Installation labor | $200–$400 |
| Plumbing disconnect/reconnect | $100–$200 |
| **Total Professional** | **$700–$1,400** |

The professional premium over DIY is $400–$900 for a standard kitchen. Worth it if you're uncomfortable with the cutting and fitting, but the skills required are genuinely basic — measuring, straight cutting with a circular saw, and jigsaw work for the sink opening.

## Step-by-Step Installation Process

Whether you DIY or hire a pro, here's what happens:

### 1. Disconnect and Remove ($0–$200)

Shut off water supply valves. Disconnect faucet supply lines and drain trap. Remove the old countertop — typically 6–10 screws from below plus caulk along the wall. Pry away from the wall carefully if you want to preserve the existing backsplash.

### 2. Check Cabinet Condition ($0–$300 for repairs)

With the old countertop off, inspect:
- **Corner blocks and brackets** — are the mounting surfaces intact?
- **Sink base cabinet** — any water damage or soft spots?
- **Level and flat** — is the top surface of the cabinets in one plane?

Water damage around the sink is present in about one in three kitchens over 15 years old. Minor repairs (new plywood panel, fresh blocks) cost $50–$150 in materials. Major repairs (cabinet replacement) run $200–$500 per cabinet and may delay the project.

### 3. Test Fit and Mark Cuts ($0)

Set the new countertop sections in place without securing. Check the fit against walls — most walls aren't perfectly straight, so you'll need to scribe (trace the wall contour onto the countertop backsplash lip) and sand or file to get a tight fit.

Mark the sink cutout location while the countertop is in place. Center the sink on the sink base cabinet, trace the template, and mark clearly.

### 4. Cut to Length ($0 if DIY)

Measure the required length. Mark a cut line using a speed square for a perfectly straight guide. Cut from the bottom with a circular saw (laminate faces down) using a fine-tooth blade. A piece of painter's tape along the cut line reduces chipping on the laminate.

For the corner miter: post-form miter kits come pre-cut at 45 degrees. Dry-fit, check alignment, then apply silicone sealant in the joint and tighten the draw bolts. Wipe excess sealant immediately.

### 5. Cut Sink Opening ($0–$100)

Position the sink template (included with most sinks or trace the sink with a 1/2-inch inset). Drill a starter hole in each corner with a spade bit. Cut with a jigsaw from above using a laminate-cutting or fine-tooth blade. Cut slowly — rushing chips the laminate.

**Critical step:** Before completing the last side of the cut, screw a support strip across the opening to hold the cutout piece. When it drops, unsupported, the falling weight can crack the laminate.

### 6. Secure to Cabinets ($0)

Apply a thin bead of silicone along the top edges of the cabinets (optional but helps prevent water migration). Set the countertop in position. Screw through corner brackets from below into the countertop — use 1-inch screws to avoid penetrating the surface.

### 7. Seal Edges and Reconnect ($25–$275)

Run a bead of silicone caulk along the wall-to-countertop seam. Install end splash caps with glue. Reconnect all plumbing — supply lines, drain, garbage disposal, dishwasher drain hose.

## Common Questions Installers Hear

**Can I install laminate over existing laminate?**
Technically yes, but it's not recommended. The added height changes the relationship between the countertop and the range, dishwasher, and sink. It's also hard to get good adhesion on the old laminate surface. The time you spend prepping the existing surface is better spent removing and replacing.

**Will a laminate countertop support an undermount sink?**
Standard post-form laminate uses particleboard substrate, which swells when the exposed edge (at the sink cutout) contacts water. Traditional undermount sinks don't work with post-form laminate. Custom laminate can incorporate a solid surface (Corian) build-up at the sink edge to support an undermount — adding $200–$400 to the project.

**How long does a laminate countertop last?**
With reasonable care: 10–20 years. The laminate surface itself can last 25+ years if not subjected to heat damage, deep scratches, or prolonged moisture exposure. The substrate (particleboard) is the failure point — once it swells from moisture, the countertop is done.

**Can I repair a chip or scratch in laminate?**
Small chips: laminate repair paste ($8–$12 at hardware stores) fills and color-matches most minor damage. Deep scratches that expose the substrate: if they're near the edge, consider an end cap or edge strip repair. If the damage is mid-surface, paste filler is your best option. Replacement is generally more cost-effective than extensive repairs.

## Where Laminate Saves You the Most

The biggest cost savings come from what laminate doesn't require:

- **No template visit** (for post-form). You measure yourself.
- **No fabrication lead time.** Buy today, install tomorrow.
- **No specialized tools.** Standard woodworking tools handle every cut.
- **No professional installation requirement.** Stone slabs need 2–3 people and specialized equipment. Laminate needs one person and a Saturday.
- **No sealing or special maintenance.** Clean with any household cleaner. No annual sealing, no special products.

For a household that wants functional, attractive countertops without a $3,000–$6,000 investment, laminate is the rational choice — especially as a bridge until a more comprehensive kitchen renovation is in the budget.
